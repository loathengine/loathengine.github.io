Chapter 23. Charging

Key points

  • A charge is only as accurate as the scale that weighed it, and a scale is trustworthy only at the weights you check.
  • A finer scale pays off only when charge errors make up a meaningful share of your velocity spread.
  • You cannot see a charging mistake after seating the bullet, so inspect every charged case while it is still open.
  • Case fill matters at both ends. A low-fill load can hide a double charge. A compressed load can push its bullet out over time.
  • Mixed powders cannot be seen, so a session starts and ends with an empty measure.

Why it matters

Charging is the step where a small slip becomes a dangerous one. A wrong powder, a double charge and a missing charge all look the same from outside a loaded round. Each one can wreck a rifle or hurt the shooter.

The charge also sets velocity. Random errors in the charge add to the spread of your velocities. How much they add depends on your load, your scale and your method. Here is how to work that out.

How it is done

Set up the workbench

  1. Put one powder on the workbench. Read the label. Confirm that it matches your load data exactly.1 For a work-up, plan it first: the step charges, the two limits and the rounds per step (see Chapter 16, “Plan the work-up”).
  2. Make sure the measure is empty before you fill it. Fast powder left in a measure from the last session is a hidden danger. It can be thrown first and then covered by the right powder. Nothing looks wrong from above.1 Look into the hopper to check that it is empty (see Figure 23-1). Then pour in only the powder you need for this session.

Verify the scale

Safety · See Chapter 2

A scale that reads low makes every charge heavy. Confirm that the scale balances at zero before you charge.1

  1. Set the scale on a solid, level surface. Keep it away from drafts, fans and vibration (see Chapter 8). Let an electronic scale warm up and calibrate it as its maker directs.
  2. Zero it with the empty pan. A beam scale must balance at zero with the empty pan.1 An electronic scale should read zero with the pan in place.
  3. Weigh check weights near your charge weight. A scale can read true at one weight and wrong at another, so check it where you will use it (see Chapter 8). Write down each reading.
  4. Hold the scale to a tolerance set in advance. Use one display step. If a reading is off by more, calibrate and check again. If it is still off, stop. Do not load with that scale.
  5. Check it again later. Repeat the zero and one check weight partway through a long session and again at the end.

Check weights are often marked in grams. Convert with this formula.

grains = grams × 15.432

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. A 5 gram check weight weighs 5 × 15.432 = 77.16 gr. A scale that shows tenths of a grain should read 77.2 gr. A reading of 77.1 or 77.3 is within one display step. A reading of 77.5 is not, so calibrate and check again.

Throw, trickle or dispense

  1. Make each charge one of three ways.
    • Throw. Throw the charge from a volumetric measure (see Chapter 8). Set the measure with your scale. Throw several charges to settle the powder and return them to the hopper. Weigh every charge until several in a row show the weight you want. Work the handle the same way every time.
    • Trickle. Throw or spoon a charge slightly light into the scale pan. Then add powder with a trickler until the scale reads your target (see Chapter 8). A small spoon helps with overfills.1
    • Dispense. Set an electronic dispenser to your target weight and let it finish the charge (see Chapter 8). Treat its scale like any other and check it with check weights before you start. If a charge overshoots, pour it back into the hopper and dispense it again.
  2. Weigh every charge for a work-up or a precision load. Thrown charges vary with the powder, the measure and your rhythm. Weigh each one on the checked scale. For practice loads from a measure that throws within your tolerance, check-weigh about every 10th charge.2
Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Charge errors affect velocity, but large-sample tests have not settled how fine a scale you need. Live-fire tests have compared thrown charges with scales resolving from about 0.1 gr down to about 0.02 gr (roughly one kernel of stick powder).3 Finer weighing alone did not always lower velocity SD, and ignition consistency can matter more.4 Detecting a 1.25 to 1 difference in velocity SD takes about 160 shots per load (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).

What a charge error costs

You can estimate the cost for your own load from two numbers. The first is how much velocity the load gains per grain. Work it out from the published start and maximum loads for your exact components.

fps per grain = (max fps − start fps) ÷ (max charge − start charge)

The second is how much your charges vary. For thrown charges, throw 20, weigh each one and find their SD (see The Precision Rifle Handbook). For charges weighed on a scale that shows tenths, the display hides up to about 0.05 gr either way. Spread evenly, that is an SD of about 0.03 gr.

Independent sources of spread add as squares. The same rule applies to chronograph error.5

velocity SD from charges = fps per grain × charge SD
total SD = √(other SD² + velocity SD from charges²)

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. Say your load gains 50 fps per grain, and everything except the charge gives a base velocity SD of 10 fps. Weighed charges (0.1 gr scale, 0.03 gr SD, adding 1.5 fps) raise total SD to √(10² + 1.5²) = 10.1 fps. Thrown charges (0.1 gr SD, adding 5.0 fps) raise it to √(10² + 5²) = 11.2 fps. Repeat the math at a 5 fps base SD (such as a tuned F-Open load): a 0.02 gr scale (0.01 gr SD, +0.5 fps) gives 5.0 fps total, a 0.1 gr scale (0.03 gr SD, +1.5 fps) gives 5.2 fps, and thrown charges (0.1 gr SD, +5.0 fps) give 7.1 fps. Even at a 5 fps base SD, thrown charges hurt noticeably, while moving from a 0.1 gr to a 0.02 gr scale removes only about 0.2 fps. Work out your own numbers before buying a finer scale.

Charge the cases

Safety · See Chapter 2

A missing or short charge can lodge a bullet in the bore. The next shot can then cause a catastrophic failure. A double charge can hide in a low-fill load, so take extreme care with any load that fills less than half the case.1

  1. Pour each charge through a funnel that fits the case mouth. Tap the funnel so no kernels hang up in it. A full-case or compressed charge can bridge in the funnel or neck. Pour slowly and tap the case, or use a drop tube (see Chapter 8). If a published charge still will not fit, something differs from the test (see Chapter 31).
  2. Work in a fixed order. Set the seating die on a dummy round before you charge the first case (see Chapter 24). Then choose one of two workflows and keep to it.
    • Batch. Charge every case in order, moving each one from the empty side to the charged side of the block as you go. Inspect them all under the light. Then seat the bullets.1
    • One at a time. Weigh and pour one charge, then look inside and compare the powder level with a reference case (an unprimed, marked case holding your target charge). Seat the bullet right away before charging the next case. No charged case sits waiting on the workbench.

Inspect every case under light

  1. Shine a bright light into every charged case. Look at the powder level and compare it with the cases around it.6 If a case looks low, high or empty, dump it and recharge it.

Know your case fill

Case fill is the poured charge as a share of usable capacity (see Chapter 13). To check by hand whether the bullet will compress the charge, charge a primed case the way you will charge the batch (tapping only if you will tap the batch) and measure from the case mouth down to the powder with a caliper depth rod. If the powder stands higher than the bullet’s seated depth (see Chapter 13), the bullet will compress the charge. Follow published compressed-charge flags, and check CBTO on stored compressed rounds before shooting (see Chapters 13 and 25).

Close the session

  1. Return unused powder to its original container. That includes the reference case’s charge. Powder left in a measure absorbs moisture, changing burn rate (see Chapter 13), and risks mix-ups.1 Dispose of any doubtful powder (see Chapter 2).
  2. Empty the measure completely. Check that the hopper is visibly empty (see step 2).
  3. Clean up spills with a brush and dustpan. Never use a vacuum.7
