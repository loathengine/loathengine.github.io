Chapter 31. Troubleshooting

On this page (6 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • Problems in the workshop
  • Problems at the range
  • Common questions
  • Notes

Key points

  • Unusual force is a symptom. More force is never the fix. A pressure sign is never noise, and its absence is not proof of safety.
  • Each fault starts at one step. Measuring shows which, and only one change at a time shows what fixed it.
  • Some defects end a case’s life: a head separation ring, a split or a loose primer pocket.
  • Small samples mislead, so many range faults are only noise. Judge stringing and far shots against chance.
  • Vertical stringing is a velocity problem only when the slow shots are the low ones. At 100 yards it almost never is.

Why it matters

Workshop faults cost brass and time. Some cost safety. A stuck case ends a loading session. A case near head separation can fail in the chamber. A round that will not chamber wastes a trip to the range. A fault at the range costs more. It costs ammunition, barrel life and trust in your own data.

Most workshop faults have simple causes. The skill is finding the step that caused them before you change anything. A fix that changes two things at once teaches you nothing. If the fault comes back, you will not know which change mattered. At the range, some faults are real and others are noise from too few shots. The skill is telling the two apart. One fault is never noise: a pressure sign.

How it is done

Problems in the workshop

  1. Stop at the first sign of trouble. Unusual force, a new sound or a strange mark is enough. More force is almost never the right answer.1
  2. Set the case or round aside and mark it. Keep it out of the batch.
  3. Find the step where the fault appears. Measure the case before and after that step. Discard any case with a safety defect. Do not try to save it.
  4. Find the symptom in the table below. Check the likely causes in the order given.
  5. Change one thing. Re-measure five to ten cases.
  6. Record what you changed and what you measured.

Problems at the range

  1. Stop at once at a pressure sign or at a shot that sounds or feels wrong. After a wrong shot, unload and check the bore for a stuck bullet before you fire again (see Chapter 2). If a round fails to fire, keep the muzzle downrange for at least 30 seconds before you open the action.14 Everything else can wait. Pressure signs are unreliable early warnings, and their absence does not prove a load safe (see Chapter 28).
  2. Look at the target. A flyer may be a hole marked in the wrong place or a shot you pulled.
  3. Count your shots. Read every number with its interval.
  4. Find the symptom in the table below. Change one thing. Then shoot enough rounds to see whether the change worked (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapters 15 and 16).
  5. Record the conditions, the change and the result.

Stringing

Compare the spread up and down with the spread side to side. Vertical much larger than horizontal is vertical stringing. Suspect velocity spread or uneven primer strikes from a weak firing-pin spring. A barrel touching the stock does it too (see Chapter 3). Horizontal much larger than vertical points at wind, the bipod or trigger push.

Judge stringing against chance, not against a fixed ratio. A rule that calls a group strung whenever one axis is 1.5 times the other would call almost half of all round 5-shot groups strung.19 Use the lines in Table 31-1 instead. A round group is then called strung about 1 time in 20.

  1. Find SD horizontal and SD vertical from the group’s center, as in the mean radius worked example (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 15).
  2. Divide the larger SD by the smaller.
  3. Call the group strung only when the ratio reaches the line for your shots.

For a composite, measure each shot from its own group’s center. Then divide each sum of squares by the shots minus the number of groups, not by the shots minus 1.

Table 31-1. When to call a group strung, and when to call velocity linked to height.

ShotsStrung when the larger SD ÷ the smaller SD reachesVelocity linked to height when the correlation reaches

One 10-shot group2.01.63
One 20-shot group1.59.44
Four 5-shot groups1.66.48

The ratio lines come from the F-test on two SDs, applied to the two axes. Both columns are two-sided lines at 0.05. Test a load’s composite once, not each group in turn. Each extra test is another chance for luck (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 16).

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

The 10-shot group in The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 15, has an SD of .33 inch across and .25 inch up and down. The ratio is .33 ÷ .25 = 1.3, well short of 2.01. That group is not strung.

Next, ask whether velocity is causing the vertical.

  1. Take each shot’s velocity from the string’s average (dv) and its height from the group’s center (dh). In a composite, take both from each group’s own averages.
  2. Work out the correlation between the two. CORREL of the two columns in a spreadsheet gives the same number.
correlation = Σ(dv × dh) ÷ √(Σdv² × Σdh²)
  1. If the correlation is positive and reaches the line in Table 31-1, the slow shots are the low ones. Velocity is moving your vertical, and a lower SD will tighten it. Below the line, a lower SD is not shown to help this group.

Distance matters a great deal here. Take a 140 gr 6.5 Creedmoor load at 2,700 fps. Example numbers. Not load data. There, 10 fps of velocity moves the bullet about .18 inch at 300 yards. At 1,000 yards it moves it about 3 inches.20 At 100 yards velocity explains almost none of the vertical.19 Look at the rifle, the rest and the barrel heat first.

Check the drop from gravity, not only the correlation. A positive correlation alone does not mean velocity caused a shot to land low. In a short string, a slow shot can happen to land low purely by chance, producing a high correlation coefficient (r). Always check the physical drop against what gravity actually does.

The change in bullet drop from a velocity change Δv is:

drop change = drop(v + Δv) − drop(v)

or approximately from time of flight t and total gravity drop from the bore axis y:

drop change ≈ −2 × y × (Δv ÷ v)

At 100 yards with the example 6.5 Creedmoor load, a shot 20 fps slow has its gravity drop increased by only about .04 inch. If that shot printed .75 inch below the group center, velocity explains only .04 inch of that displacement (about 5 percent). The other .71 inch came from rifle dispersion, aim, cant or rest movement. When the observed vertical displacement is several times larger than the ballistic drop, velocity did not cause the vertical error, even if the correlation happens to be positive.

This is also why real groups almost always remain circular. If velocity variation were a primary driver of vertical dispersion, targets would routinely stretch into vertical ellipses. When your vertical and horizontal spreads are roughly equal (the ratio in Table 31-1 is below the line), velocity variation is not the limiting factor.

From neck tension to the target. You can apply the same correlation formula to test the full chain: neck tension (or seating force) to muzzle velocity, and velocity to target height. If you log seating force in the workshop and match each round to its chronograph reading, compute the correlation between seating force and velocity. You will usually find very little correlation across normal neck interference. And because velocity explains very little vertical dispersion inside 300 to 600 yards, neck tension variation rarely reaches the target at all.

Flyers

Every shot counts once you commit to the target (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 14). A shot that looks far out is often just the normal tail of the group. In a round group about 14 shots in 100 land beyond two standard deviations of the center, so a 30-shot composite will hold about four (see Figure 31-3).

Compare a suspected flyer against the average distance of the remaining shots. Leaving the farthest shot out of that average keeps it from inflating the yardstick used to judge it.

  1. Take each shot’s distance from the group’s center, as in the mean radius worked example (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 15).
  2. Average the distances of all the shots except the farthest one.
  3. Divide the farthest shot’s distance by that average.
  4. Call it a far shot, a flyer worth a look, only when the ratio is above 3.

That line holds for one group of 5 to 30 shots. For a composite, measure each shot from its own group’s center and test the farthest shot once against all the others. The line of 3 still holds. Even then, about 1 clean target in 20 shows a far shot by chance. A far shot is a reason to look for a cause, such as a loose scope mount. Keep it in the record unless you documented the error when it happened.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

In the example in The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 15, the farthest shot is shot 9, at .711 inch from the center. The other nine add to 2.888 inches, an average of .321 inch. The ratio is .711 ÷ .321 = 2.2, under 3. Shot 9 is the normal tail of the group.

Common questions

QuestionShort answerSee
What makes a good first load?A full-case load, whose published maximum charge fills the case so a double charge cannot fit.13
Should I work up the charge or the seating depth first?The charge, with the bullet seated to the maker’s guidance. Test seating after that, with enough shots to see a real change.24, 28
What velocity SD is good enough?It depends on your distance and target. Inside about 300 yards a 15 fps SD adds only about .3 inch of vertical.PRH
How many shots do I need?Five shots can rule out a bad load. Proving a good one takes a composite of 20 to 30 shots.1, PRH
Should I measure CBTO or COAL?Both. Use CBTO, never COAL, for seating tests.21 COAL must still fit your magazine for rounds fed from it.24
How far off the lands should I seat?Start at the bullet maker’s guidance. Do not jam a hunting load.24
Why is my velocity different from the book?Your rifle is not the test barrel. Velocity above your velocity ceiling means pressure above the book’s.22 The ceiling is the book velocity at the maximum charge, lowered for a shorter barrel and never raised for a longer one.3, 15, 16, 28
Is annealing needed?Not for safety. It mainly extends neck life. A benefit on target is unproven at large sample sizes.21, PRH
Does neck tension matter?Less than many loaders expect. In live-fire tests it had no link to group size unless heavy enough to damage bullets.23 Keep one method of setting it across the lot.19
Is sorting brass by weight or uniforming primer pockets worth it?The evidence is thin. Case capacity drives pressure, so measure your brass’s capacity.11, 16, 22
Do pressure signs tell me a load is safe?No. A sign means stop now. Their absence proves nothing, so check velocity against the book.28
When do I have to work up again?After any change of primer, case maker, bullet or powder, after seating deeper than your worked-up seating or into the lands, and with a new barrel or a changed barrel length.2, 28, 30
My load shot a tiny group last week and a big one today. What changed?Maybe nothing. Of two 5-shot groups from one load, one is at least 1.5 times the other 30 percent of the time.1, PRH
What changes when I load for a semi-automatic?Bump the shoulder more, check every round in a case gauge, seat every primer below flush and choose powder for gas port pressure.26
Can I load a cartridge with no published data?Proceed with extreme caution, because there is no tested start load. An improved cartridge starts from its parent cartridge’s data, and a wildcat starts below a smaller-capacity comparison cartridge. To load near maximum pressure, send rounds to a test lab for pressure testing.16, 28
