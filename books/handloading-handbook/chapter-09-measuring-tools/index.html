Chapter 9. Measuring Tools

Key points

  • A caliper measures case length, loaded length and neck diameter. Comparators clamped to its jaws read base to shoulder and base to ogive.
  • Use the same comparator insert every time. A different insert gives a different number.
  • A runout gauge reads the total indicated runout (TIR) of a neck or a seated bullet. A ball micrometer reads neck wall thickness.
  • A case gauge checks a case against standard chamber dimensions, not your rifle’s throat. A modified-case gauge or a lands-gauge dummy finds the lands.
  • You can make the probe for incipient head separation from a paper clip.

Why it matters

Case prep and assembly run on measured numbers. You size to a base-to-shoulder reading, trim to a length and seat to a base-to-ogive length. These tools take those readings.

A reading means something only if you can repeat it. Use the same tool, the same insert and the same light touch every time. Then a change in the reading is a change in the brass or the round, not in the way you measured.

How it is done

Calipers

A dial or digital caliper of hardened stainless steel reads in steps of .001 or .0005 inch. Use it to measure case length, loaded round length and outside neck diameter (see Chapters 19, 20 and 24). Clean the jaws and check them against a zero stop or a reference standard before every session.

Base-to-shoulder comparators

The case’s fit in the chamber depends on its base-to-shoulder length, from the head to a datum line on the shoulder (see Chapter 4). A comparator body clamps to the caliper jaws and holds a hardened steel insert that meets the shoulder at a set diameter. Choose the insert its maker lists for your cartridge, and use the same insert every time. The absolute reading depends on the insert. The difference between two readings does not.

Bullet comparators

A bullet comparator is an aluminum body that clamps to a caliper. It holds an insert sized for each caliber. The insert rests on the bullet’s ogive at a fixed diameter, near where the lands meet the bullet, to measure cartridge base to ogive (CBTO; see Chapter 24). Overall length to the tip (COAL) can vary by .005 to .012 inch from round to round because bullet tips vary, even when the ogives match. CBTO is much more consistent and tracks jump to the lands directly.1 Use the same insert every time and write down which one.

Ball and tubing micrometers

A flat-anvil micrometer cannot measure the curved wall of a neck accurately. A ball micrometer has a round anvil that sits inside the neck against a flat spindle outside. It reads neck wall thickness to .0001 inch, so you can find thick and thin spots around the neck (see Chapter 19).

Runout gauges

A runout gauge, or concentricity gauge, rests the case on ball-bearing rollers under a dial test indicator. The indicator reads in steps of .0005 or .0001 inch. Turn the case, and the dial shows the total indicated runout (TIR) of the neck or of the seated bullet. Where the runout first shows points to its source (see Chapter 19).

Case gauges

A drop-in case gauge is a hardened steel cylinder reamed to the minimum SAAMI or C.I.P. chamber dimensions, with stepped reference ledges at the head and the mouth. Drop a sized case in. The ledges show at a glance whether the shoulder datum and case length are within limits for a factory chamber. A case too wide in the body will not drop in fully (see Chapter 25).

Lands gauges

Two tools find where the lands start in your chamber. Pick one and keep to it (see Chapter 24).

Modified-case gauge. A modified-case gauge is a rigid tube with a sliding plunger rod. A case drilled and tapped at the head (5/16-36 thread) screws onto it. In the chamber, the rod pushes a test bullet forward until its ogive just touches the lands. A bullet comparator then reads the CBTO at touch (see Chapter 24).

Lands-gauge dummy. A lands-gauge dummy is an unprimed, empty case with light neck tension and a bullet seated long. Mark the case so it can never be mistaken for a live round. When you close the bolt, the lands push the bullet back into the neck, and its new CBTO gives the distance to the lands (see Chapter 24).

Primer seating depth gauges

A primer seating depth gauge has a spring-loaded center probe in a wide, flat base that rests across the case head. It reads how far the primer cup sits below the head in steps of .0005 inch, so you can check the seating depth against its target (see Chapter 22).

Probes and borescopes

A feeler probe is a stiff wire with a short right-angle bend at the tip, filed to a point. Drawn up the inside wall of a fired case, it catches on the internal groove of an incipient head separation (see Chapter 18).

You can make one from a paper clip. Straighten the clip. Bend the last eighth of an inch to a right angle. File the tip to a point.

A digital borescope lets you see erosion or stress cracks inside the case.
