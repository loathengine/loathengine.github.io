Chapter 22. Primer Pockets and Priming

Key points

  • Pocket cleaning, uniforming and flash-hole deburring are optional. None has strong evidence behind it.
  • A primer fires most reliably when it sits firmly on the pocket floor, a little below flush.
  • Primers are made to fire when crushed, so priming is a job of controlled force.
  • A primer stays live until it is fired. An unusable primed case is fired or disposed of, never punched out.

Why it matters

The primer starts the shot (see Figure 12-1). Its flash passes through the flash hole and lights the powder. A primer that sits high or tilted can misfire. It can also make the action hard to close.

Primers are the most sensitive part of the cartridge. They are designed to detonate when crushed.1 So priming is a job of controlled force.

Pocket preparation is a separate question. Cleaning, uniforming and deburring are popular with precision loaders. Here is how to do each one in full. Then weigh what the evidence shows.

How it is done

Pocket work and priming happen at two different points in case prep. Remove any pocket crimp and do any optional pocket work after you trim (see Chapter 20) and before the final clean (see Chapter 18). The final clean then washes out the chips. Prime after the final clean and after you chamfer and deburr the mouth (see Chapter 20).

Set up for priming

Follow the workshop safety rules from Chapter 2. Wear eye protection, work over a hard bare floor and keep only one primer type on the workbench at a time.2 Keep primers in their factory trays until you seat them. Handle them with clean, dry hands. Return unused ones to storage when you finish.3

Check the pocket before you prime

A Berdan case has two small offset flash holes instead of one central hole. A standard decapping pin breaks on it, which is why you sorted out Berdan and steel cases before depriming (see Chapter 18).1 Next, check whether the pocket is ready to take a fresh primer.

Look for a primer pocket crimp. Many military cases have a swaged ring or stab marks that hold the primer in place.4 A fresh primer cannot seat past an intact crimp without crushing. Remove the crimp after trimming and before the final clean, as described below.

Removing a pocket crimp

A crimp comes out once. After that the pocket behaves like any other. Swage the crimp back into the pocket wall or cut it away with a reamer (see Chapter 7 for the tools). Cutting removes brass, so take care not to over-ream.4

  1. Start with decapped cases. Every batch was deprimed at the start of case prep (see Chapter 18). The crimp is easier to see with the old primer out.4
  2. Set up the tool as its maker directs. A cutter needs its depth set to remove the crimp and no more.
  3. Do one case and look. The ring or the stab marks should be gone. The pocket mouth should be smooth and even all the way round.
  4. Prime that case. Finish any other pocket work on it first, then seat a primer as described in “Seat the primer” below. Priming should feel normal.4 Because a primed case cannot go through the final clean, put a good test case in the “Primed. No powder. Fire before decapping.” container (see “When a primer goes wrong” below). If the primer still feels crunchy or too tight, stop and do not force it. Put that partly primed case in the “Primed. Do not chamber. Dispose.” container. Set the tool to remove a little more crimp and test the next case.
  5. Scrap any case whose primer goes in too easily. An over-cut pocket may not support the primer, which can cause damage or injury when fired.4 Put that case in the “Primed. Do not chamber. Dispose.” container and test the next case.
  6. Run the batch. Keep the finished cases in their own marked box.

Military brass also varies by headstamp in weight and capacity, so a change of headstamp is a change of component.4 Sort it by headstamp (see Chapter 18) and work up again for each one (see Chapter 28).

Optional pocket work

Keep notes on which cases had each job, so a later test can compare like with like.

Clean the pocket. Spent primers leave carbon fouling on the pocket floor. If heavy carbon stops a new primer from seating fully, remove the carbon with a primer pocket brush or scraper (see Chapter 7).

Uniform the pocket. Uniforming squares the pocket floor and brings every pocket to one depth. Cut each pocket with a uniforming tool until the tool reaches its stop (see Chapter 7 and Figure 22-1). Every primer then seats against the same floor. Uniform once, after any pocket crimp is removed. After that, the pocket needs only cleaning.

Deburr the flash hole. Punched flash holes often leave a burr inside the case (see Figure 22-2). Trim the burr flush with a flash-hole deburring tool (see Chapter 7). Never enlarge the flash hole. A larger hole speeds the primer flame and can spike pressure unpredictably.5

Uniforming and deburring leave chips in the case. The final clean takes them off before you prime (see Chapter 18).

Contested pocket prep gives more uniform ignition

The claim. Clean, uniform pockets and deburred flash holes give more uniform ignition and so less velocity spread.

The physics. The primer’s flame passes through the flash hole, and a burr can partly block it. A pocket of one depth lets every primer seat the same way. Both effects are real. Their size is the question.

The test. Split one lot of brass. Prepare half and leave half alone. Load both halves the same way. Fire them in interleaved blocks until each half has about 35 shots for mean radius, or about 50 for velocity SD. Those counts detect a 1.5 to 1 difference 8 times in 10. A 1.25 to 1 difference needs about 110 and 160 (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).

The data. Controlled evidence is thin and mixed. In one test series, deburring reduced velocity spread on punched flash holes but not on drilled ones.6 In one test program, heavy case prep on brass from a single lot gave no group benefit.7

The verdict. Plausible but unproven. Optional: do it if you enjoy it or if your own test shows a gain.

Seat the primer

  1. Use the primer your load data names. A primer other than the one tested can raise pressure dramatically.1 In percentage terms, changing the powder charge moves pressure about 3 times as much as velocity. Swapping the primer can move pressure 10 times as much as velocity, or more (see Chapter 12).8
  2. Turn the primers anvil up. Scatter them on a grooved flip tray. Shake it gently side to side until every primer lies anvil up.9 Fill the hand tool or the press arm from the tray (see Chapter 7 for the tools). Touch primers with your fingers as little as you can. On a progressive press, take special care when you fill and handle primer feed tubes.10
Safety · See Chapter 2

Seat every primer fully, so it sits slightly below flush.1 A primer that stands proud can make the action hard to close and can misfire.

  1. Seat with one steady stroke. Push the primer to the bottom of the pocket and feel it stop. A primer seated firmly on the pocket floor gives the best sensitivity.9 Use the same smooth stroke on every case.
  1. Stop at unusual resistance. Stop if a primer will not start, feels crunchy or needs more force than the cases before it. Find out why before you go on.1 Look for a crimped pocket, a tilted primer or carbon on the pocket floor (see Chapter 31 for other causes). Never force a primer.10
  2. Check every case by touch. Run a fingertip across the case head. A primer below flush feels like a shallow dip. A high or upside-down primer catches your finger. You can also stand the case on its head on a hard, flat surface. A high primer makes it rock (see Figure 22-3). A primer seating depth gauge gives a number if you want one (see Chapter 9). Primers and pockets are designed for the best sensitivity at .003 to .005 inch below flush.11 The industry standard for factory ammunition allows flush to .008 inch below.12 If a primer sits high, reseat it with the same steady stroke, stopping at any unusual resistance. Never push on a tipped or upside-down primer. If a primer stays high, tipped or upside down, put the case in the “Primed. Do not chamber. Dispose.” container (see “When a primer goes wrong” below).
  1. Cull loose pockets. If a primer slides in with almost no resistance, the pocket is loose. Discard cases with enlarged primer pockets.5 Because the case now holds a live primer in an unsafe pocket, put it in the “Primed. Do not chamber. Dispose.” container (see “When a primer goes wrong” below). Track how many you find in the batch. Several loose pockets after only a few firings mean the pressure is too high. Reduce the charge and set your maximum below that load (see Chapter 28).

When a primer goes wrong

Never pry a seated primer loose. Never punch out a live primer in a reloading die. Sort primed reject cases into two labeled containers by where the primer sits, and keep both apart from your primer supply. If you are unsure which container a case belongs in, use the second.

  • Primer below flush. Use a container labeled “Primed. No powder. Fire before decapping.” Fire each empty case in the rifle at the range, then decap it normally.2
  • Primer high, sideways, upside down or deformed. Use a container labeled “Primed. Do not chamber. Dispose.” Never chamber these cases, because closing the bolt can fire the primer before the bolt locks.13 Dispose of them as the primer maker’s published guidance directs.

Pick up dropped primers at once. One underfoot can fire. Return clean, undamaged primers to their original packaging. Do not seat a dented, gritty or oily primer. Never load a primer you cannot identify. Dispose of it, like a damaged primer, as a primer maker’s published guidance directs.10
