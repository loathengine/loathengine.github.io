Chapter 8. Scales and Powder Measures

On this page (9 sections)

  • Powder measures

Key points

  • A beam scale needs no power and never drifts with room temperature, so it makes a lasting reference. A digital scale reads fast but reacts to temperature swings, drafts, static and radio noise.
  • A scale that zeroes properly can still read wrong at load weight. Check it with reference weights near your target charge.
  • A measure throws ball and fine flake powders evenly and long stick powders less so. A baffle keeps its throws even as the hopper empties.
  • A trickler tops up a light charge on the scale. An automated dispenser weighs and trickles each charge for you.
  • A metal funnel that fits the neck puts every kernel in the case. A drop tube settles long kernels closer together.

Why it matters

You measure powder by weight or by volume. The charge must stay within tight limits to keep pressure safe and velocity consistent (see Chapter 23).

These tools dispense and weigh each charge, and each has its own weak spots. An electronic scale can drift. Any scale can read true at zero and wrong at charge weight. A powder measure’s throws vary with kernel shape and hopper level, and static can hold kernels inside a plastic funnel. Knowing how each tool works tells you what to watch for and which one to buy.

How it is done

Scales

Scales differ in how they sense weight and in what throws them off.

  • Mechanical beam scales. A beam scale balances an aluminum beam on polished agate or hardened steel knife edges. Sliding poise weights mark steps of 10 gr, 1 gr and 0.1 gr. Magnetic damping settles the beam quickly, without friction. A leveling screw in the base lines the pointer up with the zero mark. A beam scale needs no power, never drifts with room temperature and ignores electrical or radio noise, making it a reliable long-term reference. Keep the knife edges and agate bearings clean with a soft brush. Read the pointer square on, at eye level, to avoid parallax.
  • Digital strain-gauge scales. An electronic scale uses a cantilever load cell fitted with foil strain gauges. A charge on the pan bends the cell very slightly. That changes its electrical resistance, and the scale turns the change into a reading. The reading comes in a fraction of a second, but the scale is sensitive to its surroundings. Temperature swings make it drift, so let it warm up as its maker directs before you calibrate it. Drafts from vents or fans move the reading. Static on a plastic pan or on your clothes adds a false weight. Radio interference from phones, fluorescent light ballasts or Wi-Fi routers can make the numbers wander. Use a grounded metal pan and keep your phone away from the workbench. Re-zero often during a session.
  • Electromagnetic force restoration (EMFR) balances. A laboratory-grade EMFR balance holds the pan still with a magnet coil. It reads the weight from the current the coil needs. An EMFR scale resolves 0.02 gr or 0.001 gram, about the weight of one kernel of extruded rifle powder. It is very sensitive, so it comes with a glass or acrylic draft shield. Set it on a heavy granite slab to damp vibration.

A finer scale pays off only when scale error makes up a meaningful share of your velocity spread. Calculate how much velocity spread your current scale adds before you buy a finer one (see Chapter 23, “What a charge error costs”). Verify whichever scale you use at the start of every session (see Chapter 23). You can use the same scale to weigh cases for water capacity (see Chapter 11).

Check weights

Check weights are certified Class F or ASTM reference masses, often marked in grams. A scale that zeroes correctly is not always accurate across its range. Use check weights near the charges you actually load. For example, combine a 20 gr and a 30 gr weight to check the scale at 50.0 gr (see Chapter 23 for the check and the conversion from grams). Handle check weights with plastic tweezers or clean cotton gloves. Oil from your fingers changes their mass for good.

Powder measures

A measure throws a charge by volume.

  • Volumetric powder measures. A drum measure has a rotating drum under a powder hopper. The drum holds a cavity you set by micrometer. Raise the handle to fill the cavity. Lower it to turn the cavity over the spout and throw the charge. A measure is fast and usually very accurate, especially with ball powders.1 How well it meters depends on the powder’s shape (see Chapter 13).1 Ball powders and fine flake powders flow like liquid and often throw within ±0.05 to ±0.1 gr. Long extruded stick powders can bridge across the cavity mouth or shear against the drum edge. When the drum cuts kernels, you feel a crunch and throws can vary by ±0.3 to ±0.5 gr. Work the handle with the same smooth rhythm every time. Pause briefly at the top and bottom of each stroke (see Chapter 23).
  • Powder baffles. A curved or V-shaped metal baffle sits in the lower third of the hopper, just above the drum. Without a baffle, a tall column of powder packs the cavity tighter when the hopper is full, so throws start heavy and lighten as the hopper empties. A baffle takes the weight of the powder column off the drum so throws stay consistent from the first case to the last.

Tricklers

A trickler tops up a charge on the scale. A manual trickler has a heavy, steady base, a small powder reservoir and a threaded feed tube. Turn the knob, and a spiral inside the tube moves a few kernels at a time out into the scale pan. Throw a charge slightly light from a measure. Then trickle it up to the exact weight in seconds (see Chapter 23).

Automated dispensers

An automated dispenser weighs and trickles each charge for you. It joins an electronic scale to a motorized trickler. Makers include RCBS, Hornady and Lyman. A large tube feeds the first 90 to 95 percent of the charge fast. A small tube or a stepper motor then trickles single kernels up to the programmed weight. Modern units learn how a given powder flows, so they overshoot less. If a charge overshoots the target, pour it back into the hopper and dispense it again. Never seat a bullet over a charge that overshot. Check the dispenser’s scale with check weights like any other (see Chapter 23).

Funnels and drop tubes

A funnel guides the charge into the case. A drop tube settles it.

  • Caliber-matched powder funnels. An aluminum or brass funnel is machined to fit snugly over one neck size. A universal plastic funnel builds static, and fine flakes or kernels cling to its walls and drop late. A metal funnel drains the static, so every kernel goes into the case.
  • Drop tubes. A drop tube is an aluminum or brass tube that sits between the funnel and the case mouth. It is typically four to six inches long. As the powder falls down the tube, long stick kernels line up and nest closer together in the case. That raises the bulk fill density by 2 to 4 percent. A charge that would be lightly compressed can then settle lower, so the bullet seats with less push-back or crunching (see Chapter 23).
