Chapter 20. Trimming and Chamfering

Key points

  • Cases grow when they are fired and sized, so length is final only after sizing.
  • A case longer than the maximum case length must never be loaded. It can pinch the bullet and raise pressure.
  • The trim-to length sits .005 to .010 inch below the maximum. That margin lets a case grow for a firing or more.
  • Every trim leaves a burr. An unchamfered mouth can shave the bullet jacket or crush the shoulder in seating.
  • Growth per firing tells you when to trim, and fast growth can point to too much shoulder bump.

Why it matters

Brass flows when a case fires and again when it is sized. Some of it moves forward into the neck, and the case gets longer. How fast it grows depends on the chamber, the load and how hard the sizing die works the brass. No rule of thumb fits every rifle, so you measure.

A chamber’s neck is cut a little longer than the longest case it is made for. Ahead of the neck, the chamber narrows into the throat. A case that has grown too long pushes its mouth into that narrowing. The mouth is squeezed onto the bullet, so the bullet is held harder and starts to move later (see Figure 20-1). Pressure rises. A long case can also make the bolt hard to close.

The trim-to length leaves a margin below the maximum. A trimmed case can then grow for a firing or more before it reaches the limit.

Uniform length has a second purpose. Cases of one length should grip and seat bullets the same way.

How it is done

Know your two numbers

Every cartridge has two published lengths. The maximum case length is the industry limit. The trim-to length is the length you cut back to. It is usually .005 to .010 inch under the maximum.1

CartridgeMaximum case length (inch)Trim-to length (inch)
.308 Winchester2.0152.005
6.5 Creedmoor1.9201.910

Example numbers. Not load data. Take yours from the load data for your cartridge.

Trimming a little under the trim-to length buys more firings before the next trim. The cost is a slightly shorter neck to hold the bullet. Pick one target length for each batch and keep it.

Prepare new brass

New brass is not always uniform. Mouths can arrive dented or out of round, and lengths can vary within a box. Run new cases through your sizing die or over an expander mandrel to round out any dented mouths (see Chapter 19). Then measure and trim them to one length, give them a final clean (see Chapter 18), and chamfer and deburr the mouths just before priming.

Measure

  1. Wipe the case, then measure. Wipe the lube off each case before you measure it. The rest of the lube comes off in the final clean, after trimming (see Chapter 18). Lube left on a case can reach the powder. Measure after sizing, since sizing makes cases longer. If you use a mandrel, measure after that step too.
  1. Measure from the head to the mouth. Zero the caliper and wipe its jaws (see Chapter 9). Hold the case square in the jaws. Turn it a quarter turn and measure again. Use the longer reading.
Safety · See Chapter 2

A case longer than the maximum can jam its mouth into the end of the chamber neck. The mouth then pinches the bullet and pressure rises. Stretched cases may cause chambering problems and higher than expected pressures.2 Never load a case longer than the maximum.

  1. Sort by length. Use the table below, with one tray for each row.
Measured lengthAction
Over the maximumTrim before loading. No exceptions.
Between trim-to and maximumTrim now, or leave it until the batch reaches its trim line (see “Track growth and decide when to trim” below).
At or under trim-toLoad as is, or trim the batch to one length.

Many precision loaders trim every case every time. In a 2024 survey, 58 percent of top Precision Rifle Series (PRS) shooters did.3 That keeps a batch at one length.

Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Trimming every case to one length every firing improves precision. Uniform length should make grip and seating more uniform. No large-sample public test shows a gain on target. The 58 percent survey figure shows popularity, not proof.

A case well under the trim-to length is safe to load. It holds less of the bullet, though, so keep it out of a batch you trim to one length.

Trim

Choose a trimmer to suit how many cases you load (see Chapter 7). A trimmer that locates on the shoulder cuts even lengths only when the shoulders are sized evenly. That is why trimming follows sizing. Keep cutters sharp. A dull cutter tears brass and leaves heavy burrs.

  1. Set the trimmer with a test case. Use a sized case. Cut it and measure it (see Figure 20-2). Adjust until it comes out at your target length.
  1. Trim the batch. Measure every fifth case. If the length drifts, stop and reset.

After trimming, remove any pocket crimps and do any optional pocket work (see Chapter 22). Then give the batch its final clean (see Chapter 18).

Chamfer and deburr

Chamfer and deburr after the final clean (see Chapter 18), just before you prime. Tap each case mouth down to drop the chips.

A 3-in-1 trimmer chamfers and deburrs as it trims, before the final clean. If you use one, check each mouth after the clean, and touch up any that needs it with steps 6 and 7.

  1. Chamfer the inside of the mouth. Turn the tool a few times with light pressure. Stop when an even bevel shows all the way round. A knife edge is too much (see Figure 20-4). Choose the chamfer angle to suit your bullets (see Chapter 7). Use one tool for the whole batch.
  1. Deburr the outside. Remove the ridge the cutter left (see Figure 20-5). Stop when the edge feels smooth.
  1. Clear the chips and inspect. Tap the case mouth down and brush the neck. Look at the mouth under light. No chips may stay in the case.

A mouth left without a chamfer can shave the bullet jacket as it seats. It can also need so much seating force that the shoulder crushes (see Chapters 25 and 31).4

Flare the mouth for flat-base bullets

A boat-tail bullet guides itself into the case neck. A flat-base bullet has a square heel that sits on top of the case mouth, where it can tip during seating or shave jacket metal on the mouth edge. A slight flare gives a flat-base bullet a smooth lead-in.5

  1. Chamfer first, then try a dummy. An even chamfer is often enough for a jacketed flat-base bullet. Seat a bullet in a sized case with no primer and no powder (see Chapter 24). If the base enters square and no curl of jacket shows at the mouth, you are done.
  2. Flare slightly if the base still catches. Use a two-step neck expanding die (see Chapter 5). Turn the expander down in small steps until a bullet base just enters the mouth by hand. Flare every case in the batch at the same setting.
  3. Keep the flare small. Too much flare works the brass and shortens case life. Too little lets the mouth shave the bullet as it seats.1 A mouth that is still flared after seating can catch as the round chambers. Close it with a taper crimp die set just to touch the mouth (see Chapters 5 and 24).

Track growth and decide when to trim

  1. Log the sized length of each batch. Measure every case for the first few firings. After that, measure the same 10 cases after each sizing. Compare the averages from firing to firing.
Average sized length of 10 cases (inch)
After firing 1    2.0060
After firing 2    2.0085
After firing 3    2.0110
Growth per firing = (2.0110 − 2.0060) ÷ 2 = .0025
Margin left       = (2.0150 − 2.0110) ÷ .0025 = 1.6 firings
Longest of the 10 after firing 3   2.0120
Trim line         = 2.0150 − .0025 = 2.0125

Example numbers. Not load data. The average still has room, but the longest cases reach the limit first. The longest case is just under the trim line, so load this batch once more and trim it at the next sizing.

Trim the batch when the longest case in the sample is within one firing’s growth of the maximum. Before you load a batch untrimmed, check every case against the maximum. The sample sets when to trim. It does not clear the cases you did not measure.

Write the growth rate on the brass batch’s page in your log. If your sizing is steady, the rate should stay steady too. If growth suddenly speeds up, the shoulder may be bumped too far (see Chapter 19).
