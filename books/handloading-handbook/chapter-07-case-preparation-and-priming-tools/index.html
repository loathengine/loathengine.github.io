Chapter 7. Case Preparation and Priming Tools

On this page (8 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • Trimmers
  • Chamfer and deburring tools
  • Primer pocket tools
  • Annealers

Key points

  • Choose a trimmer to suit how many cases you load. A lathe trimmer, a motorized 3-in-1 trimmer and a press-mounted trim die all cut the case back to length.
  • An inside chamfer and an outside deburr smooth the mouth after trimming. A shallow chamfer suits long boat-tail bullets.
  • A swager removes a military pocket crimp without removing metal. A piloted reamer with a depth stop cuts away only the crimp.
  • Flame, induction and salt-bath annealers all soften the neck. Temperature-indicating paint sets the dwell of any of them.
  • A hand priming tool lets you feel each primer bottom. A press arm or a progressive feed hides that feel.

Why it matters

These tools work on the case between the first clean and the loaded round. A trimmer keeps every case in a batch at one length. Chamfer and deburring tools give the bullet a smooth entry. Pocket tools ready the pocket for a new primer. An annealer softens necks that firing and sizing have hardened. A priming tool seats each primer firmly on the pocket floor.

Each tool trades speed against gentle brass handling and repeatable dimensions. Annealing, pocket uniforming and flash-hole deburring are optional (see “Which prep tasks to do” in the Part II opener).

How it is done

Trimmers

Firing and sizing draw brass forward into the neck, so the case grows. A case that is too long jams its mouth into the end of the chamber neck. That can spike pressure dangerously (see Chapter 20). Choose a trimmer to suit how many cases you load. Each type locates the case on a different surface.

  • Manual lathe trimmers. A lathe trimmer holds the case head in a collet or a universal shell holder on a rigid base. You turn a high-speed steel or carbide cutter against the mouth with a hand crank or a cordless drill. A pilot sized for the caliber rides in the neck and keeps the cut square to the case axis. A standard lathe trimmer indexes off the case head, so an adjustable micrometer stop sets the length from head to mouth. A rotating or carbide pilot cuts friction and keeps the pilot from galling the inside of the neck.
  • Motorized 3-in-1 trimmers. A motorized trimmer feeds the case into a spinning carbide cutter head. Many index off the shoulder datum rather than the case head, so the neck length past the shoulder stays even. A trimmer that locates on the shoulder cuts even lengths only when the shoulders are sized evenly (see Chapter 19). The head has three cutting edges. In two to three seconds per case it trims to length, cuts the inside chamfer and deburrs the outside. Three hand jobs become one pass.
  • Press-mounted motorized trim dies. A motorized trim die screws into a single-stage or progressive press. The die body full-length sizes the case while a motor at the top of the die spins a carbide end mill at 3,000 rpm or more. At the top of the stroke, the mill shears off any neck that sticks out past the die, and a port draws the chips into a shop vacuum. Because a vacuum can ignite spilled powder, run this tool only during case prep with all powder and primers put away. It trims several hundred cases an hour. The cut leaves a flat, square edge, so you still need to chamfer the inside of the mouth after the final clean, just before priming (see Chapter 20).

Chamfer and deburring tools

Trimming leaves a sharp corner inside the mouth and a burr outside. An inside chamfer tool cuts a smooth entry bevel for the bullet base. A standard tool cuts a steep 45-degree angle. A Very Low Drag (VLD) chamfer tool cuts a shallower 15- to 22-degree taper. That shallow angle guides a long boat-tail bullet into the neck without peeling copper from the jacket. An outside deburring tool bevels the outer edge of the mouth so no burr keeps the round from chambering freely. Both tools can be turned by hand or mounted on a motorized case prep center. In the case-prep order, chamfering and deburring come after the final clean, just before priming (see Chapter 20).

Primer pocket tools

These tools remove military crimps and clean, square or deburr the pocket and flash hole (see Chapter 22).

  • Primer pocket swagers. Military brass, such as 5.56x45mm NATO and 7.62x51mm NATO, has a crimp staked into the case head to hold the primer under automatic fire. It is a full ring or three stab marks.1 A swager presses that displaced brass back into the pocket wall. A bench-mounted swager pushes a hardened support rod through the case mouth to back up the web while a lever drives a carbide mandrel into the pocket. A press-mounted swager uses a die body and a cup on the ram of a single-stage press. Swaging removes no metal and keeps the case head at full strength. It leaves a smooth, rounded entry that guides a new primer in. It is the better choice for large batches.1
  • Pocket reamers and countersink cutters. A reamer cuts the crimp away with a fluted cutter turned by hand, in a cordless drill or on a motorized case prep center. Reaming is fast. But an aggressive or unguided cutter held off center can remove supporting wall brass or leave an oval, oversized pocket mouth. A pocket cut too far may not support its primer properly.1 A piloted reamer with a depth stop collar removes only the crimped ring.
  • Pocket brushes and scrapers. Small stainless or brass wire brushes and flat scrapers clean baked-on carbon from the pocket floor between firings. Built-up carbon would hold the next primer high.
  • Primer pocket uniformers. A uniformer is a flat-bottomed carbide cutter with a shoulder stop. In a handle or a motorized spindle, it cuts the pocket floor flat and square to the case axis, to one depth across the lot. It also clears manufacturing burrs.
  • Flash-hole deburring tools. A punched flash hole often leaves a ragged burr inside the case web. A deburring tool has a long shaft that enters through the case mouth, a conical pilot that centers it and a cutting head that shaves the burr flat. A fixed stop collar keeps the cutter from enlarging the flash hole.

Annealers

Firing and sizing work-harden brass. Hard necks grow brittle and split. Controlled heat softens the neck and shoulder and leaves the case head hard (see Chapter 21). The three methods are flame, induction and molten salt.

  • Flame annealers. A flame annealer turns cases on a motorized wheel or rollers through one or two torches burning propane or MAPP gas. The case must keep turning. A case that sits still heats unevenly and ends up harder on one side. Dwell is usually four to eight seconds. The flame is set so its inner blue cone touches the junction of neck and shoulder without heating the lower body.
  • Induction annealers. An induction annealer passes high-frequency alternating current through a copper work coil. Its field heats the case neck quickly and evenly, in two to three seconds. With no flame, it also avoids the drift that torch pressure and drafts bring. Some units read the conductivity and mass of the case and set the power and time themselves.
  • Molten salt baths. A salt bath melts a 50/50 mix of sodium nitrate and potassium nitrate in a temperature-controlled electric pot at 900 to 1,000 °F (482 to 538 °C). You dip the case neck for a short dwell, typically four to six seconds. Liquid salt passes heat very evenly. Any moisture carried into the pot causes an instant, violent steam explosion that throws molten salt. Cases must be fully dry. Nitrate salt feeds a fire. Keep anything that burns away from the pot, and store the salt sealed and dry. Wear a full face shield, heavy heat-resistant gloves and a leather apron.
  • Temperature-indicating paint. Temperature-indicating paint, or lacquer, melts at a rated temperature. Use it to set the dwell of any annealer. A mark inside the neck shows whether the brass got hot enough to soften. The paint shows the heat, not the hardness, so it is an indirect check. A mark low on the body, near the head, shows whether the heat stayed out of the head and web. The head and web must stay hard to contain firing pressure (see Chapter 21 for the ratings and the setup).

Priming tools

Priming presses a small explosive cup into the case head. Steady, even force seats it firmly on the pocket floor without crushing the pellet or bending the cup (see Chapter 22).

The tool’s leverage decides whether you seat by feel or against a mechanical stop.

  • Hand priming tools. A squeeze handle drives a steel primer punch up into a shell holder. You feel the moment the anvil feet touch the pocket floor, then the slight extra resistance as the cup seats below flush. Most modern hand tools have a safety gate that separates the primer on the punch from the primers in the tray. If that primer pops, the gate keeps it from setting off a chain fire in the tray.2 Hand tools take their own snap-in shell holders or standard press shell holders (see Chapter 5). They let you prime away from the workbench.
  • Press priming arms and ram punches. Many single-stage presses come with a priming arm that swings into a slot in the ram, or a priming cup at the bottom of the stroke. A press priming arm needs no extra tool, but the press’s leverage hides resistance. That leverage is often between 15 and 25 to 1. It is easy to crush the pellet or flatten the cup against a tight or crimped pocket without noticing.
  • Bench-mounted priming tools. A bench-mounted tool (such as those from RCBS, Forster and Primal Rights) bolts to the edge of the workbench. A dedicated lever or rack-and-pinion drive gives a smooth, straight seating stroke. Many have an adjustable stop that seats every primer to the same depth below the case head. Set the stop so primers still bottom out in the deepest pockets of the lot, or a shallow stop will leave them high. Primers feed from a horizontal tray or a vertical magazine tube.
  • Progressive press priming systems. A progressive press primes at one station of its cycle. A slide bar, rotating disc or shuttle carries a primer from a vertical magazine tube over the seating punch as the ram cycles. With several dies working at once, you feel almost nothing of the seating. Keep the primer tube inside its heavy steel blast shield. If a pinched or upside-down primer goes off, the shield contains the fragments and vents the blast straight up, away from your face and eyes.3

Primer handling tools. These tools turn primers the right way up and load them into a priming tool. A primer seating depth gauge checks how deep they sit (see Chapter 9).

  • Primer flip trays. A flip tray is a shallow pan with ridges molded into its floor. Pour the primers from the maker’s sleeve into the tray and swirl it gently. Upside-down primers flip over the ridges until all 100 sit anvil side up. Trays of anti-static plastic or heavy metal drain off static charge. Working from a tray also keeps skin oil off the primer cups. From the tray, load primers straight into a hand tool or pick them up into a magazine tube.2
  • Primer pick-up tubes. A pick-up tube is a long, thin aluminum or brass tube with a flexible plastic tip at one end and a cotter pin at the other. Press the tip over an anvil-up primer in the flip tray, and the primer goes into the tube. Repeat until the tube holds all 100. To load a bench tool or progressive press, invert the tube over the magazine and pull the cotter pin so the primers slide in. A full tube is a column of primers that can go off together. Wear safety glasses, and fill and empty the tube gently (see Chapters 2 and 22).4
