Chapter 16. Planning the Load

Key points

  • A plan made on paper rules out bad ideas before they cost a bullet, a primer or barrel life.
  • A load model shows what to expect between the published start and maximum. Every predicted pressure is for learning, never a replacement for published data.
  • An improved cartridge starts from its parent’s published data. A wildcat borrows a start load, a maximum charge and a velocity ceiling from a published cartridge with the same bore and a capacity close to its own, but no larger.
  • Without published data, only a pressure test measures pressure.
  • Plan the work-up on paper before you load: the steps, the rounds per step and the two limits.

Why it matters

Every shot fired to learn something costs a bullet, a primer, some powder, range time and a little barrel life. A plan rules out bad ideas before they cost anything. A bullet your twist cannot stabilize, a powder too fast or too slow for the case and a velocity goal the cartridge cannot reach can all be caught on paper.

How it is done

Set the goal

Write one line before anything else: what the load is for, at what distance and at what velocity. “A match load for 600 yards, still above Mach 1.2 at the target” is a goal. Mach 1.2 is 1.2 times the speed of sound, about 1,340 fps in standard air. “The most accurate load possible” is not, because it gives you no way to choose and no point at which to stop.

You use that target velocity twice: first to shortlist powders below, and later to pick your working charge after the work-up (see Chapter 28). Let the job set the velocity. A hunting bullet must stay above its maker’s minimum expansion velocity at your longest shot (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 22). A target bullet should stay above Mach 1.2 to your farthest target (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapters 19 and 20). If the job sets no number, pick a velocity that published data reaches well below the maximum charge, adjusted for your barrel length (see Chapter 3).

For a load you will shoot at distance, add the target size and the hit chance you want. They turn into limits for mean radius and velocity SD (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 21).

Check that the bullet will fly

No load can fix a bullet that will not stabilize.

  1. Before you buy any bullets, work out the stability factor (Sg) with the Miller twist rule and the catalog bullet length (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 18). Use your twist and the coldest, densest air you expect to shoot in. Before you have a measured velocity, use the published velocity adjusted for your barrel (see Chapter 3). Aim for 1.5 or more.
  2. Buy one box and measure a few bullets with calipers. Work out Sg again with the longest before you buy a lot. Catalog lengths are often nominal.1

Shortlist powders from published data

  1. Open your published load data at your cartridge and your exact bullet.
  2. List every powder with data for that bullet.
  3. Adjust each powder’s maximum velocity for your barrel length (see Chapter 3 and “Plan the work-up” below). Then compare it with your goal.
  4. Keep the powders that reach your goal below their maximum charge, with room to spare.
  5. Prefer powders that fill the case well. Some manuals print load density or flag compressed loads. A charge that leaves much of the case empty can ignite less consistently, and ball powders suffer most.1 For a first load in a new cartridge, choose a full-case load (see Chapter 13).
Safety · See Chapter 2

Never swap one powder for another because they sit close together on a burn chart (see Chapter 13).

  1. Look the survivors up on a burn chart to see where they sit (see Chapter 13).
  2. Choose two or three candidates. Every charge you load will still come from published data for that exact powder. Each candidate gets its own work-up from its published start load. You then screen them with a few shots each, drop any that is clearly worse and confirm the best with a large sample (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapters 14 to 16).

Before you buy, count the rounds. Each candidate needs its work-up: every step from start to maximum, one to three rounds a step (see “Plan the work-up” below). Each also needs 5 to 10 screening shots. The finalist needs 20 to 30 more to confirm it (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapters 15 and 16). Add foulers, the shots that condition a clean bore (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 14), and a margin. Buy bullets and primers of one lot for the total, and each candidate powder from one lot. Cases are reused, so buy enough for the largest batch you will load at once.

A hunter confirming one proven hunting bullet and powder does not need multi-candidate match screening. Plan for the single work-up plus 10 to 20 rounds at the working charge to confirm zero, chronograph velocity for a DOPE card (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapters 10 and 20), and verify reliable feeding.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. Three candidates, each with 10 steps of two rounds and 10 screening shots, need 90 rounds. Add 30 to confirm the finalist and 15 foulers: 135 bullets and primers before the margin.

Measure your case capacity

Take five to ten fired, unsized cases from your own rifle and leave their spent primers in. Fill each with water and weigh the water (see Chapter 11). If your brass is new, measure it after its first firing. The first work-up starts at the published start load and does not wait on the figure. Case capacity is one of the biggest levers on pressure, and cases from different makers can differ by a couple of grains of water.1 Less room means higher pressure and velocity from the same charge. The model needs the figure, and so do your notes.

Predict before you load

On paper, published data gives you two points: the start load and the maximum load, each with a test-barrel velocity. Between them, velocity rises roughly in step with the charge. You cannot estimate pressure between those points by hand. You know only that the maximum charge stayed within the limit in the test barrel, with the test components.

A load model fills in the space between those points. Use it to learn what to expect. It is never intended to replace the published start and maximum.

Safety · See Chapter 2

Results from any interior-ballistics program rest on assumptions and averages. They are meant to be used together with current loading manuals and cross-checked against tested data.2

Plan the work-up

Write the plan before you load a round. It sets the steps you load in the workshop and the two lines you stop at on the range (see Chapter 28).

  1. Choose the published data whose test components match yours (see Chapter 15). In a semi-automatic, use the maker’s gas-gun data where it exists (see Chapter 26). Copy the start charge, the maximum charge and the published (“book”) velocity for each.
  2. Choose the CBTO you will shoot based on the bullet maker’s guidance, your distance to the lands and your magazine length (see Chapter 24). If you have not measured the lands yet, start from the published COAL, or from your magazine length if the rounds will feed from the magazine and the magazine is shorter. Seat a marked dummy round to that overall length and measure its CBTO (see Chapter 24, step 2). Your work-up holds only at that seating depth.
  3. Adjust the book velocity for your barrel (see Chapter 3). For a shorter barrel, subtract the velocity lost per inch for each inch of difference. Published barrel tests lose about 17 to 43 fps per inch, depending on the cartridge.3 Use 43 unless a published barrel test for your cartridge shows its own figure, because the larger loss gives the lower ceiling. For a longer barrel, leave the book velocity as printed. You will stop a little early, which is the safe direction.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. The published data lists 2,900 fps at its maximum in a 24-inch test barrel. Your barrel is 22 inches. Two inches at 43 fps per inch is 86 fps, so your velocity ceiling is 2,814 fps.

  1. Write down your two limits: the book maximum charge and the adjusted book velocity. The adjusted velocity is your velocity ceiling. At the range you also stop at any pressure sign, at a step that gains far more velocity than the steps before it, or at anything abnormal (see Chapter 28).
  2. Plan the steps: 0.2 to 0.5 gr at a time in rifle loads, and no more than about 2 percent of the charge.4 As you near the maximum, use the smaller step.
  3. Work out the book velocity for each step’s charge. Between the published start and maximum, estimate it in proportion to the charge. Adjust each result for a shorter barrel as in step 3, and write it beside the step.
fraction      = (charge − start) ÷ (max − start)
book velocity ≈ v_start + fraction × (v_max − v_start)
  1. Decide how many rounds to load per step. One shot per step is enough for safety, though not for judging accuracy (see Chapter 29). Two or three per step give a steadier velocity trend near the top.
  2. Weigh every charge (see Chapter 23). Seat every round at the CBTO from step 2. Load each step in its own labeled row or box. Mark each case with its step number before you charge it, and charge one step at a time.

Improved cartridges and wildcats: borrowing published data

Look first for published data for your exact cartridge. Some improved cartridges have it. The .280 Ackley Improved became a SAAMI standard in 2008.5 Its maximum average pressure is 65,000 psi, against 60,000 psi for the .280 Remington.6 Several bullet makers publish load data for the .280 Ackley Improved.7 A few other improved cartridges have published data too, such as the .257 Roberts Ackley Improved.8 Published data for the cartridge itself beats every method below.

When there is none, handloaders borrow from a cartridge that has data. How they borrow depends on the kind of cartridge.

An improved cartridge keeps the parent case’s head, length and neck. The body is blown out straighter and the shoulder made steeper, so the case holds more powder. Ackley Improved chambers are traditionally cut with headspace about .003 to .004 inch shorter than the parent’s.9 A parent case then fits snugly at the joint of its neck and shoulder. That holds its head against the bolt face while it fire-forms.

  1. Ask the gunsmith whether the chamber holds parent brass at the neck-shoulder joint. Not every improved reamer does.
  2. If it does not, form a false shoulder to hold the case head against the bolt face while it forms. Expand the neck to a larger diameter. Then run the case into the improved cartridge’s full-length die until it just fits the chamber snugly. Without a hold like this, the case forms with too much headspace. That can ruin the brass or even rupture the case.10
  3. Fire-form with parent factory ammunition, or with parent handloads worked up from the published start load like any new load (see Chapter 28). Expect less velocity than the same load gives in the parent’s chamber.11 Some of the energy goes into forming the case.
  4. Measure the capacity of the formed cases (see “Measure your case capacity” above).
  5. Work up from the parent’s published start load (see Chapter 28). The improved case holds more powder, so a parent charge makes less pressure in it.11
  6. Stop at the parent’s maximum charge or barrel-adjusted maximum velocity, whichever comes first. The larger improved case usually hits the charge limit first, which keeps you on the safe side.
  7. To go past either stop, you need published data for the improved cartridge or a comparison cartridge as described below. Otherwise pay for a pressure test.

A wildcat made by necking a case up or down changes the bore. The parent’s data no longer applies. Borrow from a comparison cartridge instead.12

  1. Find a published cartridge with the same bore and a case capacity close to yours, but no larger. Charges from a larger case raise pressure in a smaller one (see “Measure your case capacity” above). Measure both with water if you can, and prefer a maximum pressure no higher than the parent’s.
  2. Use the comparison’s start and maximum charges for your bullet weight, and its barrel-adjusted maximum velocity as your velocity ceiling.
  3. Work up from that start (see Chapter 28). Stop at that maximum charge, the velocity ceiling or any pressure sign, whichever comes first.

Both methods rest on velocity. Velocity tracks pressure only between like barrels, bores and bullets. A different throat, freebore or neck clearance changes the pressure behind the same velocity. Matching a borrowed velocity does not prove you match its pressure (see Chapter 28).13 Treat a borrowed stop as an estimate and keep a margin below it.

Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Borrowing from a parent or comparison cartridge is long-standing practice, and the physics supports it (see Chapter 28). No known published test measures how far a borrowed velocity ceiling can be off. Only a pressure test checks it.

When to pay for pressure testing

Only an instrument measures pressure. Pay for a pressure test when nothing published covers your load: a wildcat loaded near its ceiling, or published sources that disagree widely for your exact components.14

Ballistics laboratories measure pressure with transducers in test barrels made for each cartridge.15 Few test handloads for individuals, though some European proof-house laboratories test samples of 10 or 20 rounds.16 Ask first whether the laboratory has a test barrel for your chambering.
