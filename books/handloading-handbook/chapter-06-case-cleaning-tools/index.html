Chapter 6. Case Cleaning Tools

On this page (7 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • Dry tumblers

  • Ultrasonic cleaners
  • Separating and drying

Key points

  • Deprime first. A coarse clean protects dies and shows defects. A final clean after sizing and trimming takes off the lube and chips.
  • Dry tumblers scrub cases in walnut or corncob media. Their dust carries lead, so wear a fitted respirator when you empty them.
  • Wet tumblers with stainless steel pins clean inside and out, down to bare metal. Too much citric acid or a long soak weakens the brass.
  • Ultrasonic cleaners loosen carbon by cavitation, without abrasion or airborne lead dust.
  • Dry every case fully. Water trapped in a pocket or flash hole can kill a primer or spoil a powder charge.

Why it matters

Fired cases carry carbon, powder residue and range grit. Grit wears sizing dies and chamber walls. Dirt also hides cracks. Clean cases measure true (see Chapter 18).

Each cleaning method trades speed against wear on the brass. Choose a method based on your batch size, the time you have and how clean you want the cases.

How it is done

Depriming comes first, with a universal decapping die (see Chapter 5). A coarse clean then takes off grit and heavy grime before inspection and sizing. A final clean after sizing and trimming takes off the lube and chips (see Chapter 18). Shine is cosmetic.

Dry tumblers

A dry vibratory tumbler uses a motor with an off-center weight to shake a bowl of dry media. The media rolls in a ring and scrubs the cases clean in two to four hours. Crushed walnut hull has sharp, angular edges that strip stubborn outside carbon and baked-on soot quickly. Corncob (14/20 or 20/40 mesh) is softer and more absorbent. It burnishes brass to a high gloss rather than cutting heavy deposits. A capful of liquid polymer polish or mineral spirits cuts the dust and leaves a thin film that slows tarnish. Strips of used dryer sheet collect loose dust.

Dry tumbling makes fine dust that carries lead from fired primers. Use a sealed bowl. Run it outdoors or in a well-ventilated room. Wear a fitted respirator with N100 or P100 filters when you empty the media (see Chapter 2).1 Dry media also packs into primer pockets and lodges in flash holes. Inspect every case after tumbling and clear any blocked flash holes by hand (see Chapter 18).

Wet tumblers with pins

A rotary wet tumbler turns a watertight drum filled with water, cases and magnetic stainless steel pins. Add a little mild liquid detergent to lift oils and a pinch of citric acid (about 1/4 teaspoon per gallon). The pins clean the inside walls, the necks and the primer pockets down to bare metal in one to two hours. Pins can lodge endwise in a flash hole or crosswise in a small neck. Check every case after you separate the media (see Chapter 18).

Too much citric acid, or a long soak, leaches zinc out of the brass. This is dezincification. It leaves a telltale pink color and a weaker case. The used water carries lead from primer residue. Wear gloves or wash up after handling it (see Chapter 2).

Ultrasonic cleaners

Piezoelectric transducers in the tank floor send sound waves through the liquid. The waves form tiny vapor bubbles that collapse hard against the brass. This is cavitation. It loosens carbon inside and out without abrasion. Run the tank for several minutes before you add brass. This degassing drives off dissolved air that would otherwise weaken the cavitation.

An ultrasonic cleaner strips carbon without making airborne lead dust. It leaves a matte, satin finish rather than a shine. The used liquid still carries lead from primer residue. Wear gloves or wash up after handling it (see Chapter 2). Rinse and dry the cases fully before loading.

Separating and drying

Separate cases and pins with a rotary sifter and a magnetic pick-up wand. Then dry the cases fully before you anneal, size or prime them. A food dehydrator below 180 °F or a warm air rack does the job. Water trapped in a pocket or flash hole can kill a primer or spoil a powder charge (see Chapter 18).
