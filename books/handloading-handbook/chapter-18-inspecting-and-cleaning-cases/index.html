Chapter 18. Inspecting and Cleaning Cases

On this page (9 sections)

Key points

  • Keep each brass batch apart from the day you buy it. Lot and firing count cannot be sorted back out of a mixed pile.
  • Look at every head before its primer comes out. Then deprime every case on a separate decapping die.
  • A coarse clean comes before inspection. A final clean after sizing and trimming takes off the lube and the chips.
  • An inspection under bright light, after the coarse clean, weeds out bad cases before any work is spent on them. A split, a crease, a loose primer pocket or a sign of head separation ends a case’s life.
  • Head separation starts as a thin band inside the case just above the head. A simple wire probe finds it.

Why it matters

A case is a pressure vessel. For an instant it seals the chamber at full pressure. A split lets hot gas escape. A case that separates near the head can send gas back into the action. Inspection finds both faults before you load the case.

Keeping batches apart keeps each one uniform. Cases from different makers differ in capacity, so mixed headstamps mean mixed pressures (see Chapter 11). Lot and firing counts give each case a history you can trace. They also tell you when a batch is near the end of its life.

Cleaning protects the tools that come next. Grit scratches dies and chambers. Dirt hides cracks. Clean cases also measure true. A final clean after sizing and trimming takes off the lube before it can reach the powder.

How it is done

Keep batches apart

  1. Keep brass sorted from the day you buy it. Sort it first by headstamp, then by lot, then by firing count. Read the maker and the cartridge on each head. Give each headstamp its own container. A new headstamp is a component change, so work the load up again (see Chapter 28).
  1. Name each batch. A brass batch is one headstamp, one lot and one firing count (see Chapter 11 for lots). If you do not know the lot, treat brass bought together as one lot. Give each batch a brass batch name, such as D-3, and write it on the box and in your log. It is not the batch ID of a loading (see Chapter 27). A marker dot on the head for each firing is one way to keep count.
  1. Sort a mixed pile by headstamp only. Once cases mix, their lot and firing count cannot be sorted back out. Brass picked up at a range is one such pile. Sort it by headstamp and treat each headstamp as its own batch. Its history is unknown, so treat it as fired more than once and probe every case (step 16).

Check the heads, then deprime

Look at each head before its primer comes out. Gas-leak and pressure signs show best while the spent primer is in and the soot is still on.

  1. Look for gas leaks. A dark smudge around the spent primer means gas leaked past it. That points to a loose pocket or to high pressure. A loose pocket also shows when a new primer seats with almost no force (see Chapter 22). Discard any case with a loose pocket.

A pressure sign on a case fired with your own load means that charge is too hot for this rifle. Do not load that charge again. Set your maximum below it and note the sign in the work-up record (see Chapters 27 and 28). In a semi-automatic, judge ejector marks against those at the start load (see Chapter 26).

  1. Set aside Berdan and steel cases. Shine a light into each mouth. A case with two small offset flash holes is Berdan primed. These are best discarded, since a standard decapping pin breaks on them.1 Set Berdan and steel cases aside before they reach the decapping pin.
  1. Deprime every case. Use a universal decapping die (see Chapter 5). The spent primers drop into the catch tray. Spent primers carry lead residue. Empty the tray and wash your hands (see Chapter 2). The decapping die does no sizing, so dirty brass does it no harm. The costly sizing die then sees only clean brass.

The coarse clean

The first clean strips grit and heavy grime before you inspect the brass. It protects your dies and exposes defects (see Chapter 6 for cleaning tools). Shine is cosmetic.

  1. Clean off the grit and heavy grime. Run your cleaner as its maker directs, long enough to take off the grit. The brass does not need to shine. Work with ventilation. Spent primer residue contains lead, so wash your hands after handling media and dirty cases (see Chapter 2). Avoid cleaners that contain ammonia, which can make brass crack.2
  1. Separate the media and check every case. Wear a fitted respirator with N100 or P100 filters whenever you empty dry tumbling media.3 Look into each case and through each flash hole. Remove any media or pin you find.
  1. Dry the cases fully. Stand them mouth down in warm air. Water left in a case can wet the powder, and moisture in powder changes velocity (see Chapter 13). Look into a few cases against a light. No droplets should show inside or in the flash hole.

Inspect

One inspection now weeds out every bad case before you spend work on it. Treat range brass as brass of unknown history, fired more than once (step 3).

  1. Look at every case under bright light. Turn it one full turn. Check the mouth, the neck, the shoulder, the body and the head.
  1. Confirm the primer type and look for crimps. Shine a light into each mouth. One central flash hole means a Boxer case. Two small offset holes mean a Berdan case. Discard any Berdan case that slipped through step 5, and set aside any steel case. A swaged ring or stab marks on the head around the primer pocket mean a crimp. Keep that batch apart. The crimp comes out before priming (see Chapter 22).
  1. Discard any case with a split. Splits often start at the mouth and run down the neck (see Figure 18-1). They can also open in the shoulder or the body.
  1. Sort the dents. Sizing straightens a small dent at the mouth. A shallow dimple on the shoulder usually comes from excess lube and is harmless unless the shoulder or body has collapsed.4 Discard any case with a sharp crease or fold in the brass (see Figure 18-2).
  1. Check the head. Look for cracks and a damaged rim.

Inspect new brass too, and prepare it before its first loading (see Chapter 20).

Check for incipient head separation

A case that has room to move forward in the chamber stretches each time it fires. The stretch happens in a band just above the solid head. Brass flows out of that band and the wall thins. In time the case can break in two there. Excess shoulder bump can cause it (see Chapter 19).5 Check every batch that has been fired more than once.

Safety · See Chapter 2

A case that has started to separate can fail under pressure and release gas into the action. Distinct lines above the case web are a reason to discard brass.6

  1. Look for a bright ring. A thin, bright line around the body just above the head is the outside sign (see Figure 18-3).
  1. Feel inside every case with a probe (see Chapter 9 for how to make one). Slide the probe to the bottom. Press the tip lightly against the wall and draw it up slowly. A groove that catches the tip means the wall has thinned (see Figure 18-4). Turn the case and check again.
  1. Section a sample. Every few firings, cut one case from the batch in half lengthwise. The thin band shows plainly on the inside wall (see Figure 18-4).
  1. Act on what you find. Discard every case with a ring or a groove. Crush the neck of each discarded case so it cannot return to a batch. Record every discard in your log with its reason, such as a split neck, a loose pocket or a head ring. Then find the cause in your sizing (see Chapter 19).

The final clean

After sizing, trimming and any pocket work, the batch comes back for a final clean (see Chapters 19, 20 and 22). It takes off the lube, the brass chips and the last residue.

  1. Clean the batch again. Clean the cases as in step 7. Separate the media and look through every flash hole for media, pins and chips (step 8). Dry the cases fully (step 9).
  1. Inspect again. Sizing and trimming work the brass, so a crack can open or show only now (see Figure 18-5). Repeat steps 10 to 15 on any case that looks doubtful.

Then chamfer and deburr the mouths (see Chapter 20) and prime the cases (see Chapter 22).
