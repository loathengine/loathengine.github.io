Chapter 1. Why Handload, and Why Measure

On this page (9 sections)

Key points

  • People handload for precision, for supply, for a specific fit to one rifle and for understanding.
  • A 3-shot group is a tiny sample. It can make a poor load look good and a good load look poor.
  • The Law of Large Numbers: an average settles only as the number of shots grows.
  • Evidence means enough recorded impacts from one rifle, measured by one method, and an honest statement of the uncertainty.
  • The workflow runs from the firearm to impacts on the target. Its steps are equipment and components, research, assembly, work-up, testing and records, and adjustment. It loops back to research when the recipe changes.

Why it matters

People handload for good reasons.

  • Precision. You choose the bullet, the powder and the seating depth. You assemble every round to a written recipe and measure the result to reduce spread and uncertainty.
  • Supply. Factory ammunition for a cartridge can be scarce or missing. Components often remain. You can also load combinations that no factory sells.
  • Fit to one rifle. You size brass to your own chamber and seat bullets to your own throat. You choose bullets that your barrel’s twist will stabilize.
  • Understanding. Each step teaches you what one variable does. You stop guessing why a rifle shoots the way it does.

Some people start handloading to save money. Loading in bulk with budget components can do that. Precision handloading is not likely to. Its reward is the result.

All four reasons share one weak spot. Handloading gives you many choices, and every choice invites a test. Many range tests are too small to answer the question they ask. A handloader changes one thing, fires 3 shots and sees a smaller group. The change seems to work. It may have done nothing. The group may have been small by chance.

Telling the difference takes measurement. Real-world analysis is neither cheap nor easy. Many mid-priced modern rifles already shoot very well with factory ammunition. Improving on that takes time, discipline and often a lot of money. There are no shortcuts, gadgets, lucky charms or rituals that allow you to skip the process. Treat measurement as an essential skill.

From three shots to evidence

My father had me zero my own rifle one afternoon. I remember that day fondly. We used a simple method that nobody questioned. Fire 3 shots. Adjust the scope. Repeat until the shots were “dead nuts on.” Maybe throw out a few “flyers” I must have pulled. We left the range with a little less than half a box of .30-30 Winchester ammunition still in its yellow cardboard box. It was a rite of passage, and it “just worked.”

For years I carried that tradition forward. I relied on 3-shot and 5-shot groups to tell me everything I thought I needed to know about a rifle. The process was cheap, it was simple and it “just worked.” It was the way it had always been done.

Time passed. The targets stretched farther out and the rifles changed. The old ways began to show their cracks. But the process still “just worked.” Didn’t it?

Then the internet arrived. It brought a flood of forum posts, competing theories and endless arguments. Everyone had a system. Many claimed theirs was the only system that “just worked.”

I met a competition shooter at the range. I was intrigued. I always knew those shooters existed (F-Class, benchrest and the like). I just thought they were good ol’ boys with more money than sense, playing with shiny rifles and reloading to save money. It turned out they were much more serious than I could have imagined. They were the real deal. They all had a system too, and it “just worked.”

That caused the first reckoning in me. Being told a process “just worked” was no longer good enough. I wanted to know exactly how well it worked. I wanted to know how well one process works compared to another. I wanted to know how to change my process so it works better. And I wanted to know whether a change actually made an improvement.

Then I had a second reckoning. I made a pan of cheap weeknight lasagna. I took a square from the corner. It was as close to perfect as any I have ever made. It was browned on top, with crispy cheese on the sides. It was not too sloppy and not too dry. It was just what I wanted. Then I got a second cut from near the center. It was still cold. The cheese was not even melted. How could this be? The answer was obvious. I had no right to call my lasagna perfect on the strength of one square cut from my favorite corner. A 3-shot group is that corner square.

Those early range days are a great memory. They are one of my favorites. But what worked for an old .30-30 with ammunition from the local hardware store was not working for me anymore. As the saying goes, “In God we trust; all others must bring data.”1 So let’s go get some data.

How it is done

Anecdote versus evidence

An anecdote is an observation offered as proof. “This load shoots” after only one small group is an anecdote. Anecdotes are not lies. They are claims resting on samples too small to back them up.

Evidence has four marks: enough shots to answer the question asked, every shot recorded (including the ones you dislike), the same method of measurement for every load, and an honest statement of the uncertainty that remains.

Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. “My rifle groups well at 100 yards” is a modest claim that only needs a modest sample of shots. “This 0.1 gr change halved my group size” is an extraordinary claim. Large tests rarely find effects that big from changes that small (see Chapter 29). Before you believe it, you need enough shots to rule out everything else, including luck (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).

The Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers says that the average of a sample moves toward the true average as the sample grows. Short runs are noisy and obscure the truth. Long runs settle toward the truth. Three examples from outside shooting show how strong the effect is.

The casino. A gambler bets on black at a roulette table and wins three times in a row. In that sample of three, black has won every time. Should the gambler assume he has found a winning method? The casino is not worried. An American wheel has 38 pockets, and 18 of them are black. Over the long run, black wins 47.4 percent of spins. Over 10,000 spins, the share of black settles close to that figure. The house keeps its edge of about 5.26 percent on every even-money bet.

The shooting parallel. A 3-shot group is the gambler’s three wins. It feels like a winning method. It may be a lucky cluster of noise.

The coin. Flip a fair coin 10 times. Eight heads and two tails is an ordinary result. A split of 8 to 2 or worse, in either direction, happens about 11 times in 100. Stop there, and your data says the coin lands heads 80 percent of the time. Flip the same coin 10,000 times. The share of heads will land between 49 and 51 percent about 9,500 times in 10,000.

The shooting parallel. A rifle shoots one tiny group, with 2 shots outside it written off as “flyers.” One group, and especially one group with the “flyers” removed, cannot separate a good rifle from good luck. Only more shots and honest shots can prove a rifle.

The medical trial. A drug company gives a new heart medicine to five patients. Each patient gets a different dose. Patients 3 and 4 improve. Has the company found the perfect dose? No regulator would approve the drug on that evidence. Five patients cannot separate the drug’s effect from chance. Regulators want large trials to wash out that noise. A late-stage trial typically enrolls 300 to 3,000 patients.2

The shooting parallel. A ladder test fires one shot at each charge. Two neighboring charges that land close together are patients 3 and 4 (see Chapter 29). Two bad results do not condemn a load, and two good results do not prove one.

The problem with small groups

Small groups mislead in three major ways.

  1. High volatility (“They swing widely”). Two small groups fired from the same rifle and ammunition often look completely different by chance alone. Fire two 5-shot groups from the same load, and one will be at least 1.5 times the size of the other about 30 times in 100. With 3-shot groups, that happens 46 times in 100.
  2. Selection bias (“The best of several is flattering”). Picking the best group and ignoring the rest gives a false picture of precision. Across seven 5-shot groups from the same load, the smallest group averages 0.66 times the load’s true 5-shot average. The largest averages 1.38 times that average. The smallest group is usually just the luckiest of the seven.
  3. Statistical center bias (“They read small”). You measure a group from the center of the shots on the target, not the true center the load would show over many shots. Because that center is pulled toward the shots you just fired, the group reads smaller than the load’s true spread. Mean radius (the average distance of shots from the center) reads low by:
    • about 18 percent for a 3-shot group
    • about 11 percent for a 5-shot group
    • about 5 percent for a 10-shot group

The fewer shots in the group, the more it understates your true spread (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).

All three sets of figures are calculations that assume normally distributed shots.

It is not just groups. A velocity standard deviation (SD) from a 5-shot string swings from one string to the next even more widely than a 5-shot group does (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).

A larger sample fixes all three problems. It also tells you when two loads really differ.

Small samples are not useless. A few shots can reject a gross fault, such as a loose scope (see Chapter 3). They cannot prove a small gain.

The price of certainty

The shots you need depend on the size of the difference you want to detect, not on whether you want to prove a load good or reject it as poor. A gross fault shows in a few shots. A small gain or loss takes dozens. The usual statistical standards allow a false alarm 1 time in 20 and ask for an 80 percent chance of catching a real difference. By those standards, you need about 35 shots per load (seven 5-shot groups) to tell apart two loads whose true group sizes differ by 1.5 to 1. A 1.25 to 1 difference takes about 110 shots per load (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).

Uncertainty does not shrink in a straight line. It shrinks with the square root of the shot count. To cut your uncertainty in half, you must fire four times as many shots (for example, 40 shots instead of 10). Going from 3 shots to 30 cuts your uncertainty to less than a third. Going from 30 shots to 60 cuts it by only about 30 percent more, so the extra time and ammunition buy a much smaller gain.

The workflow on one page

The work follows a continuous path that loops from the rifle to the final application:

  1. Firearm. Record the rifle specifications and lock its hardware.
  2. Equipment and components. Learn what each tool and each component does and how to choose the right ones for your goals.
  3. Research. Read published data, check bullet stability, and plan the load on paper.
  4. Assembly. Assemble the rounds carefully according to your written recipe.
  5. Work-up. Safely climb from the start load in small steps, chronographing every shot. Stop at the published maximum, at your velocity ceiling or at any pressure sign.
  6. Testing and records. Keep meticulous records of every load and every shot (Chapter 27), confirm safe function and velocity, and run a proper statistical evaluation (The Precision Rifle Handbook).
  7. Adjustment. Compare loads and diagnose any issues (Chapter 31). Adjust the recipe or try a new approach, and loop back through research, work-up and testing if necessary.
  8. Shoot for record. Take your verified load to the field. Build its drop chart, find its hit probability and use it in a match or on a hunt (The Precision Rifle Handbook).

Steps 1 through 5, along with record keeping, safe work-ups, and workshop troubleshooting, take you from raw components to safe, functional ammunition that meets your expectations. Extended statistical evaluation, external ballistics, drop charts, and hit probability are covered in The Precision Rifle Handbook.

Even a load that works comes back round. Barrels wear, seasons change and powder lots run out (see Chapter 30).
