Chapter 2. Safety in the Workshop, at the Range and in Storage

Key points

  • A loaded round is a small pressure vessel, and you are the person closest to it.
  • The worst mistakes are often the simplest ones, and each rule prevents at least one of them.
  • Safety comes from preventing errors. Pressure signs are poor warnings.
  • Published data holds only for the exact components it was tested with.
  • Storage and lead carry slower risks. Confined powder can burst its container, and lead enters the body by mouth and breath.

Why it matters

A rifle chamber holds tens of thousands of psi on every shot. The SAAMI limit for the .308 Winchester, for example, is 62,000 psi (see Chapter 4).1

The simple mistakes are the wrong powder, a double charge, an empty case, a bullet seated too deep or a charge pushed past the maximum. Every rule below prevents at least one of them. These rules stop mistakes at the workbench instead of trying to catch them after the shot.2 Pressure signs are poor warnings (see Chapter 28).3

How it is done

Set up the workshop

Set up in a space that is free of distractions, well lit and dry. Keep carpet out of the loading area. It traps combustible dust and builds static charges that can fire a primer. Clear the workbench before you begin. Keep only one powder and one primer type on it, with the published data for your exact components. Keep open flame and tobacco away from the workbench, the powder and the primers. Wear safety glasses whenever you load. A primer can go off as you seat it, and your eyes are right above it.

Load only when you can give the job your full attention. Do not load when tired, ill, hurried or after drinking. Those are the times a step gets skipped or doubled. Develop a fixed routine and follow it every time. A consistent routine makes a skipped or doubled step immediately stand out.

Every loading session

  1. Verify the scale with a check weight before the first charge (see Chapter 23). Record any anomalies.
  2. Read the powder label and compare it with the recipe. Confirm the lot number.
  3. Check that the measure is completely empty from the last session. Pour only the powder you need for the session.
  4. Charge the cases in a logical order in a loading block, such as right to left and top to bottom. Weigh every charge for a work-up or a precision load. If you throw charges from a powder measure, check-weigh the throws often.
  5. Look into every charged case under good light before you seat any bullet. The powder levels should match. Dump and recharge any case that looks higher or lower than its neighbors. Take extra care with reduced loads or any charge that fills less than half the case. Those cases can hold a double charge without overflowing. A load that fills 95 to 105 percent of the case cannot be double-charged without spilling (see Chapter 13).
  6. Seat the bullets. Measure each round’s CBTO and cartridge overall length against the recipe (see Chapter 24).
  7. At the end of the session, pour unused powder back into its original container. Powder left out draws moisture from the air, which can change the pressure it produces. Unmarked powder can be mistaken for another.
  8. Empty the hopper completely. Kernels left in the measure can drop into the first cases of your next session and hide under the new powder.
  9. Put unused primers back in their original packaging.
  10. Label the box with the load and the date (see Chapter 25).

Loads and component changes

Always begin at the published start load for your exact components, never at the maximum. The start load leaves room for the ways your rifle differs from the test barrel. If a source lists only a maximum, start at least 10 percent below it. Never load below a published start charge.4 Too light a charge can ignite erratically or stick a bullet in the bore. Do not exceed a published maximum charge. The maximum was safe in the source’s test barrel with the source’s components. Your rifle may reach the same pressure with less powder (see Chapter 28).

Stop the work-up if you reach your velocity ceiling before the last step. Your velocity ceiling is the published velocity at the maximum charge. Lower it for a shorter barrel, and never raise it for a longer one, because the gain from extra barrel length is only an estimate (see Chapter 16). The ceiling is a pressure limit, not a goal (see Chapter 28).

Predicted data never replaces published data. Never change a model’s powder settings to match your chronograph. That shifts its predicted pressure too.5

Work up again from the start load after any change of primer, case maker, bullet or powder. Do the same with a new barrel or a changed barrel length. A different component can change peak pressure substantially. A new lot of the same component usually shifts it only a little. Record the new lot number. Load 10 check rounds at your current charge, or one step lower if it sits at or near the published maximum. Compare their velocity with your records before you load in bulk. If the new lot runs faster by more than twice the noise (see Chapter 30), lower the charge and work up again from below. Do not substitute a powder because its name or its place on a burn chart looks similar. A burn chart is only a rough ranking, and two powders can swap places from one cartridge to another (see Chapter 13).

If you seat a bullet deeper than the published COAL, deeper than your tested recipe, or into the lands, work up again from the start load. Deeper seating shrinks the powder space and raises pressure (see Chapter 4). Seating into the lands raises it too (see Chapter 24). Never enlarge a flash hole past its specified diameter. A larger hole changes the primer flame and can spike pressure.

Cases and rounds

  • Discard any case with an incipient head separation ring, a split or an enlarged, loose primer pocket (see Chapter 18). A case that fails on firing releases gas into the action, toward your face.
  • Trim any case longer than the maximum case length before you load it. An overlong case can jam its mouth into the end of the chamber neck. The mouth then pinches the bullet and pressure rises (see Chapter 20).
  • Anneal only empty cases. Heat can fire a live primer or light any powder left in the case. Keep water away from molten salt. Water that reaches the pot turns to steam at once and throws molten salt (see Chapter 21).
  • Seat every primer fully, below flush. A high primer can make the action hard to close or misfire, and in a semi-automatic it can fire as the bolt closes (see Chapters 22 and 26).
  • Do not force a primer. A primer that resists has encountered a crimp, a tilted start or carbon in the pocket (see Chapter 22). Crushing it can fire it.
  • Do not force a live round into or out of the chamber. A round that resists does not fit, and forcing the bolt can leave a live round stuck with the bolt not locked. Reject the round instead (see Chapter 25).
  • Monolithic copper bullets need their own published data and more jump than lead-core bullets. A monolithic bullet is longer than a lead-core bullet of the same weight. It sits deeper in the case and carries more bearing surface, and both raise pressure (see Chapters 14 and 24).
  • In a semi-automatic, use gas-gun data where the maker publishes it. Gas-gun data stops at a lower pressure than bolt-action data for the same cartridge, and a bolt-action load can be unsafe in a gas gun (see Chapter 26).

To take a live round apart, pull the bullet and pour out the powder. Do not punch out a live primer. The decapping pin strikes it much as a firing pin does, and it can fire in the die. If the primer sits below flush, fire the empty primed case in the rifle at the range, then decap it. Do not chamber a case with a high or crooked primer. Closing the bolt can fire the primer before the bolt locks. Dispose of it as the primer maker directs (see Chapters 22 and 25).

At the range

Wear eye and ear protection at all times, and make sure everyone nearby does the same. Fire the steps of a work-up in order, lowest first, and chronograph every shot (see Chapter 28). Stop at once at any pressure sign. Signs include a stiff bolt lift, an ejector mark and a flattened, cratered or pierced primer. The absence of signs does not prove a load is safe. Stop as well when a shot reaches your velocity ceiling, or when a step gains far more velocity than the steps before it. After any stop, take the unfired rounds home and pull their bullets.

If a shot sounds or feels weak or abnormal, stop immediately. Unload the rifle and inspect the bore for a stuck bullet before you fire again. A squib is a shot too weak to push the bullet out of the barrel. The next shot behind that stuck bullet can cause a catastrophic failure. Take any suspect rounds home and pull them apart. Do not try to fire them.

If a round fails to fire, keep the muzzle pointed downrange for at least 30 seconds, because the round may be a hang-fire, one that fires after a delay.6 Keep your face away from the action. Then open it, remove the round and look at the primer.7 A dented primer means the round failed to fire. Take it home and pull the bullet (see Chapter 25). A primer with no dent means the firing pin did not strike it. Have the rifle checked before you fire it again. Check that the bore is clear before you load another round.8

Keep ammunition out of direct sunlight and hot vehicles. Never leave a round in a hot chamber between strings. Heat raises chamber pressure, and on a load near maximum that leaves no safety margin. At an indoor range, shoot only where the mechanical ventilation is adequate. Every shot puts lead from the primer into the air you breathe (see “Lead hygiene” below).

Lead hygiene

Lead builds up in the body over time and harms the nervous system. It can cause birth defects, reproductive harm and other serious injury.9 It enters mainly through the mouth and the lungs. Primers and bullets contain lead. Spent primers and fired cases carry a residue of it.

Wash your hands and face thoroughly with soap and water after loading, shooting or cleaning a rifle. Wash them as well after you handle fired cases, spent primers or cleaning media. Do not eat, drink or smoke in the workshop or at the range until you have washed. Keep your hands away from your nose and mouth while you handle components. Keep children entirely away from the workshop and from fired brass. A growing child absorbs lead more readily than an adult, and no safe level of lead in children is known. Children also put their hands in their mouths.10

Wear a fitted respirator with N100 or P100 filters whenever you empty dry tumbling media.11 The media traps and concentrates fine lead residue. Clean the workbench and floor with a damp cloth or mop, not a vacuum cleaner. A vacuum blows fine lead dust into the air and risks igniting spilled powder.

If you shoot or load frequently, consult a physician about whether periodic blood lead testing makes sense for your routine.

Storing powder

Store smokeless powder in its original container, which is built to split or vent at low pressure if the powder ignites. Never store powder in any other container.12 Keep the containers in a cool, dry place. Choose one away from heat sources, furnaces, water heaters, direct sunlight and electrical equipment. Keep them well separated from solvents, flammable gases and other combustibles.

House powder in a storage cabinet built of insulating material with weak seams or vents that allow gas to escape freely. Never store powder in a sealed or strong container such as a gun safe. Confined gas can burst a rigid container with explosive force.

Keep only the quantities you need. Several small containers present less hazard than a single large one. Clean up any spilled powder promptly with a brush and dustpan. Many places limit how much powder and how many primers you may store, so find out what applies where you live.

Storing primers

Keep primers in their original factory packaging until you use them. Store them cool and dry so heat does not degrade them. Never store primers loose or in bulk containers. Massed primers can detonate together, producing a blast equal to a grenade in an enclosed space.4 Handle primers only with clean, dry hands. Skin oils can degrade ignition sensitivity. Do not load a primer you cannot identify. Primers of different types look alike, and a change of primer can change peak pressure substantially (see Chapter 12). Dispose of old or unidentified primers as a primer maker’s published guidance directs.4

Old powder and disposal

Check stored powder now and then. Open the lid and smell it. An acrid, acidic smell is a sign of breakdown (see Chapter 13). Dispose of such powder immediately. Deteriorating powder releases heat and acid, which speeds up the reaction and can cause spontaneous fire.

Dispose of old or suspect powder as its container directs. If the container gives no direction, burn it in an open, isolated clearing. Spread it in shallow trails no more than an inch deep, one pound at a time. Light it with a fuse or slow-burning train that gives you time to get well clear before the flame reaches the powder.4 Empty each container completely before you dispose of it.

Do not load a powder you cannot identify. Powders that look alike can burn at very different rates, and an unknown powder has no data. Pass up inherited or second-hand containers of unknown history. Do not blend powders. A blend has no published data, and no one can say how it will burn.

You may reuse the powder from a round you pull at the workbench in the same session, such as one with a badly seated bullet. You still know what that powder is. Once a round has left the workbench, dispose of its powder when you pull it. After a night in a range bag you can no longer prove what is in it. The same goes for powder from discarded cartridges. Only this session’s powder goes back into its container: powder from the measure, the scale pan, a case you charged and dumped, or a round you pulled before it left the workbench.

Why each rule exists

Each number is a rule in Before You Begin and on the Appendix B card:

RuleWhat it prevents
1. Full attentionA skipped or doubled step
2. Safety glasses whenever you loadAn eye injury from a primer that goes off as you load
3. One powder and one primer type on the workbenchThe wrong powder or primer in a case
4. Exact published data, and only known powderA substitute or unknown powder that burns far too fast
5. Start at the start load, in small steps, never past the maximumPressure you cannot see in your rifle, and case or action failure
6. Work up again after a component or barrel change, and check a new lotA pressure jump from a new primer, case, bullet, powder or barrel
7. Discard damaged cases, and trim overlong onesA case that fails and releases gas, or a pinched bullet
8. Verify the scale every sessionCharges heavier or lighter than you think
9. Look into every charged caseA double charge, or an empty case that lodges a bullet in the bore
10. Treat seating deeper or into the lands as a new load and work up againPressure from less powder space or a jammed bullet
11. Empty the measure at the end of a sessionA hidden layer of the wrong powder
12. Eye and ear protectionInjury from a case failure, or from noise
13. Steps fired in order, a chronograph and two limitsPassing the published maximum pressure with no sign
14. Stop at any pressure signMore shots at a pressure already too high
15. Keep rounds coolHeat pushing a near-maximum load over pressure
16. Stop after a shot that sounds or feels wrong, and wait out a misfireA second shot behind a bullet stuck in the bore, or a hang-fire that fires as the action opens
17. Original containers, cool storage and lead hygieneA fire that turns into an explosion, and lead taken in by mouth or breath
18. Predictions are for learningA load set from a model instead of published data
