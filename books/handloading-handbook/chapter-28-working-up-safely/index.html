Chapter 28. Working Up Safely

Key points

  • The published maximum charge (the book maximum) was safe in the test lab’s barrel. You must find your own rifle’s limit from below, in small steps.
  • Velocity follows pressure. A chronograph is the best pressure gauge you have at home.
  • Stop at the book maximum charge or your velocity ceiling, whichever comes first. The velocity ceiling is the book velocity at the maximum charge, lowered for a shorter barrel and never raised for a longer one.
  • Pressure signs mean stop now. Their absence does not mean safe.
  • A work-up holds only for the components, seating and barrel it was done with. A change sends you back to the start load, and a new lot needs a velocity check.

Why it matters

The published maximum was safe in the source’s test barrel with the source’s components. Your rifle may reach the same pressure with less powder. Differences in bore, throat or case capacity can each do it. The work-up finds out from below, one small step at a time.

Pressure climbs faster than velocity, so the last steps before the maximum add a little speed and a lot of pressure.1

The warnings many handloaders rely on, such as flattened primers and marked case heads, often come late or not at all. You need a better gauge, and a chronograph gives you one.

How it is done

Plan the work-up before you load a round (see Chapter 16, “Plan the work-up”). Bring the plan and its two limits to the range, with the rounds boxed by step.

The stop rules

Stop at once, and go no higher, at the first of these:

  • The published maximum charge.
  • Any shot that reaches your velocity ceiling.
  • Any pressure sign (see “Pressure signs, and why they fail” below).
  • A step that gains far more velocity than the steps before it.
  • A shot that sounds or feels weak, or anything else abnormal or unexplained.
Safety · See Chapter 2

Stop at the book maximum charge or your velocity ceiling, whichever comes first. A pressure sign means stop now, and no sign does not mean safe.

At the range

  1. Set up the chronograph as its maker directs. Count its readings against your shots to check that it reads every one (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 14). If the chronograph misses a shot, do not move to a heavier step until you get a velocity reading at the current step. If you have no more rounds at that step, stop the work-up there. Because a chronograph that reads artificially low can let you pass your velocity ceiling unnoticed, use a Doppler unit when you can (see Chapter 10).
  2. Start with a cool barrel and keep an even pace between steps. Fire any foulers from factory ammunition at a separate aim mark (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapter 14). Fire the steps in order, lowest first, so that any warning comes at the lowest charge possible.
  3. Record every velocity, and what you see on each case (see Chapter 27).
  4. After each shot, check the bolt lift, the primer and the case head before you load the next round (see “Pressure signs, and why they fail” below). If a shot sounds or feels weak, unload the rifle and check the bore for a stuck bullet before you fire again (see Chapter 2). In a semi-automatic, load one round at a time, so the action cannot chamber the next round before you have looked at the case. Ejector or extractor marks that are new or heavier than at the start load are a pressure sign: stop (see Chapter 26). A flattened, cratered or pierced primer still means stop. Note how the action cycles.
  5. After each step, compare its velocity with two things: the book velocity for that charge and the gain over the step before.
  6. Stop at the first stop rule you meet. Do not fire the remaining higher steps. Take them home and pull the bullets.

Your first step may already be faster than the book’s start velocity, even after the barrel adjustment. If so, expect to meet the velocity ceiling before the maximum charge. If the start load itself reaches the ceiling, stop there. Never load below the start charge. Change a component and work up from its own published start load (see Chapter 2).

Steps of the same size should each add roughly the same velocity (see Figure 28-1). When the step size changes, compare the gain per grain instead. Say each step has added about 40 fps, and the next one adds 90 fps. That step gained far more than the steps before it, so stop there. Example numbers. Not load data.

Velocity: the best pressure gauge you own

Velocity comes from pressure pushing the bullet down the barrel. To leave a barrel of the same length faster, with the same powder and bullet, the bullet needs more push. So velocity tracks pressure.

Evidence · Supported by data

The velocity rule: if you exceed the book’s velocity, you have exceeded the book’s pressure. Matching it does not prove you are safe. In percentage terms, pressure moves about 3 times as much as velocity for a change of charge, and 10 times or more for a primer swap.1 Velocity is the best pressure indicator a handloader has at home.2

The rule holds only for a barrel like the test barrel. That is why you adjust the book velocity for a shorter barrel before you start.

Even in a barrel like the test barrel, velocity is an indirect indicator of pressure. A chronograph reads the bullet, not the chamber. Your velocity ceiling is only an estimate of the velocity your rifle gives at the book’s maximum pressure. It is more reliable than a sticky bolt or a cratered primer (see “Pressure signs, and why they fail” below), but it is still an estimate. The velocity ceiling is a safety limit, not a target to reach. Pick your working charge to meet the velocity goal you set in advance, well below the ceiling (see “Leave a margin” below).

Pressure signs, and why they fail

Pressure signs are the marks and feelings that some loads leave behind.

  • A stiff or sticky bolt lift.
  • An ejector mark: a bright spot on the case head where brass flowed into the ejector hole in the bolt face.
  • A cratered primer, with metal pushed back around the firing-pin dent.
  • A pierced or blown primer, with a hole through the cup or the primer pushed out of its pocket.
  • A flattened primer, its rounded edge pressed square into the corner of the pocket.
  • A black ring of soot around the primer, where gas leaked past it.3
  • A primer pocket that seats the next primer loosely after one firing.4
  • Case-head expansion: growth in the diameter of the case head.

Once a load shows any of these signs (see Figure 28-2), do not fire it or any heavier step again. Set your maximum below the step that showed it.

Nearly every manual teaches these signs. The evidence shows they are poor early warnings. Here is the case, from claim to verdict.

Contested primers and case heads give early warning

The claim. Primers and case heads warn you before pressure gets dangerous.

The physics. These marks depend on more than pressure. Excess headspace, for one, flattens primers at low pressure. A pocket worn by many firings can leak gas around the primer without high pressure.5

The test. A comparison set case-head expansion against pressures measured by instrument.6

The data. Case-head expansion usually points only to a pressure somewhere between 45,000 and 70,000 psi. It needs 50 to 100 measurements to mean anything.6 Hatcher called primer reading “notoriously unreliable”.7 In a laboratory test with handgun cases, a load at almost twice the pressure limit left a fired case that looked normal. A chronograph would have revealed it.8 A stiff bolt lift, a flattened primer, a cratered primer and an ejector mark all turn up in factory ammunition.9

The verdict. Folklore. The signs fail as an early warning. A sign means stop now. No sign proves nothing about safety.

Leave a margin

The charge where you stopped is not the charge to load. Back off from it.

Evidence · Supported by data

Loading 5 percent below maximum gives about 10 percent less pressure for about 3 percent less velocity.10

At 2,800 fps, 3 percent is 84 fps. Giving up 84 fps buys about a tenth off the peak pressure. Example numbers. Not load data.

Heat is the other reason for a margin. A load near the line on a mild day has nothing left for the hottest day your ammunition will see. Every powder responds to heat differently, and “temperature stable” describes a range of behavior, not zero change (see Chapter 13). Leave a heat margin of at least 1 fps for every degree Fahrenheit between your ammunition’s temperature during the work-up and the hottest temperature it will ever face. Account for every warm spot the rounds may encounter: a vehicle in the sun, a pocket, a box on a sunny range or a warm chamber. Keep your working charge’s velocity at least that far below your velocity ceiling. If your own records show a steeper temperature slope, use that larger number, never a smaller one (see Chapter 30).

Worked example. The work-up was fired with the ammunition at 60 °F, and the hottest it will see is 100 °F. At 1 fps per °F, the working charge must run at least 40 fps below the velocity ceiling. Example numbers. Not load data.

Choose the working charge from the velocity goal you set (see Chapter 16), not from the top of the work-up. Take the lowest step that meets all three of these conditions:

  • It must meet your goal.
  • It must sit at least one full step below the step where you stopped.
  • Its velocity must sit below your velocity ceiling by at least the room for heat.

One full step is the least margin, and the 5 percent figures above show what a larger margin buys (see Figure 28-3). If no step meets all three, lower the goal or change components. Do not trade the margin away. Then test that one charge for precision, with enough shots to mean something (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, Chapters 14 to 16).

Work up again after any change

A work-up belongs to one set of components. Change a component and the old stopping point no longer applies.

  • Primer. A primer change can raise pressure far more than velocity, so the chronograph may not show it (see Chapter 12).1
  • Case maker. A couple of grains of water less capacity raises pressure near the maximum (see Chapter 11).12
  • Bullet. Two bullets of the same weight can differ in length, bearing surface and jacket, and so in peak pressure (see Chapter 14). Monolithic copper bullets build pressure differently and need their own data.13
  • Seating (CBTO). Seating deeper than tested leaves less room for the powder and raises peak pressure. Seating into the lands raises it too (see Chapter 24).
  • Powder. A different powder is a new load, even when the name sounds similar (see Chapter 13).
  • Barrel. A new barrel, or a changed barrel length, starts over (see Chapter 30).

Drop back to the published start load and work up again. It costs a few rounds. It is the only way to see the new combination from below.

A new lot of the same component is not a new component. Modern lots usually differ only a little, but record the lot and check it rather than assume. Load 10 check rounds at your current charge, or one step lower if it sits at or near the published maximum. Fire them interleaved with old-lot rounds at the same charge if you have any, or compare their velocity with your records at that charge (see Chapter 30). If the new lot runs faster by more than twice the noise (see Chapter 30), lower the charge and work up again from below. Stop at the book maximum charge or your velocity ceiling, whichever comes first, as in any work-up. Load in bulk only after the check.

Safety · See Chapter 2

Work up again from the published start load after any change of primer, case maker, bullet, powder or barrel. Do the same after seating deeper than the worked-up seating or into the lands. Velocity can hide a primer change.1
