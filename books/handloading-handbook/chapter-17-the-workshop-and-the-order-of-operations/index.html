Chapter 17. The Workshop and the Order of Operations

On this page (7 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • The order of operations
  • Where to measure
  • Workshop workflow and station discipline
  • Record keeping from day one
  • Notes

Key points

  • The workshop steps follow a fixed order, and each one sets up the next.
  • Each step sits where it cannot spoil earlier work. Depriming comes first. Cleaning and annealing come before sizing, and sizing comes before trimming. A final clean follows the trim, and the chamfer is the last cut before priming.
  • A step that changes a dimension is a step to measure. A reading that is not written down is lost.
  • When a whole batch goes through each step together, every case in it sees the same setup.
  • A log kept from the first case lets you trace an odd group or a stiff bolt back to its cause.

Why it matters

Every workshop step changes the case, and some steps undo others. Sizing pushes brass forward and makes the case longer. A case trimmed before sizing can come out of the die too long. Annealing changes how far brass springs back, so a shoulder set before annealing may move. Lube left on a case can reach the powder.

The order of the steps matters, and it is easy to get wrong. The table below gives the whole order, with the reason for each position.

How it is done

The order of operations

1Keep batches apart. Sort mixed brass by headstamp.18Lot and firing count cannot be sorted back out of a mixed pile.

3Coarse clean and dry18Grit scratches dies and chambers. Dirt hides cracks.
4Inspect18Bad cases leave before you spend work on them.
5Anneal (optional)21Heat changes springback, so anneal before you set any dimension.
6Lubricate19A dry case can stick in the sizing die.
7Size and bump the shoulder19Sizing pushes brass forward. Length is not final yet.
8Expand the neck on a mandrel (optional)19The mandrel moves brass too.
9Measure length and trim20Only now is the length final.
10Remove any pocket crimp. Clean or uniform primer pockets, or deburr flash holes (optional).22The final clean washes out the chips.
11Final clean and dry18Lube can reach the powder. On a loaded round it can raise the thrust on the bolt.1 The clean also takes off the chips and the last residue.
12Chamfer and deburr. Flare the mouth for flat-base bullets (optional).20The cutter leaves a burr. This is the last cut, just before the primer goes in. A flat base needs a lead-in. Tap the chips out.
13Prime22A charged case would spill powder through the flash hole.
14Charge23The seating die is already set on a dummy round (see Chapter 24), so no charged case waits while you set it up. Look into every case before a bullet covers it.
15Seat. Crimp if the load calls for it, and taper crimp a flared mouth.24Seating closes the case and sets the CBTO.
16Inspect and gauge25A last look and a fit check.
17Label, record and store25Write the record while the facts are fresh.

New brass starts at step 1. It has no spent primers and no grime, so it skips steps 2 and 3. It still gets the inspection in step 4, then goes on from step 6 (see Chapter 20).

Eight rules set this order: deprime first, clean and anneal before you size, size before you trim, run the final clean after trimming, chamfer just before you prime, prime before you charge, and charge right before you seat. Decide which optional steps you will use and keep to them, so every batch shares the same history.

Workshop workflow and station discipline

A few habits keep the order working from batch to batch.

  • One batch, one step. Take the whole batch through each step before you set up for the next. Size every case in the batch before you prime any of it. Check the shoulder bump on a sample before you trim. Every case in the batch then sees the same setup.
  • One powder, one primer. Keep only one powder and one primer type on the workbench at a time (see Chapter 2). Before you bring out a different powder or primer, put the first powder back in its original container and the primers back in their factory packaging.
  • Die setup. Each time you thread a sizing or seating die into the press, check its setting before you run the batch. Check a sizing die by comparing a sized case with your fired cases on a base-to-shoulder comparator (see Chapters 9 and 19). Set a seating die on a dummy round, checked with a bullet comparator, before you charge any case. Before you prime, set aside one sized case for the seating dummy. Leave it unprimed and mark it (see Chapter 24).
  • Progressive press. It demands strict discipline (see Chapter 5 for the press). Use it only for loads you have already worked up. Weigh every work-up charge (see Chapters 16 and 23).
    • It throws charges by volume, so check them on a scale at intervals.
    • Size and trim the cases in a separate prep pass, so no case is trimmed before its final sizing. Leave the sizing die off the press, so the press does not size the trimmed cases again.
    • Pull each stroke smoothly all the way down, and pause at the bottom before you return the handle.
    • Look into every case after the powder station and before its bullet is seated. A powder-check station adds to that look and never replaces it.
    • If you feel any hitch, such as a tipped primer or a case that enters hard, stop at once. Clear the shell plate, or check every station one by one, before you cycle the press again. Never guess the state of a round left in the press after a stop.

Record keeping from day one

A workshop log turns a box of ammunition into evidence. Without one, an odd group or a stiff bolt cannot be traced to its cause. Give each batch a page in the log and an ID, and write the ID on the box. Fill in the page as you work (see Figure 17-2). Note each lot as you open its container, each step with its date and each reading as you take it. Keep every raw reading. An average can be worked out later, but a lost reading is gone. The page is the batch record, one link in a chain of IDs that ties each batch to its load, its rifle and every shot fired from it (see Chapter 27).
