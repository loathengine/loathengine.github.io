Chapter 15. Reading Published Load Data

Key points

  • Published data for your exact bullet and powder sets the range you load within, from the start load up to the maximum.
  • Each row was measured in one test barrel with one primer, one case and one COAL. Your rifle will differ.
  • Pressures in psi and in CUP come from different instruments. They cannot be converted into each other.
  • Sources disagree because their rifles, components and methods differ. That is normal.
  • A charge printed without a start load is a ceiling only. Your start sits well below it.

Why it matters

Published load data comes from powder and bullet makers. Each load was fired in a test barrel, and only charges that stayed within the cartridge’s pressure limit were printed. Many sources also print the pressure they measured. For most handloaders, these tables are the only link to real pressure measurement. Everything else in load development rests on them.

A row in a load table is the record of one test. It is not a promise about your rifle. Your chamber, bore, brass and primer can push pressure higher or lower than the test barrel did. A careful reading tells you three things: where to start, where to stop and how far your setup is from the one that was tested.

Misreading a load table is how handloaders get hurt, and the mistakes are simple ones: reading the row for a powder with a similar name, using one bullet’s data for another bullet of the same weight, or treating a maximum-only charge as a start load. Here is how to avoid each mistake.

How it is done

Start and maximum loads

Most tables give two charges for each powder.

  • The start load is the charge the source tells you to begin with. It sits well below the limit, so it leaves room for a rifle that runs hotter than the test barrel.
  • The maximum load is the heaviest charge the source fired that stayed within the limit in its test barrel. It is a ceiling, not a target. Never exceed it.

Each charge comes with the velocity the source measured. That velocity belongs to the test barrel, and yours will differ. Some manuals print several steps between start and maximum.

Safety · See Chapter 2

Start at the published start load for your exact components and work up from there (see Chapter 28).

The test conditions

Every source records how its data was taken. These are the conditions that matter.

Test barrel. A shorter barrel gives less velocity (see Chapter 3). Even two barrels of the same length often differ in velocity, because their bores, throats and finishes differ.1

Primer. A different primer makes a different load.

Case. Water capacity varies from maker to maker (see Chapter 11).

COAL. Seating deeper than tested raises pressure (see Chapter 24).

Bullet. Two bullets of the same weight can differ in length, bearing surface and construction (see Chapter 14).

Each difference can change pressure, and some can change it a great deal (see Chapter 28).

Pressure: psi and CUP

Published pressures come in psi or in CUP (copper units of pressure). The two scales come from different measurement systems and cannot be converted into each other (see Chapter 4). Compare a CUP figure only with other CUP figures, and a psi figure only with psi.

Published load tables use both units. A CUP figure can sit above or below a cartridge’s psi limit, because the two numbers are on different scales. Where a row lists only one pressure, it applies to the maximum charge. Some sources also list pressure for the start load. A dash means the source did not report pressure, not that the pressure was low.

Why sources disagree

Open two sources for the same bullet and powder and their numbers will rarely match. The reasons are ordinary.

  • Different test barrels: length, bore, throat and chamber.
  • Different primers and cases, so different ignition and different capacity.
  • Different COAL for the same bullet.
  • Different powder lots. Burn rate varies from lot to lot.
  • Different test temperatures, pressure systems and limits. One European powder maker tests at 68 °F and holds its loads to the lower of the C.I.P. and SAAMI limits.2

Disagreement is usually not an error in either source, though misprints do happen. That is one more reason to compare sources (see below). Mostly, the spread shows how far a load can move between rifles. So you work up in your own rifle instead of loading someone else’s maximum.

Choose the source whose test components match yours most closely. If two sources match equally well, take the lower start load and treat the lower maximum as your limit. If they disagree widely for your exact components, a pressure test may be needed (see Chapter 16, “When to pay for pressure testing”).

Maximum-only loads

Some sources publish only a maximum charge, with no start load. Work out your own start from it.

start charge = maximum charge × 0.90

Round the result down to your scale’s resolution, never up. Then work up from there (see Chapter 28).

Safety · See Chapter 2

Where only a maximum load is published, start at least 10 percent below it. Where a maker asks for a larger margin, such as 15 percent, follow the maker.3

Reading published data, step by step

  1. Open the maker’s current data, from its latest manual or its website. Makers revise their data, and each new edition replaces the one before.4
  2. Find your cartridge. Note the maximum COAL and the standard, SAAMI or C.I.P., that sets the pressure limit (see Chapter 4).
  3. Find the data for your exact bullet, not just its weight.
  4. Read the test conditions. Compare test barrel length, COAL, primer and case with yours, and write down every difference.
  5. Find the rows for your exact powder. Never use a powder with a similar name.
  6. Read the published start load. Copy the charge and its velocity.
  7. Read the published maximum load. Copy the maximum charge, its velocity and the pressure with its unit.
  8. If the source publishes only a maximum charge, work out your start load with the formula above.
  9. Write all of it on your load record, the written recipe for this load (see Chapter 27), before you open the powder.
