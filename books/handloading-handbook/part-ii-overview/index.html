Workshop Kit Lists & Prep Costs

The press, the dies and the other tools do the work of handloading. Each chapter covers one family of tools. It says what the tools do, what types there are and how to choose one.

  • Presses and dies (Chapter 5).
  • Case cleaning tools (Chapter 6).
  • Case preparation and priming tools (Chapter 7).
  • Scales and powder measures (Chapter 8).
  • Measuring tools (Chapter 9).
  • Range equipment (Chapter 10).

The procedures that use these tools come later. The workshop steps are in Chapters 17 to 26. Safe work-ups and function testing are in Part VI (Chapters 27 to 31), and extended range testing is in The Precision Rifle Handbook.

Equipping the workshop

Workshop kit lists by function

Judge a tool by what it does, not by its brand. Any tool that does the job with measured repeatability will serve.

  • Starter workshop.
    • A solid workbench (see Chapter 5).
    • A storage cabinet for powder and primers (see Chapter 2).
    • A rigid single-stage press (see Chapter 5).
    • A priming tool and a primer flip tray (see Chapters 7 and 22).
    • A primer pocket swager or piloted reamer, if you load military brass (see Chapters 7 and 22).
    • A full-length sizing die, a seating die and the shell holder the die maker lists for your cartridge (see Chapter 5).
    • A universal decapping die, used on every batch before cleaning (see Chapters 5 and 18).
    • Case lubricant, a neck brush and dry lube (see Chapters 5 and 19).
    • A case cleaning system (see Chapters 6 and 18).
    • A scale resolving to 0.1 gr, with check weights (see Chapters 8 and 23).
    • A powder measure, a trickler and a funnel (see Chapters 8 and 23).
    • A 6-inch caliper (.001 inch resolution), with base-to-shoulder and bullet (base-to-ogive) comparators (see Chapters 9, 19 and 24).
    • A case trimmer, and a chamfer and deburring tool (see Chapters 7 and 20).
    • A case gauge (see Chapters 9 and 25).
    • Loading blocks (see Chapter 5).
    • A kinetic bullet puller (see Chapter 5).
    • A workshop log (see Chapter 17).
    • A chronograph, with Doppler radar preferred for load testing (see Chapter 10 and The Precision Rifle Handbook).
    • Eye and ear protection (see Chapter 2).
    • Current published load data from the powder and bullet makers (see Chapter 15).
  • Rifle and range kit.
    • A torque driver and a paint pen for the hardware baseline (see Chapters 3 and 10).
    • A solid rest, or a bipod and a rear bag (see Chapter 10 and The Precision Rifle Handbook).
    • A spotting scope or a target camera (see Chapter 10 and The Precision Rifle Handbook).
    • Targets printed at actual size (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).
    • A pocket weather meter that reads air temperature and station pressure, and an insulated ammunition box with a thermometer in it (see Chapters 10 and 30, and The Precision Rifle Handbook).
    • A scope level (see Chapter 10 and The Precision Rifle Handbook).
  • Precision workshop additions.
    • Bushing or expander mandrel dies, for fine control of neck tension (see Chapters 5 and 19).
    • A competition micrometer seating die with a sliding alignment sleeve (see Chapters 5 and 24).
    • An analytical scale or automated dispenser resolving to 0.02 gr, once charge error is a real share of your velocity spread (see Chapters 8 and 23).
    • A concentricity gauge for runout (see Chapters 9 and 19).
    • A ball micrometer for neck wall thickness (see Chapters 9 and 19).
    • A motorized 3-in-1 trimmer that indexes on the shoulder datum (see Chapters 7 and 20).
    • An induction or flame annealer, with temperature-indicating paint (see Chapters 7 and 21).
    • A modified-case length gauge (see Chapters 9 and 24).

Which prep tasks to do

Safety and function come first. Inspect cases, probe for incipient head separation (see Chapter 18), bump the shoulder by measurement (see Chapter 19) and trim under maximum case length (see Chapter 20). Always do these.

Every other prep task is optional. None has shown a gain on target in a large-sample test, and each costs money, time or both. The table below measures cost in “rounds’ worth,” where 1 unit equals the component cost of one loaded round. A tool’s cost is the number of rounds its price would buy, and one hour of work counts as 20 rounds (see The Precision Rifle Handbook, “Where effort pays”). The total cost for your first 1,000 rounds adds the tool cost to the labor hours. Tasks are listed from least to most expensive over the first 1,000 rounds. Example numbers. Tool costs are approximate.

Even if a task shrank groups by 5 percent, it would add about 1 hit per 100 at 700 yards, and less at 1,000 (see The Precision Rifle Handbook). Annealing’s proven benefit is longer neck life. That saves brass only if split necks are what retire your cases (see Chapter 21).

Test any optional step before you adopt it. Split one brass lot in half: prepare one half and leave the other alone. Fire the two halves in interleaved 5-shot blocks (see The Precision Rifle Handbook). Fire about 35 shots of each to compare mean radius, or about 50 of each to compare velocity SD. Those sample sizes catch a 1.5 to 1 difference 8 times in 10. Catching a 5 percent difference would take about 2,255 shots per half, so a 35- or 50-shot test will almost always show no difference. That result simply tells you the effect is smaller than 1.5 to 1.
