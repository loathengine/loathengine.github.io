Chapter 19. Sizing and Shoulder Bump

Key points

  • Fired cases from your own chamber are the reference for setting the sizing die.
  • A small bump, .001 to .002 inch for a bolt action, lets the case chamber easily without leaving it room to stretch.
  • Neck tension comes from interference. A bushing sets it from outside the neck, a mandrel from inside.
  • Runout can show on fired cases, sized cases or loaded rounds. Where it first shows points to its source.
  • Once the first batch shows steady readings, a sample is enough.

Why it matters

A bottleneck case stops in the chamber on its shoulder (see Chapter 4). When it fires, the case swells to fill the chamber and then springs back a little. Sizing squeezes it back down so it chambers again. The die also pushes the shoulder back toward the head. That move is the bump.

With too little bump, the bolt closes hard or not at all. With too much, there is a gap between the shoulder and the chamber, and the case stretches just above the head every time it fires (see Chapter 18). The right bump is small, and you measure it against your own chamber.

The neck holds the bullet. Its grip depends on how much smaller the sized neck is than the bullet. That difference is the interference, and sizing sets it.

How it is done

Choose a sizing method

Pick the die for your rifle and your goal (see Chapter 5).

MethodWhat it sizesUse it when
Full-length dieNeck, shoulder and bodyThe default for any rifle.
Full-length bushing dieShoulder and body, with a bushing for the neckYou want to choose neck tension.
Neck dieNeck onlyBolt actions only, with cases fired in this rifle. The body still grows, so a body or full-length die is needed later.
Body dieShoulder and body onlyNeck-sized cases have grown tight in the chamber.

Belted magnums headspace on the belt by standard (see Chapter 4), but size them by the shoulder like any other bottleneck case. Push the shoulder back no further than smooth chambering needs. Belted cases tend to stretch just ahead of the belt, and oversizing shortens their life.1

Contested neck sizing shoots smaller groups

The claim. A neck-sized case keeps its chamber’s shape, so it holds the bullet in line and shoots smaller groups.

The physics. A fired case does fit its chamber closely. But the body and shoulder grow a little with each firing. Sooner or later the bolt closes hard, and the case needs a body die or a full-length die. A full-length die set for .001 to .002 inch of bump also leaves the case close to chamber size.

The test. Size one batch each way and load both the same. Fire about 35 of each in interleaved blocks, pool each batch’s groups into one composite group and compare them (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).

The data. No large-sample controlled test has been published. In a 2024 survey, 94 percent of top Precision Rifle Series (PRS) shooters sized full-length.2

The verdict. Plausible but unproven. No large-sample test shows a precision gain or rules it out. The popularity of full-length sizing among top shooters is not proof either. Run the test above if the question matters to you.

Measure fired brass from your own chamber

  1. Pick five to ten cases fired in this rifle. Keep them unsized as your fired reference. If you read runout, use them for step 15 too. Any maker will do, such as factory cases fired in this rifle or the cases from your baseline (see The Precision Rifle Handbook). Use cases fired with a normal load, not a light one. Measure them clean and dry, before any lube touches them.

If you have no fired cases yet. Set the die from new brass. Measure a few new cases base to shoulder. Back the die off, size a case and turn the die down in small steps until the whole neck is sized. Stop before the base-to-shoulder reading drops. After the first firing, measure those cases (step 2) and set the die for bump (steps 5 to 9).

  1. Measure each case with the base-to-shoulder comparator. Clamp it to the caliper jaws (see Chapter 9). Zero the caliper the same way every time. Seat the case head flat on the fixed jaw and close the jaws gently on the shoulder (see Figure 19-1). Write down every reading. Work out the average and the spread.
Fired readings (inch):  1.631  1.632  1.631  1.632  1.631
Average = 8.157 ÷ 5 = 1.6314 (call it 1.631)
Spread  = 1.632 − 1.631 = .001
Bump for a bolt action = .001 to .002
Target sized reading   = 1.629 to 1.630

Example numbers. Not load data. The absolute readings depend on the insert. The differences between them do not.

Lubricate

If you anneal, do it before you lube (see Chapter 21).

  1. Put a thin film on the case body. Roll the cases on a lube pad, or spray them standing in a loading block (see Chapter 5). Keep lube off the shoulder.
  1. Lube the inside of the neck lightly if your die has an expander ball. A neck brush dipped in dry lube does the job. The ball then passes without grabbing.

Watch the first cases. Excess lube trapped in the die dents the shoulder. Too little lube can stick a case in the die. If one sticks, stop and do not force the handle. Free it with a stuck-case remover made for the die (see Chapters 5 and 31). On a long run, swab the die after about 50 cases.3

Set up the full-length die by measurement

  1. Set the pin and start high. Fit a starting bushing if your die takes one (see step 10). Take out the expander ball if you will open necks on a mandrel (see step 12). The decapping pin should stand at least 3/16 inch below the die.4 Raise the ram and screw the die in until it touches the shell holder (see Figure 19-2). Lower the ram and back the die out one full turn. A shoulder pushed back too far cannot be pulled forward again, so start long and work down.
  1. Size one lubed case and measure it. Wipe off the lube before you measure. Use the same insert and the same zero as for the fired cases (see Figure 19-1).
  1. Turn the die down in small steps. Size the same case after each step and measure it again. Turn the die down an eighth of a turn at a time (about .009 inch) until the shoulder reading starts to drop. Once the shoulder moves, turn only about 5 degrees at a time (about .001 inch). If the first drop overshoots your target bump, back the die out an eighth of a turn, take a fresh fired case, and work down in 5-degree steps. The thread math tells you how far each turn moves the die.
Most dies: 14 threads per inch
Die travel per full turn = 1 ÷ 14 = .0714 inch
Turn for .001 inch = .001 ÷ .0714 = .014 of a turn
.014 × 360 degrees = about 5 degrees

Example numbers. Not load data. The math holds only while the die floats above the shell holder. Once they touch, more turning only loads the press frame. The shoulder then moves less than the math says, and less evenly.

  1. Stop at the target and check the fit in the rifle. Stop when the sized reading is .001 to .002 inch below the fired average, or .003 to .005 inch below it for a semi-automatic.5 Chamber the sized case. The bolt should close with the same feel as on an empty chamber. In a semi-automatic, check the case in a case gauge instead (see Chapter 26).
  1. Lock the ring. Once the case fits, lock the ring. Mark the die and the ring so any later movement shows.
Evidence · Supported by data

Excess shoulder bump shortens case life and can cause head separations.6

Neck tension

Neck tension is the grip of the neck on the bullet. Interference is the part you set with tools. It is the bullet diameter minus the inside diameter of the sized neck. Most loaders work between .001 and .003 inch. Pick a value in that range and hold it steady. Neck hardness, wall thickness and the length of bullet held in the neck also change the grip.

Neck inside  = sized neck outside − (2 × neck wall thickness)
Interference = bullet diameter − neck inside diameter
Quick check  = loaded neck outside − sized neck outside

Example (.308 Win):
  Bullet .3080, neck wall .0135, sized outside .3325
  Neck inside = .3325 − .0270 = .3055
  Interference = .3080 − .3055 = .0025 inch
  Loaded outside .3350 − sized .3325 = .0025 inch

Example numbers. Not load data. The check needs no wall measurement, so it is the quick way to confirm interference in the workshop. With a standard full-length die, the expander ball sets it for you. Read it with the check above. To choose a different value, use a bushing or a mandrel (see Chapter 5).

With a bushing

  1. Choose a bushing. Measure the neck of a round loaded with your bullet in this brass (see Figure 19-3). With no such round, add twice the neck wall thickness to the bullet diameter. Start with a bushing smaller than that figure by the interference you want.
  1. Size a few cases and check them. Brass springs back after it leaves the bushing, so the neck reads larger than the bushing (see Figure 19-4). Use the check above. Change the bushing one .001 inch step at a time until it gives your interference.

With an expander mandrel

  1. Size the neck smaller than the mandrel. Use a sizing die with no expander ball (see Figure 19-5), or one with a small bushing.
  1. Open the neck on the mandrel. The mandrel sets the inside diameter directly. Because the brass springs back slightly smaller after the mandrel comes out, pick a mandrel about .0005 to .001 inch larger than your target inside neck diameter. Measure a sized neck and adjust if needed.

An expander ball or a mandrel can add runout. Measure your own (see the runout steps below).

Evidence · Supported by data

Live-fire testing found no link between neck tension and group size, unless the tension was heavy enough to damage bullets as they were seated. Tension that heavy also tends to make CBTO vary more.7 Tests also show almost no link between neck tension and muzzle velocity across normal interference (.001 to .003 inch). At short to medium ranges, velocity differences move bullet drop so little that groups remain circular rather than vertically strung (see Chapter 31).

Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Opening necks on a mandrel makes neck tension more uniform. In one small test, new necks opened on a mandrel needed less seating force and gave smoother force curves than untouched new necks.8 In the 2024 survey, 54 percent of top PRS shooters used a mandrel.2 Popularity is not proof.

Neck turning and neck-shoulder donuts

Neck turning shaves brass from the outside of the case neck to make the wall thickness uniform all the way round. Factory chambers have generous neck clearance, so standard brass chambers without turning. Tight-neck custom match chambers are cut to a specific loaded neck diameter. Cases for those chambers must be turned so the neck has enough clearance to release the bullet safely on firing.

Repeated firing and sizing also cause brass to flow forward from the shoulder into the neck-shoulder junction. That thickened internal ring is called a donut. When long, heavy bullets are seated deep into the case, as is common in overbore cartridges such as the 7mm Remington Short Action Ultra Magnum (7mm SAUM), the bullet’s shank can reach the donut. That contact raises seating force, makes neck tension uneven, and can raise chamber pressure if the thickened neck pinches the bullet in the chamber. Check for a donut by sliding a bullet or a pin of bullet diameter down through a sized neck by hand. If it stops or drags at the neck-shoulder junction, ream the inside of the neck, expand the neck on a mandrel and turn the outside, or seat the bullet ahead of the junction if your chamber and magazine allow it.

Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Neck turning is required for tight-neck custom chambers to maintain safe neck clearance. In standard factory chambers with quality brass, large-sample controlled tests have not shown a clear group-size gain from neck turning alone.

Size the batch

  1. Size the batch and sample it. Measure every case in the first batch. Once the readings hold steady, measure one case in five. If the readings drift, stop and check the lube and the brass hardness first (see Chapters 21 and 31). With a mandrel, open each neck after sizing (step 13). Leave the lube on the batch for now. The final clean takes it off after trimming (see Chapter 18).

Runout

Runout is how far the neck or the bullet swings off the case axis in one turn. The full swing is the total indicated runout (TIR). Reading it takes a concentricity gauge (see Chapter 9), and these steps are optional.

  1. Read a fired neck, then a sized neck. Rest the case body on the gauge supports, one near the head and one near the shoulder. Put the indicator tip on the neck. Turn the case one full turn and note the highest and lowest readings.
  1. Read a loaded round. Put the tip on the bullet just ahead of the case mouth (see Figure 19-6). Turn the round one full turn and note the readings.
  1. Work out the TIR and find the source. Use the table below. Set your own runout limit from your records, and test it with enough shots (see The Precision Rifle Handbook).
TIR = highest reading − lowest reading
Example: .0021 − .0004 = .0017 inch

Example numbers. Not load data.

Where runout showsLikely sourceWhat to try
Fired casesThe chamber or the brassCheck neck wall thickness around the neck. Sort out uneven cases.
Sized cases, but not fired casesThe sizing die or the expanderLube the neck. Try a bushing or a mandrel.
Loaded rounds, but not sized casesThe seating dieCheck the seater stem fit (see Chapter 24).

Lower runout gives smaller groups. Straight ammunition should start straight into the throat. No public large-sample test has yet tied a runout limit to group size.
