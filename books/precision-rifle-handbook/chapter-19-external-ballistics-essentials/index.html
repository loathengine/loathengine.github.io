Chapter 19. External Ballistics Essentials

Key points

  • Drag shapes everything after the muzzle. It depends on air density, velocity and the bullet’s shape. It changes fastest near the speed of sound.
  • A ballistic coefficient (BC) compares your bullet with a standard shape. G7 fits long boat-tail bullets and G1 fits flat-base bullets. A BC means nothing without its own drag model.
  • Wind drift depends on lag time, the time that drag costs the bullet. A fast bullet with a low BC can drift more than a slower bullet with a high BC.
  • Spin causes spin drift, and a crosswind causes aerodynamic jump. Both are small and predictable, so a good drop chart includes them.
  • The Earth’s rotation matters only past roughly 800 to 1,000 yards. Air density matters at every distance.

Why it matters

Your load leaves the muzzle at a measured velocity. From then on, the air, gravity and the bullet’s spin decide where it lands. Every drop chart rests on a few inputs: muzzle velocity, a BC and its drag model, the air, sight height and zero. When one of them is wrong, the miss looks like a load problem. Shooters then change the load to cure an input error.

Most of these effects have a short formula, so you can check a chart by hand.

How it is done

Drag and the Mach regimes

Air slows the bullet from the moment it leaves the muzzle. The drag force is:

Fd = 0.5 × ρ × v² × A × Cd(M)

Fd      drag force
ρ       air density (rho)
v       bullet velocity through the air
A       frontal area of the bullet
Cd(M)   drag coefficient at Mach number M
M       v ÷ speed of sound

Drag rises with the square of velocity and in step with air density. The drag coefficient is not a constant. It changes with the Mach number, the bullet’s velocity divided by the speed of sound.

The speed of sound depends on air temperature. It is about 1,116 fps on a standard sea-level day at 59 °F, so a bullet at 2,700 fps flies at about Mach 2.4. As the bullet slows, its Mach number falls and its drag coefficient changes.

Ballisticians divide the flight into three regimes.

RegimeMach numberWhat happens
SupersonicAbove 1.2A shock wave forms at the bullet’s tip. Drag behaves predictably and models fit well
TransonicAbout 0.8 to 1.2Airflow partially detaches and buffets the bullet. Drag changes fastest, real bullets depart most from the standard drag curves, and some lose stability
SubsonicBelow about 0.8Skin friction and base drag make up most of the drag, and the drag coefficient is lower than it is near Mach 1

For a typical rifle bullet, the drag coefficient rises as the bullet slows toward Mach 1 and peaks near it.

Predictions lose their grip in the transonic zone. Real drag curves differ most from the standards there.1 Some bullets tumble as they slow through it.2 So treat the range where your bullet slows to Mach 1.2 as the honest end of a predicted chart. That speed is near 1,340 fps, which for many cartridges is in the neighborhood of 1,000 yards.1

Subsonic and rimfire (.22 LR) flight. Standard-velocity .22 Long Rifle (.22 LR) match ammunition leaves the muzzle below the speed of sound, typically around 1,050 to 1,090 fps. Because the bullet stays subsonic for its entire flight, it avoids the transonic drag spike and buffeting that disturb faster rimfire loads. High-velocity .22 LR rounds launch near 1,200 to 1,280 fps and slow through Mach 1 within the first 40 to 60 yards, which often opens groups at longer rimfire distances.

Subsonic flight does not eliminate wind drift. A typical 40 gr lead round-nose .22 LR bullet has a low BC (roughly G1 .120 to .140) and a long time of flight. By the lag-time rule below, that long flight time produces large wind drift even in light breezes at 100 to 300 yards. Air temperature also matters more near Mach 1. A standard-velocity rimfire load that stays cleanly subsonic at 75 °F (where Mach 1 is about 1,134 fps) can enter transonic airflow on a freezing morning (where Mach 1 drops to about 1,087 fps), changing both drag and point of impact.

Ballistic coefficient, G1 and G7

A BC is not a physical constant. It is an index that compares your bullet’s drag with the drag of a standard reference projectile.

BC = sectional density ÷ i

sectional density   weight ÷ diameter² (see below)
i                   form factor = Cd of your bullet ÷ Cd of the
                    standard projectile at the same Mach number

A bullet shaped exactly like the standard has a form factor of 1. A sleeker bullet has a form factor below 1, so its BC is higher.

Three reference standards are in common use.

  • G1. A flat-base projectile about 3.3 calibers long with a 2-caliber-radius ogive. It comes from artillery firing trials in Germany and France in the late 19th century, later turned into firing tables.3
  • G7. A long boat-tail projectile with a 10-caliber-radius ogive and a 7.5-degree boat tail. Its shape is close to that of a modern long-range bullet.
  • RA4. A rimfire-specific drag curve modeled on a standard 40 gr .22 LR lead round-nose projectile. It tracks subsonic rimfire deceleration far better than the artillery-derived G1 curve.

A G1 BC for a long boat-tail bullet does not hold steady. The G1 number that fits the trajectory falls as the bullet slows, which shooters call BC decay. Some makers publish several G1 BCs, one per velocity band, to work around it. A G7 BC for the same bullet usually needs only one number.4

For the same bullet, the G7 number is roughly half the G1 number. The two are not interchangeable. Entering a G1 BC with the G7 drag model selected (or vice versa) produces a badly wrong trajectory that the solver cannot flag.5

Use G7 for long boat-tail bullets, G1 for centerfire flat-base and round-nose bullets, and RA4 or a custom Doppler drag model (CDM) for .22 LR rimfire. With a standard that does not resemble your bullet, the drag can be right in the middle of the flight and wrong at both ends.6 For subsonic .22 LR, a single G1 BC often diverges by 0.3 to 0.6+ MIL between 100 and 300 yards because the G1 curve does not match how a heeled, blunt-nosed lead bullet decelerates near Mach 0.95 down to Mach 0.70.

Neither G1 nor G7 matches a real bullet exactly. A bullet’s own measured custom Doppler drag curve does better, most of all in the transonic zone and across subsonic .22 LR trajectories. For centerfire rifles, expect little or no gain from a custom curve if the bullet never slows below about 1,340 fps.1

A published BC is also referenced to a standard atmosphere: either the ICAO standard atmosphere or the Army Standard Metro.4 Because their air densities differ by about 2 percent, using an Army Metro BC in a solver set to ICAO (or vice versa) starts with a 2 percent drag error. When the bullet maker specifies the atmosphere standard, match it in your solver.

Evidence · Supported by data

One Doppler radar test of several current bullets found only one that matched the G7 curve in both value and shape. Even so, G7 predicts these bullets better than G1 does.7 G7 BCs predict well until the bullet nears transonic speed.1

Sectional density

Sectional density is the bullet’s weight divided by the square of its diameter.

Sectional density = w ÷ (7,000 × d²)

w      bullet weight in grains
d      bullet diameter in inches
7,000  grains per pound

Example numbers. Not load data. A 140 gr bullet of .264 inch diameter has a sectional density of 0.287. A 175 gr bullet of .308 inch diameter has a sectional density of 0.264.

Drag pushes against the bullet’s frontal area while mass resists slowing down. Between two bullets of the same shape, the one with higher sectional density retains velocity better. That is why BC equals sectional density divided by form factor: sectional density accounts for mass per frontal area, and form factor accounts for nose and base shape. Shape still matters heavily, so a heavy, blunt bullet can have a lower BC than a lighter, sleeker one.

Ogives and boat tails

The ogive is the bullet’s curved nose, and its profile is tangent, secant or hybrid (see The Handloading Handbook, Chapter 14). A secant ogive gives a sleeker nose for its length, so very-low-drag designs use it. A boat tail tapers the base and reduces base drag. Small differences in the tip and the base from one bullet to the next change drag from shot to shot (see Chapter 21).

The ogive and the boat tail matter in the seating die too (see The Handloading Handbook).

Air density and density altitude

Drag rises in step with air density. Air density depends on pressure, temperature and humidity.

Pressure comes in two forms. Station pressure is what a barometer reads where you stand. Weather reports give pressure corrected to sea level. A ballistic solver must know which one you entered. Enter the wrong type and it applies the altitude correction twice or not at all.

Density altitude (DA) folds the air into one number. It is the altitude in the standard atmosphere that has the same air density as the air you are in. Higher temperature, lower pressure and higher elevation all raise DA. More humidity raises it slightly, because moist air is a little lighter than dry air.

Higher DA means thinner air, and thinner air means less drag, less drop and less drift. Cold, dense air at sea level means more of all three.

The sizes are worth knowing. A 3 percent error in air density moves the trajectory as much as a 3 percent error in BC. Each change below moves air density by about the amount shown.8

ChangeAir density change
15 °F of temperature3%
0.9 inHg of pressure3%
1,000 feet of density altitude3%
0 to 100% humidity at 60 °F0.7%
0 to 100% humidity at 80 °F1.3%
0 to 100% humidity at 100 °F2.4%

Humidity matters least in mild air. Warm air holds far more water, so its effect grows with heat. The humidity figures are for sea-level pressure.

An older rule of thumb says each 2,000 feet of DA takes about 1 MOA off the drop at 1,000 yards. It fits a typical 6.5 mm match load. A faster, higher-BC bullet changes less. A slower bullet that nears the speed of sound by 1,000 yards changes more. Treat the rule as a rough check on your solver.

In practice, a drop chart from a hot August day will not hold in a cold December. A chart from a home range at 500 feet will not hold at 6,000 feet. Air temperature also sets the speed of sound, so it moves the range where your bullet goes transonic. Powder temperature is a separate effect on muzzle velocity (see Chapter 23).

Wind and lag time

Drag acts along the air flow the bullet feels. In a crosswind, that flow comes partly from the side. So part of the drag pushes the bullet downwind. A bullet with no drag would not drift at all.

The classical lag-time rule follows from this.

Deflection = W × (TOF − dist ÷ v0)

W                 crosswind speed in feet per second
TOF               actual time of flight to the target in seconds
dist              distance to the target in feet
v0                muzzle velocity in feet per second
dist ÷ v0         time of flight in a vacuum
TOF − dist ÷ v0   lag time

Lag time is the time drag costs the bullet. Less drag means less lag and less drift. Total time of flight does not set the drift. So a bullet that starts fast but sheds speed quickly can drift more than a slower bullet that holds its speed.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. At 1,000 yards (3,000 feet), a bullet launched at 2,700 fps has a vacuum flight time of 3,000 ÷ 2,700 = 1.11 seconds. With a charted TOF of 1.52 seconds, lag time is 1.52 − 1.11 = 0.41 seconds. A 10 mph crosswind (10 × 5,280 ÷ 3,600 = 14.7 fps) therefore produces 14.7 × 0.41 = 6.0 feet (about 72 inches) of drift.

Only the crosswind part of the wind moves the bullet sideways.

Crosswind = wind speed × sin(wind angle)

wind angle = the angle between the wind and the line of fire

Winds from 3 or 9 o’clock are full value; 12 or 6 o’clock have no crosswind component. Winds at 1, 5, 7, and 11 o’clock (30 degrees off) are half value; 2, 4, 8, and 10 o’clock (60 degrees off) are about 87 percent value.

Table 19-1. Drift per 1 mph of full-value crosswind for four loads, computed on a standard day with a 100-yard zero.6 Example numbers. Not load data.

Load300 yd500 yd700 yd1,000 yd

The 55 gr .223 starts fastest yet drifts most due to its low BC and long lag time; stepping up to the 77 gr match bullet cuts 1,000-yard drift by nearly 30 percent, while the 6.5 Creedmoor drifts about 36 percent less than the .308.

Spin drift

As gravity curves the trajectory downward, a spinning bullet’s nose stays slightly above the path. Gyroscopic action turns this into a slight nose-right attitude (for right-hand twist), and air pushes the bullet right—formally yaw-of-repose drift, or spin drift.7 Right-hand twist drifts right and left-hand left, independent of wind, growing faster than distance to several inches by 1,000 yards.9 A published estimate uses stability factor and time of flight:10

Spin drift (inches) = 1.25 × (Sg + 1.2) × TOF^1.83

Sg    gyroscopic stability factor (see Chapter 18)
TOF   time of flight in seconds

Example numbers. Not load data. At Sg 1.6 and 1.5 s TOF, spin drift is 1.25 × 2.8 × 2.10 = 7.4 inches. Use this cautiously: a comparison across five bullets (Sg 1.6 to 2.2) found the formula tended to read high,11 and short bullets in fast twists extrapolate beyond that band.9 Spin drift adds to wind drift in the same direction (e.g., left-to-right wind with right-hand twist) and subtracts from opposing wind.

Aerodynamic jump

A crosswind also deflects a spinning bullet vertically—aerodynamic jump. With right-hand twist, wind from the right lifts the shot and wind from the left drops it (left-hand twist reverses both); jump scales linearly with crosswind speed and vanishes in a pure headwind or tailwind.

In a full-value crosswind, the Table 19-1 6.5 Creedmoor jumps about .28 inch/mph at 700 yards and .39 inch/mph at 1,000 yards.6 A right-hand-twist card built for a 3 o’clock wind under-holds elevation in a 9 o’clock wind by twice the jump—about 1 MOA for a 175 gr .308 in a 10 mph wind.9 Misreading crosswind therefore costs both windage and a little elevation (see Chapter 21).

Coriolis and Eötvös

Earth’s rotation adds two small effects: horizontal Coriolis deflects bullets right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern (growing with range and latitude), while Eötvös shifts eastbound shots high and westbound shots low (zero due north or south). Out to 1,000–1,500 yards both remain smaller than normal shot-to-shot velocity, drag, and wind variation,7 but they still shift group center a few inches at 1,000 yards—enter latitude and azimuth past about 800 yards6 and always when truing a solver (see Chapter 20).
