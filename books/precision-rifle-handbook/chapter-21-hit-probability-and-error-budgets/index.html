Chapter 21. Hit Probability and Error Budgets

Key points

  • The goal of load development is hits on a real target at a real distance. The load with the smallest group up close is not always the one that hits farthest.
  • Hit chance comes from an error budget. Every uncertainty, in inches at the target, adds to one spread on each axis.
  • Past about 500 yards for a typical centerfire rifle, the shooter’s wind call usually costs more hits than the rifle or the ammunition.
  • Gains from lower velocity SD shrink below about 10 fps for typical long-range targets. The SD worth chasing depends on your distance and target.
  • Run the error budget backward to turn a hit goal into limits for mean radius and velocity SD.

Why it matters

Load development ends with one goal. Groups on paper are a means to an end, and the end is hit probability. A Weapon Employment Zone (WEZ) analysis combines the rifle’s dispersion, the ammunition’s velocity spread and the shooter’s own errors in reading wind and range. Its answer is a plain sentence, such as: “With this load, I have an 88 percent probability of hitting a 10-inch plate at 800 yards in a 5 mph wind.”

That sentence also shows where effort pays. A handloader can spend a season chasing velocity SD. Hit probability tells you whether that season would buy more hits than practice at reading wind.

How it is done

From groups to hits

  1. Start with a composite group of 20 to 30 shots or more. Take its corrected mean radius (MR) in MOA. For a cautious answer, use the upper end of its 90 percent interval (see Chapter 15).
  2. Convert the mean radius to a standard deviation on each axis.
σ = MR ÷ 1.2533
  1. If you have separate horizontal and vertical standard deviations for the group, use those instead.
  2. Convert to inches at the target distance. One MOA is about 1.047 inches per 100 yards. That is 3.1 inches at 300 yards, 5.2 at 500, 7.3 at 700 and 10.5 at 1,000.

Use the distribution that matches your target’s shape (see Chapter 15). A square or rectangular plate uses the normal (single-axis) percentages on width and height separately. A circular plate uses the Rayleigh (radial distance) formula below.

A group shot at 100 yards shows almost none of the velocity SD’s effect.1 So add velocity SD separately at distance. A group shot outdoors already carries some of that day’s wind. Adding a full wind error on top errs on the wide side.

List every uncertainty

Use each uncertainty as one standard deviation. The published WEZ wind and range figures are 95 percent bounds, which are two standard deviations.2 Halve them. Using the ± figure as the standard deviation doubles the error.

Keep the same sight setting, and do not re-zero. Take the difference at each distance. Divide the velocity difference by 10 for inches per fps. If your solver automatically re-zeroes the rifle at 100 yards when you add 10 fps, it understates how much a faster shot rises downrange, because a faster shot also strikes slightly higher at 100 yards. For this load, a re-zeroed chart shows a 2.83-inch change at 1,000 yards (the right number for truing a zeroed chart in Chapter 20), whereas a shot that is 10 fps faster at a fixed scope setting actually strikes 3.03 inches higher. The difference is the .02-inch rise at 100 yards multiplied by 10 (for 1,000 yards): 2.83 + 10 × .02 = 3.03 inches.

A misread crosswind also moves the shot a little up or down through aerodynamic jump. For this load that is .28 inch per mph at 700 yards and .39 inch at 1,000 (see Chapter 19).3

Combine them

Independent errors add by root-sum-square. Combine the horizontal sources and the vertical sources separately.

σ total = √(σ1² + σ2² + σ3² + ...)

The rule has a consequence. The largest source dominates the total. Shrinking a small source barely changes it.

Turn the spread into a hit chance

For a rectangular plate of width W and height H, with the group centered on it:

P(hit) = (share within ±(W ÷ 2) across)
         × (share within ±(H ÷ 2) up and down)

z = (half the plate's width or height) ÷ σ on that axis

Read the share for each axis from Table 21-1, or from any normal table. Between rows, use the spreadsheet formula below, or the row with the smaller z for a cautious answer.

In a spreadsheet, the share for z is 2 × NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE) − 1, and z for a given share is NORM.S.INV((1 + share) ÷ 2).

For a round plate of radius R, when the group is round with the same sigma on both axes:

P(hit) = 1 − exp(−R² ÷ (2 × σ²))

If the two sigmas differ, use the larger one for a cautious answer.

Both formulas assume normal errors and a group centered on the target (see Figure 21-1). A zero error or an aim error moves the group off center. That costs more hits than these formulas show.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Take the example 6.5 Creedmoor load and a 12-inch square steel plate at 700 yards. Rifle and ammunition dispersion is 0.35 MOA, one sigma on each axis. That is 2.56 inches at 700 yards (0.35 × 7.3). The velocity SD is 10 fps, which adds 1.23 inches of vertical spread.

The wind call error is 1.25 mph, a typical medium-confidence call.2 At 700 yards that adds 3.98 inches of horizontal spread (1.25 × 3.18). Range error, BC variation, cant and the aerodynamic jump from the wind call error (see Chapter 19) are left out to keep the example short. Each would lower the hit chance a little.

Horizontal σ = √(2.56² + 3.98²) = 4.73 inches
Vertical σ   = √(2.56² + 1.23²) = 2.84 inches

Horizontal: z = 6 ÷ 4.73 = 1.27   share = 0.796
Vertical:   z = 6 ÷ 2.84 = 2.11   share = 0.965

P(hit) = 0.796 × 0.965 = 0.77

About 77 shots in 100 hit the plate.

From a hit goal to load limits

Run the same steps backward to set a goal for a load before you test it.

  1. Write the target, the distance and the hit chance you want.
  2. Split the hit chance between the two axes. The two shares multiply to the hit chance. The wind call usually dominates the horizontal, and the charge barely changes the horizontal spread. So give the vertical a high share, such as 95 percent, and let the horizontal take the rest. For a round plate, work forward with the round formula, trying limits until the hit chance meets the goal.
  3. For each axis, find z for its share in Table 21-1. The largest sigma the axis can carry is half the target’s size on that axis divided by z.
  4. Take out the errors the load cannot change, by root-sum-square. On the horizontal that is the wind call, and cant if you shoot without a level. What is left is the most the rifle and ammunition may add. If the wind call alone uses up the whole allowance, the goal cannot be met at that call. Lower the goal, shoot closer or call the wind better. Work the limits for each candidate bullet, since its drift per mph sets the wind term.
  5. Convert that rifle-and-ammunition sigma to MOA and then to a mean radius: MR = 1.2533 × σ.
  6. The rifle-and-ammunition sigma counts on the vertical too. Check that it is below the vertical’s largest sigma. If so, take it out by root-sum-square. What is left is the velocity allowance. Divide it by the inches per fps at that distance to get the largest velocity SD. If not, the split does not fit. Give the vertical a smaller share, give the horizontal the rest, and repeat steps 3 to 6.

Because these target limits depend on how you split the budget between horizontal and vertical, they are one way to meet the goal, not the only way. Judge a candidate load using the upper ends of its 90 percent confidence intervals (see Chapter 16). Before rejecting a load that misses one limit, calculate its forward hit probability using those upper bounds: tighter dispersion can offset a slightly higher velocity SD (or vice versa) and still meet your hit goal.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

The same 12-inch plate at 700 yards, with the same medium-confidence wind call of 3.98 inches. The goal is 75 hits in 100.

Shares:      horizontal 78.9% (z = 1.25), vertical 95.4% (z = 2.0)
             0.789 × 0.954 = 0.75

             2.68 is under 3.00, so the split fits
             velocity allowance = √(3.00² − 2.68²) = 1.35 inches

The load needs a corrected mean radius of about 0.46 MOA or less and a velocity SD of about 11 fps or less. With a perfect load, the 1.25 mph call alone still holds the hit chance to 87 in 100. A wind call good to 0.5 mph would leave 4.53 inches for the rifle on the horizontal. That is more than the vertical’s 3.00, so work the split again with a smaller vertical share before you use it.

Which uncertainty costs the most hits

Change one input at a time and work the hit chance again. Then rank the changes by hits gained or lost. WEZ analysis can serve as this kind of sensitivity test.2

Halving the velocity SD adds about 1 point. Moving from a medium-confidence to a high-confidence wind call adds about 15. At 1,000 yards on a 20-inch plate, with 1 percent BC variation added, the pattern holds. Halving velocity SD adds about 2 points there, and a high-confidence wind call adds about 24.

The same numbers give a miss budget. In the worked example, about 20 percent of shots miss out the sides and about 3.5 percent miss high or low. Horizontal misses outnumber vertical ones about six to one, so work on the wind before the ammunition (see Figure 21-2).

Why wind call error usually dominates

For a typical centerfire rifle, past about 500 yards, wind call error usually costs the most hits.3 In subsonic .22 LR, those distances compress sharply: wind call error dominates by 100 yards, and a 15 fps velocity SD adds roughly 4 to 5 inches of vertical spread by 300 yards. There are three reasons wind dominates.

Drift grows faster than range. Rifle and ammunition spread grows in step with distance. Wind drift grows faster, because the bullet sheds speed. For the example load, drift per 1 mph of crosswind grows from .52 inch at 300 yards to 7.19 inches at 1,000.3 That is about 14 times the drift over a little more than three times the distance.

Calling the wind is hard. A high-confidence call, with good indicators, is good to about ±1 mph. A call in a difficult place may not get within ±4 mph (see the table above).2

A misread costs its full drift. The shot moves by the full drift of the error, whatever the wind’s speed.

Gusts add on top. A wind that varies by 3 mph (one sigma) spreads the example load by 9.5 inches at 700 yards. Direction errors cost little near full value and a lot at shallow angles. In a 10 mph wind at 700 yards, a 10-degree error costs the example load .5 inch near full value but 5.1 inches near a 30-degree wind.3

Try a simple test. Work your hit chance twice, once with your honest call error and once with none. The difference is your wind-reading skill measured in hits. For most shooters at distance it is larger than anything reloading can add.

Evidence · Plausible but unproven

In the worked example, wind call error is the largest term at 700 yards: 3.98 inches against 2.56 for the rifle and ammunition. That is a model result, built on measured drift and published estimates of call error.2 No study has yet measured how call error grows with wind speed.5

How much velocity SD is worth chasing

The SD worth chasing depends on your distance and target. To set your own SD target from hit probability:

  1. Name the real job: the target’s size and the longest distance you shoot it.
  2. Use your honest wind call error.
  3. Work the hit chance at your measured SD.
  4. Work it again with the SD 5 fps lower (see Figure 21-3).
  5. Compare that gain with what a better wind call buys at the same distance.
  6. Chase SD only when the gain is worth the work to you, or when vertical misses lead the miss budget.

How low to push the SD is a contested question.

Contested long range needs a single-digit velocity SD

The claim. A velocity SD under 10 fps is the gold standard for long range. It is single digits or nothing.

The physics. Velocity SD moves shots up and down only. It adds by root-sum-square to rifle dispersion and BC variation. Its cost grows with range, but wind error usually grows faster.

The test. Work the hit chance at your distance and target, at your measured SD and at a lower one.

The data. In one modeled long-range scenario with centered groups, cutting SD from 20 to 15 fps gave about 5 more hits in 100. Cutting it from 15 to 10 fps added about 3 more, and cutting it from 10 to 3 fps only about 1 more. The gains were a little larger on the farther of two targets, and they shrank at every step.6 For a typical centerfire rifle, inside about 300 yards, a 15 fps SD adds only about .3 inch of vertical. In subsonic .22 LR, by contrast, wind dominates by 100 yards and a 15 fps SD adds roughly 4 to 5 inches of vertical by 300 yards.

The measurement limits the chase too. Instrument error adds to the true SD (see Chapter 7). Ten shots pin an SD only loosely (see Chapter 15). A single-digit SD from a 5-shot string proves little.

The verdict. Plausible but unproven. Lower SD helps, but the gains shrink below about 10 fps when wind error is present.6 The gains are largest at long range, on small targets, for shooters who call wind well. Inside about 300 yards for a typical centerfire rifle they barely exist. These figures come from a model built on measured drift and dispersion, not from hits counted on targets. A fixed SD target is a habit, not a finding.

Worked example: when single-digit SD protects score (1,000-yard F-Class). Example numbers. Not load data.

F-Class long-range competition is the classic exception where single-digit velocity SD and tight BC consistency genuinely pay off. At 1,000 yards, the circular 10-ring is 1.0 MOA (10.47 inches diameter, radius 5.24 inches) and the X-ring is 0.5 MOA (5.24 inches diameter, radius 2.62 inches). Suppose a bench-rested F-Class rifle shoots 0.25 MOA one-sigma dispersion (2.62 inches at 1,000 yards) and range wind flags compress wind-call error to 0.40 mph (2.88 inches at 7.19 inches per mph), giving a horizontal σ of 3.89 inches. Once wind flags and a 0.25 MOA rifle shrink the other error terms, vertical spread becomes the bottleneck: cutting velocity SD from 15 fps down to 5 to 7 fps with consistent BC adds roughly 16 to 19 percentage points of 10-ring hits and 7 to 9 points of X-count.

Where effort pays

The same sums rank every step of the process, not only the wind call and velocity SD. Weigh what each step costs against the hits it adds at your distance, and do the cheap steps that pay first.

Count cost in rounds’ worth. The components of one loaded round count as 1: the bullet, powder, primer and once-fired brass. A tool counts as the number of rounds its price would buy. Count an hour of your time as 20 rounds’ worth, or whatever it is worth to you. A count in rounds keeps its meaning as prices change.

  1. List the steps you could add, and what each costs in rounds fired, tools and hours.
  2. Write down what each step changes: a velocity error, the velocity SD or the dispersion.
  3. Work the hit chance with and without it, at your distance and target (see “Turn the spread into a hit chance” above).
  4. Divide each step’s cost by the hits it adds. Start with the steps that cost least for each hit.

For scale, a wind call improved from 1.25 to 0.5 mph adds about 15 hits per 100 at 700 yards and 24 at 1,000 (see “Which uncertainty costs the most hits” above). It costs practice, not tools.

Three things stand out.

  • The cheapest steps that fix a velocity error pay most: holding the ammunition temperature and truing the chart.
  • Choosing between bullets and powders pays next. Fine changes to the charge or the case do not (see Chapter 16).
  • Per-round case prep returns about a hit per 100 at most, even if it works. Proving a small difference costs more than it can ever return, and the small-sample tuning methods return nothing.

Your numbers will differ. Inside about 300 yards for a typical centerfire rifle, velocity errors and velocity SD barely move the shot, so dispersion and the wind are all that count. A measure that throws wider than The Handloading Handbook‘s example makes weighing pay more. Work the table for your own load, target and distance.

Weapon Employment Zone analysis

WEZ analysis is the full version of this method. It runs a ballistic solver many times. Each run draws every input from its uncertainty, and each simulated shot either hits or misses.2

  1. Define the target’s shape and size. The results depend heavily on them.
  2. Set each uncertainty. One published table gives three confidence sets. Crosswind is ±1, ±2.5 or ±4 mph. Range is ±1, ±10 or ±50 meters. Rifle and ammunition precision is a group size of 0.5, 1.0 or 1.5 MOA, not a standard deviation. Velocity SD is 10, 15 or 20 fps.4 Read each group size as a 5-shot extreme spread. A 5-shot extreme spread averages about 3 standard deviations, so divide the group size by about 3 to use it as one sigma.
  3. Fly 1,000 shots at each range. Results then repeat within 1 or 2 percent.2
  4. Plot the hit percentage against range.
  5. Mark the transonic range. Show hit percentages beyond it in gray and read each one as somewhere between that number and zero.2
  6. Compare systems under the same uncertainty set. Comparison is the main use.

One example compares two bullets in one cartridge. Bullet A is heavy and slower, with a high BC. Bullet B is light and faster, with a lower BC. In a high-confidence set both hit 100 percent out to 800 yards, and beyond that A leads. A stays supersonic to 1,450 yards and B to 1,100. With large range errors, the faster and flatter B might be the better choice.2 The answer depends on the uncertainties.

The root-sum-square method above gives nearly the same answer as a simulation when the errors are roughly normal and modest in size. A spreadsheet can also run the simulation. Draw each error with a normal random function, such as NORM.INV(RAND(), 0, sigma). Add the errors for each simulated shot, score it as a hit or a miss and count.

Most precise up close, or hits farthest

These are different questions. A ranking by group size finds the load with the best average group. Hit chance at distance also depends on BC, velocity SD and how long the bullet stays supersonic. So the smallest 100-yard group can lose at 800 yards. The winner there may group slightly larger but have a higher BC, a tighter velocity SD or more supersonic margin.

To compare loads by hand, work each load’s hit chance at each distance. Use its own mean radius and velocity SD, with the same wind call and target for every load. Watch for the distance where the curves cross. Call a winner only where the gap is larger than the uncertainty in your inputs.
