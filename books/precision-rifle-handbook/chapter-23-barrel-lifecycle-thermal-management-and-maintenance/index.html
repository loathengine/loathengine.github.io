Chapter 23. Barrel Lifecycle, Thermal Management, and Maintenance

Key points

  • Hold the ammunition at a steady temperature when you fire it, and the velocity you recorded stays true.
  • A new lot can change pressure. Your velocity records, not an assumption that it matches, tell you whether it did.
  • The throat wears with every shot, so jump and velocity drift over a barrel’s life.
  • Every change to a component, the rifle or the season reopens a finished load.

Why it matters

A load is not finished when it wins. Seasons change. Powder lots run out. The throat wears a little with every shot. Each change moves velocity, pressure or both. The data you gathered stays true only if you keep checking it.

How it is done

Hold the ammunition temperature

Powders respond to temperature, and each load responds in its own way (see The Handloading Handbook). Measuring that response takes more control than a home range gives. A round warms or cools toward the air as soon as it leaves its box, and a warm chamber heats it further. What you can control is the temperature of each round when you fire it. Keep that temperature steady, and your recorded velocity holds true even if you do not know the powder’s exact temperature curve.

Safety · See Chapter 2

Hot ammunition runs at higher pressure. A vehicle in the sun, a pocket and a warm chamber all count toward the hottest your ammunition will see (see The Handloading Handbook). Never warm ammunition on purpose.

  1. Read the ammunition temperature at every session, with a thermometer in the ammunition box (see Chapter 7). Record it with the session (see Chapters 13 and 14).
  2. At the range, keep the ammunition in a closed, insulated box in the shade. Never leave it in the sun, on a dark mat or in a closed vehicle.
  3. In the field, carry the rounds in an inside pocket, or in a pack away from the sun. Body heat holds them far steadier than the air around you.
  4. Keep the action open until you are ready to fire. Chamber each round only then. In a hold or a cease-fire, unload.
  5. If a round sits in a warm chamber before it fires, note it on its shot line before you look at the target (see Chapter 14).
  6. Chronograph the load the way you carry it. A hunter’s drop chart comes from rounds carried in the pocket, and a competitor’s from rounds kept in the shaded box (see Chapter 20).
Evidence · Supported by data

A powder’s response to temperature depends on the cartridge, the lot and the rifle, and it is often not a straight line. In one measured powder the slope in the cold was about twice the slope in the heat (see The Handloading Handbook).1

Watch for heat

Some heat reaches the rounds however careful you are. Your velocity records show it, with no special test.

In a long string. Barrel and chamber heat climb through a long string, and each round that waits in a warm chamber takes up some of it. In any string of 15 shots or more, compare the mean velocity of the last five shots with the first five. Judge the gap against its noise.

noise = SD × √(2 ÷ 5)       SD = the string's velocity SD
A rise of more than twice the noise is heat reaching the rounds.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

A 20-shot string has a velocity SD of 10 fps. Its first five shots average 2,701 fps and its last five average 2,719 fps.

noise      = 10 × √(2 ÷ 5) = 6.3 fps
twice that = 12.6 fps
rise       = 2,719 − 2,701 = 18 fps

The rise is more than twice the noise, so heat is reaching the rounds (see Figure 23-1). Slow the pace, shorten the strings or keep the action open longer between shots. Fire load tests in interleaved 5-round blocks, so no load meets only the hottest barrel (see Chapter 14).

In a timed 20-shot match string. F-Class, High Power, and benchrest matches often require 20 record shots plus sighters inside a fixed clock, where you cannot stop for ten minutes to cool the barrel. Manage heat with three habits: fit a mirage shield (or band) over the barrel so rising heat waves do not bend your sight picture through the scope; open the bolt immediately after each shot so air moves through the chamber while the target is scored; and chamber each fresh round only when you are ready to break the shot, keeping chamber dwell time identical from shot 1 to shot 20.

On a hot day. When the ammunition is warmer than it was at the work-up, fire one round alone first and read its velocity. If it reaches your velocity ceiling, the load is too hot for that day. Stop and reduce the charge (see The Handloading Handbook). Any pressure sign means stop now.

On any day far from your records. On a day much hotter or colder than any in your records, compare the session’s mean velocity with the record nearest its ammunition temperature. Note the gap in the velocity history (see Chapter 13).

Over a season. The velocity history holds each session’s mean velocity and ammunition temperature. Once it spans cold and warm days, it shows how the load responds. Use sessions from one lot, fired after the barrel’s first 100 to 200 rounds, so lot changes and a new barrel’s speed-up stay out. Group the sessions into cold days and warm days and compare their averages.

slope = (warm mean − cold mean) ÷ (warm temperature − cold temperature)
        in fps per °F

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Four cold sessions average 2,712 fps with the ammunition at 34 °F. Four warm sessions average 2,748 fps at 78 °F.

slope = (2,748 − 2,712) ÷ (78 − 34) = 36 ÷ 44 = 0.82 fps per °F

Day-to-day changes in setup and barrel condition add extra scatter beyond single-session shot noise. Trust a calculated temperature slope only after you have several sessions at both cold and warm temperatures, and apply it only within the temperature span you have measured. Use a measured slope to increase the 1 fps per °F heat margin from The Handloading Handbook if needed, never to reduce it.

Ammunition and powder lot changes

Factory ammunition and smokeless powder are both manufactured in lots, and two lots of the same product can differ in velocity, pressure, or dispersion. Your chronograph and target records show whether a new lot matches the old.

Safety · See Chapter 2

A new component lot can change pressure, and a velocity check is your safeguard. In percentage terms, changing the powder charge moves pressure about 3 times as much as velocity, and swapping the primer can move pressure 10 times as much or more.2 A velocity rise at the same charge is a clear warning of higher pressure. Act on what you measure, and never assume a new lot matches the old.

Factory ammunition lot checks. Before buying a case or taking a new factory lot to a match or hunt:

  1. Record the new lot number from the box flap as a new batch in your log (see Chapter 13).
  2. Fire 10 check rounds of the new lot over a chronograph at 100 yards (or 50 yards for .22 LR), interleaved with 10 rounds of your old lot if you still have any (or compare against your velocity history at a similar ammunition temperature).
  3. Check mean velocity and velocity SD against your old lot using the noise rule below, and check point of impact against your zero. Update your ballistic solver’s muzzle velocity and re-confirm zero if the new lot shifted.
  4. Confirm precision with a 20- to 30-shot composite (see Chapter 16) before relying on the new lot at distance, especially in .22 LR rimfire where lot-to-lot differences can be large.

Handload powder and component lot checks.

  1. When you can, buy enough of one lot for the life of a load.
  2. Record the lot number with every load.
  3. Do not assume the new lot is different. Do not assume it is the same. Test it.
  4. Load new-lot and old-lot rounds at the same charge: your current charge, or one step lower if it sits at or near the published maximum. Load 10 rounds of each lot.
  5. Fire new-lot rounds interleaved with old-lot rounds in one session. If the old lot is gone, compare with the velocity in your records at that same charge, for the same rifle and similar conditions.
  6. Judge the difference against its noise: √(old SD² ÷ old shots + new SD² ÷ new shots), in fps. A difference under about twice the noise is not shown to be real. A larger one probably is (the barrel-wear example below works the same sum). With 10 shots of each lot and SDs of 9 and 10 fps, the noise is √(8.1 + 10.0) = 4.25 fps, and twice the noise is 8.5 fps. Example numbers. Not load data.
  7. If the new lot’s mean velocity exceeds the old lot’s by more than twice the noise from step 6, the velocity rise (and the pressure rise behind it) is real. Reduce the charge and work up again from below. If you tested one step below a near-maximum charge and saw no real velocity rise, step up to your working charge while respecting your velocity ceiling (see The Handloading Handbook). If velocity dropped by more than twice the noise, the new lot burns slower. If you adjust the charge upward to restore velocity, never exceed the published maximum charge or your velocity ceiling, whichever comes first.
  8. Confirm the new-lot load with 20 to 30 shots before you rely on it at long range.

Barrel wear, velocity drift and the lands

Every shot erodes the throat a little, and the lands move forward (see The Handloading Handbook). At a fixed CBTO the jump grows as far as the lands move.

Keep an accurate round count for the barrel. Re-measure the distance to the lands at regular intervals. Use the same tool and the same technique every time (see The Handloading Handbook).

Decide in advance how you will handle the moving lands. You can chase them, seating the bullet longer to keep the jump the same. Or you can keep a fixed CBTO and let the jump grow. A load developed in a wide, flat jump band usually tolerates moderate throat erosion without losing precision. If you seat longer to follow the lands, check that the longer round still fits the magazine and feeds reliably.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

At the rate measured in 6 and 6.5 mm barrels, .004 to .007 inch per 100 rounds (see The Handloading Handbook), the lands move .016 to .028 inch over 400 rounds. A load seated .020 inch off the lands at the start could then be .036 to .048 inch off. Overbore cartridges such as 7mm SAUM, 6.5 PRC, and belted magnums erode faster—commonly .010 to .015 inch per 100 rounds—so re-check the lands every 150 to 200 rounds on a barrel burner.

Velocity also drifts over a barrel’s life. Many new barrels speed up over their first 100 to 200 rounds. The rise varies from barrel to barrel, and one measured rise was about 80 fps.3 A load worked up to your velocity ceiling in a new barrel can pass it as the barrel speeds up. Through the first 100 to 200 rounds, check its velocity against the ceiling. If it reaches the ceiling, drop the charge and work up again (see The Handloading Handbook).

Later, as the throat erodes toward the end of the barrel’s accurate life, velocity usually falls.4 How fast it falls varies too. Track the mean velocity from session to session. Compare each session only with sessions fired at a similar ammunition temperature, and check the change against its noise before you blame wear.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Your recorded velocity is 2,744 fps from 30 shots, with the ammunition at 62 °F and an SD of 9 fps. The barrel has now fired 2,600 rounds. Today, with the ammunition held at 60 °F, 10 shots average 2,722 fps with an SD of 10 fps. Is the barrel slowing down?

noise = √( (9 ÷ √30)² + (10 ÷ √10)² )
      = √(1.64² + 3.16²) = 3.6 fps
drop  = 2,744 − 2,722 = 22 fps, about 6 times the noise

The ammunition was held within 2 °F of the record, so temperature cannot explain a 22 fps drop. The drop is real. Before you blame wear, rule out a new lot and the chronograph setup (see Chapter 24). Had today’s ammunition been 25 °F colder, this comparison would say nothing about wear. Compare such a day with a session near its own temperature instead (see Figure 23-2).

When to re-test

Re-test when one of these changes:

ChangeWhat to do
New lot of factory ammunitionLog the lot number, chronograph 10 check rounds against your previous lot or velocity history, verify zero and solver velocity, and confirm precision with 20 to 30 shots before buying in bulk or shooting a match (see “Ammunition and powder lot changes” above).
New primer, case maker, bullet or powderWork up again (see The Handloading Handbook). Confirm with 20 to 30 shots.
New lot of the same componentLoad 10 check rounds at your current charge, or one step lower if it sits at or near the published maximum. Compare their velocity with your records. If the new lot runs faster by more than twice the noise, lower the charge and work up again from below (see “Ammunition and powder lot changes” above). Load in bulk only after the check.
New scope, rings, stock or triggerStart a new baseline (see Chapters 4 and 14).
New barrel or a changed barrel lengthStart over: new lands, new baseline and a new work-up.

Lands moved out of your jump bandRe-seat and confirm with 20 to 30 shots.
Seating deeper than your worked-up seating, or into the landsWork up again from the start load at that seating (see The Handloading Handbook).
A new seasonRe-check your zero and drop chart (see Chapter 20).

Barrel break-in, fire-cracking, and velocity speed-up

A new barrel changes noticeably during its first 100 to 200 rounds. Microscopic reamer marks in the throat smooth down, early thermal fire-cracking begins just ahead of the chamber, and bore friction settles. As this happens, muzzle velocity commonly climbs by 20 to 80 fps over the first 100 to 200 rounds.3 Wait until the barrel passes this speed-up phase before you finalize a long-range drop chart or draw fine conclusions from load comparisons.

Before firing a new barrel for the first time, run solvent and dry patches through the bore and chamber to remove factory preservative oil and any stray machining debris. Oil left in a chamber increases bolt thrust and can cause erratic early velocities.

Safety · See Chapter 2

Confirm the rifle is unloaded and the chamber is empty before cleaning. Always wipe oil and solvent out of the bore and chamber before firing, and use chemical cleaners in a well-ventilated area.

Cleaning cadence, carbon rings, and copper fouling

Good cleaning protects precision. Poor cleaning damages barrels faster than shooting does.

  1. Use the right tools. Use a one-piece coated or polished stainless steel cleaning rod, never a jointed rod whose seams can scrape the rifling. Insert a snug bore guide in the action to align the rod with the bore and keep solvent out of the trigger and bedding. Use brass or nylon jags and brushes sized to your caliber, and push patches from chamber to muzzle whenever the action allows so you do not nick the crown.
  2. Manage carbon and copper separately. Every shot leaves powder carbon and trace jacket copper. A thin, uniform fouling layer often stabilizes velocity and point of impact. Heavy, uneven copper buildup can constrict the bore, raise pressure, and open groups. Use a carbon solvent for routine maintenance, and use a dedicated copper solvent only when bore inspection, velocity rise, or opening groups show copper is building up.
  3. Control the carbon ring. Hard carbon accumulates right at the front of the chamber neck (centerfire) or chamber mouth and leade (rimfire), especially in high-round-count centerfire match barrels and rimfire chambers. Left unchecked, a carbon ring pinches the case mouth or engraves the bullet unevenly on firing. That spikes chamber pressure, raises velocity SD, stiffens bolt closing or lift, and opens groups. Inspect the neck-throat junction or rimfire leade with a borescope if you have one, or clean that area deliberately with a carbon solvent and a properly sized nylon or bronze brush on a regular schedule.
  4. Keep a consistent cadence. Clean on a repeatable round-count schedule matched to your cartridge and measured results, such as every 150 to 300 rounds for many centerfire precision rifles, or sooner if groups open or velocity climbs. After every cleaning, fire and log your fouling shots before judging zero or group size (see Chapter 14). Rimfire (.22 LR) barrels rely on bullet-lube seasoning and are usually cleaned sparingly, focusing on the breech face, chamber, and carbon ring unless groups degrade.
  5. Semi-automatic / AR-15 maintenance. Gas-operated precision rifles accumulate fouling in the action as well as the bore. On a regular schedule, disassemble and clean the bolt carrier group, inspect the gas rings for wear (a properly ringed bolt should support the carrier’s weight when stood upright on the bolt face), wipe the bolt face and extractor, clean the firing pin channel dry, and swab the chamber locking lugs with a chamber brush and lug swabs so the bolt locks into battery consistently.

Switching .22 LR bullet waxes (3-step seasoning protocol). Different .22 LR makers coat lead bullets with incompatible lubricants (such as oil/wax blends versus beeswax/tallow). Mixing waxes inside the bore degrades groups until the old film clears. Whenever you switch .22 LR brands:

  1. Patch out the old wax. Push solvent-dampened patches followed by dry patches through the bore and chamber until the patches come out clean, and wipe wax buildup off the breech face and extractor.
  2. Fire 15 to 30 conditioning shots. Shoot 15 to 30 rounds of the new brand at a fouling aim mark so the bore coats evenly with the new lubricant.
  3. Zero and test only after the bore seasons. Confirm your 50-yard zero and begin recording test groups (see Chapter 14) only once velocity and impacts settle.

Contested barrel break-in rituals

The claim. Firing one shot and cleaning to bare metal for 5 to 10 shots, followed by 3-shot strings with cleaning between each, smooths the bore, reduces copper fouling, and extends barrel life.

The physics. Chamber reamers leave fine tool marks across the throat, perpendicular to bullet travel. Firing bullets smooths those marks, and cleaning between shots removes copper so the next bullet contacts steel directly. Hand-lapped match barrels have very few reamer marks to begin with, and hot propellant gas drives throat fire-cracking regardless of how often you clean.

The test. Compare fouling rate, velocity stability, and 30-shot composite mean radius between matched barrels broken in with a shoot-and-clean ritual and barrels simply cleaned before first firing and shot normally.

The data. Controlled large-sample tests are scarce, and barrel makers themselves disagree. Some makers suggest a brief shoot-and-clean sequence for the throat, while others state that cleaning before first firing and shooting normally works just as well.5 Neither approach stops the normal 100- to 200-round velocity speed-up.3

The verdict. Plausible but unproven. Clean a new barrel thoroughly before its first shot to remove oil and debris. Beyond that, an elaborate 50-round clean-after-every-shot ritual has no proven precision or barrel-life payoff. Follow the barrel maker’s basic guidance if you choose, or simply shoot the rifle and clean on a steady cadence.

Contested cleaning to bare metal every session

The claim. Scrubbing every trace of carbon and copper down to bare steel after every range trip keeps a precision rifle at its best.

The physics. Bare steel has different friction than a fouled bore. After stripping to bare metal, muzzle velocity and point of impact often shift for 5 to 20 fouling shots until a steady layer of carbon and copper returns. Frequent heavy brushing or abrasive bore pastes also risk mechanical wear at the throat and crown.

The test. Track cold-bore point of impact, velocity SD, and 20- to 30-shot composite mean radius across sessions cleaned to bare metal every trip versus sessions cleaned on a measured round-count cadence while keeping the carbon ring clear.

The data. Competitive shooters and range logs routinely show a cold-clean velocity dip and point-of-impact shift after bare-metal cleaning, followed by stable velocity and tight groups for hundreds of rounds until heavy carbon or copper buildup degrades performance.

The verdict. Plausible but unproven. Stripping a barrel to bare metal after every short session spends barrel life and ammunition re-fouling the bore with no proven gain on target. Clean on a consistent round-count cadence guided by your target and velocity records, keep the carbon ring clear, and strip copper only when your data call for it.
