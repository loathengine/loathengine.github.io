Chapter 12. Evaluating and Selecting a Cartridge

On this page (7 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • The six-factor evaluation framework

  • Practical cartridge classes

Key points

  • Evaluate cartridges objectively across six dimensions: ballistic coefficient, muzzle velocity, recoil, supersonic reach, barrel life, and cost.
  • At extended distance, a higher-BC bullet fired slightly slower almost always drifts less in wind than a lighter, faster bullet.
  • Recoil limits practical accuracy by preventing self-spotting and inducing fatigue over long strings.
  • Test factory match ammunition with the same statistical rigor as handloads; if it falls short, either test another lot or take the data to the reloading workshop.

Why it matters

Choosing a cartridge by marketing velocity or forum folklore locks you into excess recoil, short barrel life, or poor wind performance. An objective framework matches the cartridge to your actual targets and distances.

How it is done

The six-factor evaluation framework

Every cartridge trades one trait for another. A flat-shooting magnum extends supersonic reach, but it burns throats quickly and kicks hard enough to hide bullet impact in the scope. A small-bore trainer costs little to shoot and lasts for thousands of rounds, but crosswinds push it off target early. Judging a cartridge across six measurable factors keeps those trade-offs in plain view.

  1. Ballistic coefficient (BC). Higher BC means less aerodynamic drag (see Chapter 19). Less drag means less wind drift and slower velocity loss downrange. Because wind call error dominates misses past about 500 yards (see Chapter 21), BC is usually the single strongest ballistic lever at distance.
  2. Muzzle velocity. Higher launch speed flattens the trajectory and reduces time of flight. Flatter trajectories forgive range-estimation errors, which matters in field hunting and unknown-distance shooting. Velocity also raises impact velocity for terminal expansion (see Chapter 22).
  3. Recoil and self-spotting. Free recoil energy rises with bullet weight, powder charge weight, and muzzle velocity, and falls as rifle weight increases. Heavy recoil causes shooter fatigue over long strings and lifts the muzzle off the target. If the scope jumps off the plate, you cannot watch your own trace or splash to make a fast wind correction. In practical rifle competition, being able to spot your own misses often adds more hits than a small gain in BC.
  4. Supersonic reach. Standard ballistic predictions lose accuracy as a bullet slows into the transonic zone near Mach 1.2, roughly 1,340 fps on a standard day (see Chapter 19).1 Some bullets also lose dynamic stability there.2 Choose a cartridge whose bullet stays above Mach 1.2 past your longest intended target distance, with margin for cold, dense air.
  5. Barrel life. High powder charges forced through a small bore diameter erode the throat rapidly. An overbore cartridge can lose match-grade precision in 1,000 to 1,500 rounds, while a mild .308 Winchester or .223 Remington barrel can shoot well past 4,000 to 5,000 rounds. Barrel replacement is a real part of the cost per shot.
  6. Cost per round and availability. Count the full cost of components or factory ammunition, plus barrel replacement divided by barrel life. A cartridge that costs half as much to feed lets you shoot twice as much practice. Practice reading wind and building steady positions usually buys far more hits than a marginal ballistic upgrade (see Chapter 21).
Safety · See Chapter 2

Always verify that the cartridge headstamp matches the chamber marking stamped on the barrel before loading a magazine or chambering a round. Several modern precision cartridges share similar case bodies and can chamber in the wrong rifle with dangerous results.

Ballistic coefficient vs. muzzle velocity

Shooters often equate high muzzle velocity with low wind drift. Physics disagrees. Wind drift depends on lag time, which is the extra time aerodynamic drag adds to the flight compared with a vacuum (see Chapter 19). A light bullet launched fast sheds speed quickly, builds lag time, and drifts heavily. A heavier, sleeker bullet launched slower holds its speed, builds less lag time, and drifts less.

The 55 gr .223 Remington leaves the muzzle 540 fps faster than the 6.5 Creedmoor. By 300 yards, it already drifts more than twice as far in the same wind. At 1,000 yards, it drifts nearly three times as far. Even within the same cartridge, stepping up to a heavy 77 gr .223 Remington match bullet cuts 1,000-yard wind drift by nearly 60 inches despite starting 520 fps slower. The .308 Winchester, starting 640 fps slower than the 55 gr .223 Remington, cuts wind drift almost in half.

Between the .308 Winchester and the 6.5 Creedmoor, a 100 fps velocity edge combines with a 30 percent higher G7 BC to cut 1,000-yard wind drift by about 36 percent.

Higher muzzle velocity still has one clear advantage: less drop per yard of range error. When you range targets with a laser rangefinder or shoot at known distances, range error is small and BC wins. When you estimate distance by eye in the field, a flatter trajectory reduces vertical misses (see Chapter 21).2

Evidence · Supported by data

Wind-drift physics and Weapon Employment Zone modeling show that higher BC beats higher muzzle velocity whenever wind call error exceeds range-estimation error.4

Practical cartridge classes

Most precision rifles fall into four broad classes. Pick the mildest class that meets your supersonic reach, wind, and terminal energy requirements.

Rimfire precision and trainers (NRL22, PRS Rimfire, smallbore). Cartridges such as .22 Long Rifle (.22 LR), .17 Hornady Magnum Rimfire (.17 HMR), and .22 Winchester Magnum Rimfire (.22 WMR) offer the lowest cost per round, almost no recoil, and barrel life measured in tens of thousands of rounds. Standard-velocity .22 LR launches subsonic at roughly 1,050 to 1,100 fps with a low BC. At 100 to 300 yards, its drop and wind drift mimic a centerfire rifle at 600 to 1,000 yards. That makes .22 LR both a competitive precision discipline of its own and an efficient trainer for positional shooting, trigger control, and wind reading on short ranges. The faster .17 HMR and .22 WMR shoot flatter inside 150 yards for small game, though match-grade ammunition selection is broadest in .22 LR.

Small-bore centerfire. The .223 Remington (and 5.56x45mm NATO in rifles chambered for it) bridges rimfire training and full-size match cartridges. Recoil is light, components are widely available, and barrels last thousands of rounds. In a fast-twist barrel (1:7 or 1:8), heavy 69 to 80+ gr match bullets stay supersonic past 800 yards and serve well in Service Rifle competition, varmint work, and high-volume practice. Wind drift remains higher than in 6mm or 6.5mm cartridges, so it teaches disciplined wind calling without punishing your shoulder or budget.

Low-recoil match and mid-range. The 6mm class includes 6mm Benchrest (6mm BR), 6mm GT, 6mm Creedmoor, and the classic .243 Winchester (6mm-08 class). The 6.5mm class includes 6.5 Creedmoor and 6.5x47 Lapua. Together they dominate practical precision matches, mid-range target shooting, and deer-sized game hunting. High-BC bullets in 105 to 115 gr (6mm) and 130 to 147 gr (6.5mm) stay supersonic past 1,000 yards while producing mild recoil in a heavy match rifle. Shooters can spot their own impacts through the scope and stay fresh across a two-day match. Inside this class, the main trade-off is barrel life versus speed. Efficient short cases like 6mm BR and 6mm GT offer longer barrel life than faster 6mm Creedmoor or .243 Winchester loads. The 6.5 Creedmoor trades slightly more recoil for longer throat life and broad factory match ammunition support.

Extended-range and hunting. The .270 and 7mm classes include .270 Winchester, 7mm-08 Remington, 7mm Remington Short Action Ultra Magnum (7mm SAUM), and 7mm Precision Rifle Cartridge (7mm PRC). The .30-caliber class includes .308 Winchester, .30-06 Springfield, .300 Winchester Magnum, and .300 Precision Rifle Cartridge (.300 PRC). These cartridges serve F-Class long-range competition, extreme long-range shooting, and big-game hunting. Heavy 7mm and .30-caliber bullets carry high BCs. They retain enough impact velocity and energy for ethical hunting at longer distances (see Chapter 22), or remain supersonic past 1,500 yards on steel. The milder .308 Winchester, 7mm-08 Remington, .270 Winchester, and .30-06 Springfield offer long barrel life and manageable recoil inside 800 to 1,000 yards (and flat hunting trajectories inside 400 to 500 yards). The magnum cartridges add supersonic reach and terminal energy at the cost of heavier recoil, higher ammunition expense, and shorter barrel life.

Factory match ammunition vs. handloads

You do not have to handload to shoot a precision rifle well. Modern factory match ammunition is loaded with consistent bullets and powders, and in popular chamberings (.22 LR, .223 Remington, 6.5 Creedmoor, .308 Winchester, and 6mm Creedmoor) it often delivers tight groups and low velocity SD straight from the box. Specialty match chamberings like 6mm GT and 6mm BR have far fewer factory SKUs than 6.5 Creedmoor, making lot testing and buying in bulk especially important if you do not handload.

Test factory ammunition with the same statistical discipline you would use for handloads (see Chapters 14 through 16).

  1. Test by lot number. Record the lot number printed on every box (see Chapter 13). Two lots of the same factory SKU can differ in muzzle velocity and group size, especially in .22 LR where rimfire ammunition cannot be handloaded and lot testing is the main tuning lever.
  2. Use real sample sizes. Do not buy a case of ammunition because one 3-shot or 5-shot group looked tight. Screen two or three candidate SKUs or lots with 10 rounds each, then confirm the leader with a 20- to 30-shot composite and chronograph string (see Chapter 16).
  3. Judge against your hit-probability goal. Enter the factory load’s measured mean radius and velocity SD into a Weapon Employment Zone model for your real target and distance (see Chapter 21). If the load clears your hit-probability goal, buy enough of that lot for the season and spend your time practicing.
  4. Know when to move to the workshop. Factory ammunition falls short when no available lot meets your precision or velocity SD goal, when you shoot a cartridge with limited factory match support, or when high-volume shooting makes handloading more economical. When a handload falls short during range evaluation, do not guess at the range. Take your measured mean radius, velocity SD, and target diagnostics back to the reloading workshop (see The Handloading Handbook) and change a major variable such as bullet or powder.
