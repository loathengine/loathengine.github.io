Chapter 20. Ballistic Solvers, Truing, and DOPE

Key points

  • In the supersonic part of the flight, drop error is mostly velocity error. So velocity comes before drag.
  • Velocity and drag each have their own truing range: supersonic for velocity, near the transonic zone for drag. A large adjustment points to a wrong input, not a better fit.
  • A zero error is an angle, so it grows with range. A tenth of an inch at 100 yards becomes about an inch at 1,000.
  • Maximum point blank range picks the zero that keeps the bullet inside a vital zone for the longest span.
  • You can set a far zero at a short range by aiming for a computed offset.

Why it matters

A zero that is dead on for a .30-30 at 100 yards is good enough there (see Chapter 1). At long range, every small error in the zero grows with distance. The task is to get the zero, and everything built on it, right enough for that.

A DOPE card (data on previous engagements) turns a trajectory into numbers you dial or hold. It is only as good as its inputs. A wrong velocity, sight height or zero gives a card that looks precise and still misses.

The chart comes from a ballistic solver, a program that works out the trajectory from your inputs. Working a full trajectory by hand is not practical.

Setting up a ballistic solver follows a common order of work. The inputs come first, then muzzle velocity, then drag, within limits.1 Many apparent errors in ballistic predictions are really errors in how the scope adjusts.2

How it is done

Building the card takes six steps.

  1. Gather the inputs.
  2. Chronograph 10 or more shots for the muzzle velocity.
  3. Zero the rifle and measure the zero offset.
  4. Enter it all in the solver.
  5. Check the chart, or true it, at your longest distance.
  6. Print the card (see “The DOPE card and the field”).

Gather the inputs

Choose a solver that takes every input in the table below, including the drag model and the pressure type. It should let you true the velocity and scale the drag near Mach 0.9. At each distance, it should give the elevation, the wind per mph, the velocity and the time of flight.

A card trued at long range absorbs that far error, so a wrong sight height then shows up close.3 Sight height is usually 1.5 to 2 inches on a bolt-action rifle and more on an AR-pattern rifle.3 Measure yours (see Chapter 4).

Muzzle velocity first

In the supersonic part of the flight, drop depends mostly on muzzle velocity. Short-range drops are poor at revealing it. In one published example, half a MOA of drop error at 200 yards matches about 180 fps of velocity error. At 900 yards the same half MOA matches about 15 fps.4

  1. Chronograph a string of 10 or more shots of the load as you will shoot it. Prefer a Doppler unit (see Chapter 7). Use the mean. Even a 10-shot mean carries its own error (see Chapter 23).
  2. Check whether your chronograph reports true muzzle velocity or velocity at the sensor. An optical unit placed 15 feet downrange reads the velocity at 15 feet. For bullets with a G1 BC of .400 or higher, the bullet loses about 7 to 11 fps over that first 15 feet (less as BC rises).5 Add that loss back if your chronograph does not correct to the muzzle automatically.
  3. Record the powder temperature with the velocity and print it on the card. Muzzle velocity changes with it, so hold the ammunition at that temperature when you shoot (see Chapter 23). On a day you cannot, true the card on observed drops (see “Truing on observed drops” below). If your records give the load a slope, you can correct the velocity with it, within the temperatures those records cover:
velocity today = card velocity
                 + slope × (powder temperature today − card temperature)
  1. Enter the velocity. Do not adjust drag yet.

One view prefers a measured velocity to one worked back from drops, because a wrong velocity also puts the drag at the wrong Mach number.5 Another view favors calibrating velocity on observed drops, because chronographs are not always as accurate as hoped.2 Both views fit one order. Measure velocity well, then true it on drops.

Zero and zero offset

Small zero errors grow with range. A group center .1 inch off at 100 yards puts the impact about 1 inch off at 1,000 yards. An error of .5 inch becomes about 5 inches.5

In short, zero this way.5

  1. Measure the distance to the zero target. If the turrets were dialed off zero for a test (see Chapter 14), set them back first.
  2. If your first shots land more than a click or two from the aim mark, click them close.
  3. Fire a 5-shot group without moving the aim or the turrets. For long-range work, fire two or three 5-shot groups and use their combined center (see Chapter 17).
  4. Measure the distance from the aim mark to the group center to .1 inch.
  5. Give that offset to your solver instead of chasing it with clicks.

A click is coarse. A 1/4 MOA click is about .26 inch at 100 yards. A mil, or milliradian, is 3.6 inches at 100 yards. That is about 3.4 MOA, so a 0.1 mil click is about .36 inch. A measured offset is finer than any click.

If you shoot a second load in the same rifle, build a separate chart for it. Fire its zero group with the scope turrets left at your primary load’s zero, then enter the new load’s measured X and Y offset into its chart. If you use a longer zero for hunting (such as a maximum point blank range zero), note the click adjustment from your primary zero on that card. For a hunting load, also record where the first shot from a cold barrel lands, with the bore clean or fouled to match how you carry the rifle afield, across several separate days.

Truing on observed drops

Truing makes the chart match where your bullets actually land. Do it in order.

Rule out input errors first. Before you change the drag, check every input in the table above.5 Then check four that show only at the range: your aim, the parallax setting, cant and the wind. Set the parallax so the reticle stays on the target when you move your head. Level the reticle to a plumb line when you mount the scope, and use a scope level for every shot (see Chapter 7). Cant moves the shot sideways by the drop below the bore line times the sine of the cant, about 1.7 inches per degree for each 100 inches of drop.

Check the turret. In one example, a scope dialed 40 MOA moves the impact only 38.5 MOA, so every dialed solution falls short.2 Test yours on a tall target at 100 yards. The dialed group lands about 1.05 inches higher for each MOA, so 40 MOA needs about 42 inches above the aim mark. Hang a plumb line down the target, or draw a line checked with a level, and put the aim mark near its bottom.2

  1. Measure the distance to the target to within a foot at 100 yards, since each yard off is 1 percent.
  2. Fire 5 shots at the aim mark.
  3. Dial at least the largest correction on your card and fire 5 more at the same aim mark.
  4. Dial back and check that the group returns to the zero.
  5. Measure the distance between the two group centers along the line and convert it to MOA or mils at that distance.
  6. Divide the measured move by the dialed amount to get the tracking factor. Example numbers. Not load data. For the scope above, 38.5 ÷ 40 = 0.9625.
  7. Dial each correction divided by the tracking factor.

Then true. Work through these steps in order.

  1. Build the chart with measured inputs, including your latitude and firing direction. Note the ranges where the bullet slows to Mach 1.2 and Mach 0.9. Leaving out the Earth’s rotation skews the truing. For example, a .30-caliber bullet fired east at 1,000 yards in North Texas sees nearly 3 inches of vertical Coriolis. A chart trued without it while firing east is then about 6 inches off when you fire west.6
  2. Shoot a group at a range where the bullet is still above Mach 1.2 and the drop is large. The drop should be at least 20 MOA (about 6 mils).2 Use 5 to 10 shots so you can locate the group center. If no distance you can shoot reaches 20 MOA, keep the measured velocity and check the chart at your longest distance. Change nothing for a miss the group center’s own error can explain (see step 5).
  3. Work out the observed drop. Take the chart’s elevation for that range. Convert how far the group center printed low or high to MOA or mils at that range. Add it if low, subtract it if high.
  4. Change the velocity in your solver until the chart’s drop at that range equals the observed drop.
  5. Compare the trued velocity with your chronograph average. If the difference falls within the group center’s normal wander, it is just shot noise, so keep your chronograph velocity (see the worked example below). If the difference is larger than group wander but within your chronograph’s accuracy tolerance (see Chapter 7), confirm it with a second group and adopt the trued velocity. If the difference exceeds what chronograph error could explain, another input is wrong; re-check the table above before changing your chart.
  6. If you shoot past the Mach 1.2 range, shoot a group near the Mach 0.9 range. Scale the drag at that Mach number only and leave the supersonic part alone. A single trued BC can fit on a warm day and miss on a cold one.5
  7. Keep the drag change small. Barrel differences usually fit within a drag factor of 0.95 to 1.05.5 A factor near or beyond 0.90 or 1.10 means another input is wrong.
  8. Log each truing with its range, Mach number, air and result. Keep the log with the rifle and load records (see Chapter 13).

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Take a 140 gr 6.5 Creedmoor load at 2,700 fps with a G7 BC of .315, on a standard day with a 100-yard zero. At 1,000 yards its chart calls for 31.37 MOA, well over 20 MOA. The bullet arrives at about 1,440 fps, still above Mach 1.2.

Your group at 1,000 yards centers 4 inches below the aim mark. At 1,000 yards 1 MOA is 10.47 inches, so the group is 0.38 MOA low. Add 0.38 MOA to the chart’s 31.37 MOA. That total, 31.75 MOA, is the observed drop to true against.

For a quick sense of size, 10 fps moves this load’s 1,000-yard drop about 2.83 inches on a chart that stays zeroed at 100 yards. So a 4-inch miss low suggests a velocity about 14 fps slower than the chart assumes (4 ÷ 2.83 × 10). The group center has its own error, about the vertical sigma divided by the square root of the shots (see Chapter 17). Here, 0.35 MOA of rifle dispersion and a 10 fps velocity SD give a vertical sigma of about 4.75 inches at 1,000 yards. A 10-shot center is then good to about 1.5 inches, or about 5 fps. So true on two or more groups before you trust the change.

Evidence · Supported by data

Drop errors in the supersonic band are mostly velocity errors.4 Doppler-measured drag curves depart from the standards mostly in the transonic band.2

Subsonic and rimfire (.22 LR) truing

The supersonic-first truing sequence above assumes the bullet starts well above Mach 1.2. Standard-velocity .22 LR leaves the muzzle near Mach 0.95 (about 1,050 to 1,090 fps) and never flies above Mach 1.2, so you cannot separate velocity from drag using supersonic and transonic ranges.

Rimfire zero geometry also magnifies small setup errors. Most precision .22 LR rifles use a 50-yard zero rather than a 100-yard zero. Halving the zero distance doubles both sight-height and zero-offset sensitivity downrange: a 0.1-inch zero offset at 50 yards becomes a 0.6-inch error at 300 yards—twice the angular error of a 0.1-inch offset at 100 yards.

True a subsonic .22 LR solver in four steps:

  1. Lock down the 50-yard zero and exact sight height. Confirm turret tracking first, measure bore-to-scope sight height carefully (see Chapter 4), and shoot two or three 5-shot groups at a measured 50 yards to establish a clean zero offset.
  2. Chronograph 10 to 20 shots with a Doppler unit. Measure the lot’s mean muzzle velocity and record the air and ammunition temperature. Because .22 LR velocities change modestly with temperature and lot, keep the chronograph data tied to that lot number (see Chapter 13).
  3. Verify muzzle velocity at 100 to 150 yards. Fire a 5- to 10-shot group at 100 to 150 yards and check observed drop against the solver. Make small muzzle-velocity adjustments here only if the observed drop exceeds group-center wander and zero/sight-height inputs are verified.
  4. True drag or BC scale at 200 to 300 yards. Use the RA4 drag model or a custom Doppler drag curve (CDM) for your ammunition rather than a single G1 BC (see Chapter 19). Fire a 10-shot group at 200 to 300 yards and adjust the drag scale or BC until predicted drop matches observed drop across the full rimfire distance span.

Maximum point blank range

Maximum point blank range (MPBR) is the longest span over which you can aim dead on. Over that span the bullet stays within half a vital zone above or below the line of sight.

  1. Choose the vital zone’s diameter for your target.
  2. Make a path table at fine steps, such as every 5 or 10 yards.
  3. Change the zero until the highest point of the path, the apex, sits half the vital zone above the line of sight.
  4. Find where the path falls to half the vital zone below the line of sight. That distance is the MPBR.
  5. Note the two places where the path crosses the line of sight. These are the near zero and the far zero. Zero the rifle at the far zero, or at a shorter range with the computed offset (see “Zeroing at a distance you cannot shoot” below). The near zero is so close to the muzzle that any error in it grows many times over by the far zero.

Inside the MPBR, aim at the center (see Figure 20-2). Beyond it, dial. The size of the vital zone drives the answer more than any other input. The chart holds only with the rifle zeroed for its far zero.

Game classTypical vital-zone diameterHalf-band (± above/below line of sight)
Varmint and small predator4 to 6 inches±2 to ±3 inches
Deer and pronghorn8 inches±4 inches
Elk and large big game10 to 12 inches±5 to ±6 inches

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Take a .243 Winchester (6mm-08 class) 95 gr hunting bullet (G1 BC .480) at 3,000 fps with a 1.5-inch sight height and an 8-inch deer vital zone (±4.0 inches). Setting the far zero at 270 yards puts the near zero near 24 yards, places the apex at +4.0 inches near 150 yards, and keeps the path at +3.3 inches at 100 yards, +3.3 inches at 200 yards, and −2.4 inches at 300 yards. The bullet crosses −4.0 inches at about 317 yards, which is the MPBR. Aiming dead center on the vitals keeps the bullet inside the 8-inch zone from the muzzle out to roughly 317 yards without touching a turret—and you can establish that 270-yard zero on a 100-yard range by centering your group 3.3 inches high.

Zeroing at a distance you cannot shoot

Your range may stop at 100 yards while your chart wants a 200-yard zero or an MPBR far zero. You can zero at the short range by aiming for a computed offset.

A path table for one zero converts to a table for another.

New path at dist = old path at dist
                   − (dist ÷ new zero) × old path at new zero

dist       the distance you can shoot
new zero   the zero distance you want
old path   your current path table, inches above (+) or below (−)
           the line of sight

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. For a 100-yard zero shooting 4.1 inches low at 200 yards, wanting a 200-yard zero on a 100-yard range gives: new path at 100 yards = 0 − (100 ÷ 200) × (−4.1) = +2.05 inches (see Figure 20-3). Center the group about 2.1 inches high—about 2.0 MOA (8 clicks of 1/4 MOA) or 0.57 mil (6 clicks of 0.1 mil).

At the range:

  1. Build the chart using measured velocity, BC, air, and sight height—at short range, sight height can shift the offset by over an inch.7
  2. Read the short-range offset (or compute it above) and print a target with aim and impact marks that far apart at actual size, never “fit to page” (see Figure 20-4); verify scale with a ruler before shooting.
  3. Fire a 5-shot group at the aim mark and adjust until group center sits as close to the impact mark as your clicks allow.
  4. Measure any residual offset to .1 inch, scale it to the true zero distance (double it for a 200-yard zero shot at 100 yards), enter it as your solver’s zero offset, and confirm at the real distance when able.

The DOPE card and the field

A DOPE card (see Figure 20-5) should list the air and powder temperature it was built for, with rows at regular or known stage distances showing elevation (in turret units and clicks), wind holds (often built at 10 mph from 3 o’clock and scaled proportionally), and remaining velocity to flag the transonic zone.

Two cautions: with right-hand twist, a card built for a 3 o’clock wind under-holds elevation in a 9 o’clock wind due to aerodynamic jump (see Chapter 19); and because MOA and mil values differ by ~3.4×, dialing the wrong unit causes immediate misses.

Field shots add three factors:

  • Angle. Multiplying slant range by cos(slope angle) stays within ~0.1–0.2 MIL under 20–25° and 400–500 yards (Chapter 11), but errors grow beyond that—enter look angle directly in a solver for steeper or longer shots.5
  • Movers. Lead (feet) = crossing speed (fps) × TOF. Example numbers. Not load data: a target walking at 3 mph (4.4 fps) at 500 yards with 0.7 s TOF needs ~3 feet of lead.
  • Wind per target. Compute wind angle along each target’s own line of fire.
