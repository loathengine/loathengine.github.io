Chapter 10. Zeroing, Spotting, and Shot Calling

On this page (7 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • Bore-sighting and mechanical zero
  • Statistical zeroing
  • Contested: cold-bore shift
  • Spotting and shot calling
  • Notes

Key points

  • A three-shot zero leaves large mean-point-of-impact uncertainty; verify zero with 5 to 10 shots.
  • Distinguish cold-bore shots from cold-clean-bore shots and measure any shift with repeated sessions.
  • Calling your own shot before impact separates shooter error from wind or ammunition error.
  • Give spotter corrections in angular units (MIL or MOA), never linear inches.

Why it matters

Every ballistic table and drop chart assumes your zero is centered on the aim point at your zero distance. An off-center zero does not average out downrange. A 0.3 MIL zero error at 100 yards remains a 0.3 MIL bias at 600 yards and 1,000 yards, shifting your entire group off the edge of the target before wind or drag even act.

How it is done

Zeroing is a two-step job: get on paper mechanically, then center the mean point of impact statistically so you do not chase random noise.

Bore-sighting and mechanical zero

Bore-sighting aligns the scope closely enough to the bore that your first live shot lands on paper.

Safety · See Chapter 2

Confirm the rifle is completely unloaded before looking through the bore or inserting any bore-sighting tool. If you use a muzzle-mounted laser or optical bore-sighter, remove the arbor from the muzzle before chambering any ammunition. Firing a round with a bore-sighter left in the barrel causes a bore obstruction and catastrophic barrel damage.

Optical bore-sighting (bolt-action rifles). Place the rifle firmly in sandbags or a rest at the range, pointed at a large target at 50 or 100 yards. Remove the bolt. Look straight through the rear of the receiver and down the bore, shifting the bags until the target sits centered in the circular opening of the muzzle. Without bumping the rifle, look through the scope and dial the elevation and windage turrets until the reticle centers on the same target. Reinstall the bolt.

Semi-automatic rifles. Where the receiver design prevents looking straight through the bore, use a chamber-inserted laser cartridge, a muzzle collimator (removed immediately after alignment), or fire your first check shot at 25 yards on a large paper backer. Confirm impact, adjust onto the bullseye at 25 yards, and move back to your true zero distance (commonly 100 yards for centerfire rifles and 50 yards for .22 LR rimfire).

Setting mechanical zero and zero stops. Do not slip your turrets to “0” after a single three-shot group. First verify your zero with 5 to 10 shots (see “Statistical zeroing” below). Once the mean point of impact is centered on the aim point:

  1. Follow the scope maker’s instructions to loosen the turret cap screws or lift the locking cap.
  2. Rotate the outer turret dial so the “0” mark lines up cleanly with the index line on the scope tube, without turning the internal adjustment shaft.
  3. Set the mechanical zero stop if your elevation turret has one. Most shooters set the zero stop 0.2 to 0.5 MIL (or 1 to 2 MOA) below true zero. Leaving that small margin below zero lets you dial slightly downward if cold air, a new ammunition lot, or a closer target requires a minor negative correction, while still stopping you reliably near zero when dialing down in the dark or under time pressure.
  4. Tighten the turret screws to the maker’s torque specification and fire two confirmation shots to verify nothing slipped. Record your zero distance, sight height, and environmental conditions (see Chapters 4, 6, and 13).

Service Rifle 200-yard zero and two-load offsets. Highpower Across-the-Course Service Rifle competitors commonly zero at 200 yards (the closest match stage) and record verified elevation come-ups for 300 yards and 600 yards rather than zeroing at 100 yards. Because many shooters run magazine-length 77 gr match ammunition at 200 and 300 yards and switch to longer, single-loaded 80 gr match ammunition at 600 yards (see Chapter 4), verify and record the separate elevation and windage zero offset between the two loads instead of assuming they share the same 200-yard zero.

Statistical zeroing

Every rifle and load shoots a pattern of random scatter around its true center, called the mean point of impact (MPOI). Because individual shots land randomly inside that cone of dispersion, the average center of a small group rarely matches the true center of the rifle.

The uncertainty of a sample’s mean point of impact on either the horizontal or vertical axis depends on the rifle’s one-axis standard deviation (sigma) and the number of shots (n):

SE_MPOI = sigma / sqrt(n)

Here SE_MPOI is the standard error of the mean point of impact on one axis (in MIL or MOA), sigma is the true one-axis standard deviation of the rifle-and-ammunition system, and n is the number of shots fired. For a rough 95 percent confidence band on each axis, multiply SE_MPOI by 2 (+/- 2 * sigma / sqrt(n)).

Shots (n)Multiplier (1 / sqrt(n))95% axis band (+/- 2 / sqrt(n))Practical use
30.58+/- 1.15 * sigmaRough bore-sight check only; too noisy for final zero
50.45+/- 0.89 * sigmaMinimum check; pair two 5-shot groups for final zero
100.32+/- 0.63 * sigmaReliable zero verification for precision rifles
200.22+/- 0.45 * sigmaHigh-confidence baseline zero (see Chapter 15)

Example numbers. Suppose a rifle averages about 1.0 MOA extreme spread across 5-shot groups, which corresponds roughly to a one-axis standard deviation of sigma = 0.30 MOA (about 0.09 MIL).

  • After 3 shots, SE_MPOI = 0.30 / sqrt(3) = 0.30 / 1.732 = 0.17 MOA. The 95 percent uncertainty band on each axis is about +/- 0.34 MOA. Your 3-shot group center can easily print a third of an MOA high or left purely by chance, even when the true zero is dead center.
  • After 5 shots, SE_MPOI = 0.30 / sqrt(5) = 0.30 / 2.236 = 0.13 MOA, giving a 95 percent band of +/- 0.27 MOA.
  • After 10 shots, SE_MPOI = 0.30 / sqrt(10) = 0.30 / 3.162 = 0.095 MOA, cutting the 95 percent band down to +/- 0.19 MOA.

Chasing every 3-shot group with turret clicks is called chasing your zero. When you click 0.25 MOA right to fix a random 3-shot offset, you often push your true zero 0.25 MOA off center. On the next trip, another 3-shot group prints right, and you click back left.

When a shift is real versus noise. Verify a final zero with a 5-to-10 shot group, or two 5-shot groups fired at a steady pace so barrel heat stays controlled (see Chapter 14). Measure the exact horizontal and vertical offset from your aim point to the center of the group. Adjust your turrets only when the measured offset is larger than roughly 2 * SE_MPOI (or clearly exceeds one turret click plus normal scatter). If a 5-shot confirmation group sits within 0.1 MIL or 0.25 MOA of center on a 1.0 MOA rifle, leave the turrets alone and verify again on your next range trip.

Evidence · Supported by data

Small-sample groups produce wide random swings in both apparent group size and apparent point of impact. Using 5 to 10 shots (or pooled groups across sessions) reduces mean-point-of-impact uncertainty by the square root of the sample size.1

Contested cold-bore shift

The claim. The first shot fired from a cold barrel strikes away from the rest of the group, requiring a dedicated cold-bore hold or turret offset in your shooting log.

The physics. Two very different bore conditions are often lumped together under one name. A cold-clean-bore shot is fired through a freshly scrubbed barrel stripped of copper and carbon fouling, often with trace amounts of cleaning solvent or oil left in the grooves. Stripping the bore changes friction, engraving resistance, and muzzle velocity until the first one to five shots lay down a fresh fouling layer (see Chapter 23). A cold-fouled-bore shot is fired through an ambient-temperature barrel that already carries normal fouling from previous shooting. Between shot 1 and shot 2 in a fouled barrel, bore friction barely changes, though a small thermal step, stock-bedding pressure, or the shooter settling into position on the first shot can still move impact in some rifles.

The test. Separate the two conditions completely. To test a cold-fouled bore, leave the bore fouled and fire one cold shot at a dedicated 100-yard bullseye across 5 to 10 separate range trips, allowing the rifle to cool fully to ambient air temperature between shots. Record each cold shot’s horizontal and vertical offset from your established 10-shot zero. Run a second, separate test where you clean the bore to bare steel before each first shot and log its offset. Compare the average offset of each series against the rifle’s normal shot-to-shot standard deviation (see Chapters 15 and 16).

The data. Repeated testing shows that cold-clean-bore shifts are common and often measurable, with velocity and point of impact stabilizing only after several fouling shots (see Chapter 23). Cold-fouled-bore shifts are far less consistent. When shooters track 10 or more true cold-fouled shots on a single composite target, many perceived “cold-bore flyers” turn out to sit squarely inside the rifle’s normal dispersion cone, or disappear once the shooter runs a dry-fire settle routine before shot 1. A subset of rifles, often slim sporter barrels or rifles with uneven bedding contact, do display a repeatable directional shift on the first cold shot.

The verdict. Plausible but unproven as a universal rule; rifle-specific. Treat cold-clean-bore shifts as real and fire fouling shots after cleaning before hunting or competing. For a cold-fouled bore, never invent a correction from one stray first shot. Measure your first cold shot across 5 to 10 sessions, and apply a cold-bore offset only if the mean shift is consistent and larger than your rifle’s normal scatter.

Spotting and shot calling

Good shooting does not end when the trigger breaks. Calling your shot and reading downrange feedback tell you why a bullet landed where it did.

Calling your shot before impact. Keep your eye in the scope through the trigger break and note the exact spot on the target where the reticle sat the instant the firing pin fell. State your call mentally or aloud before you look for the bullet hole or splash: “Good break, center,” or “Pulled 0.2 MIL low-right.”

Shot calling separates shooter error from external error:

  • If you called “0.2 MIL low-right” and the bullet struck 0.2 MIL low-right, your zero, rifle, and wind call are correct. Fix your position or trigger press, not your turrets.
  • If you called a dead-center break and the bullet landed 0.4 MIL left, you have real information about wind, zero, or ammunition.

Reading bullet trace and impact splash. When spotting for yourself or a partner, position the spotting scope as close to the rifle’s bore line as practical, directly behind and slightly above the shooter’s shoulder. Seeing trace requires looking nearly along the bullet’s flight path. Focus the spotting scope sharply on the target, then back the focus slightly toward the shooter (roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the way to the target). That focal plane puts the descending arc of the bullet’s air disturbance into sharp relief.

When a bullet strikes dirt or gravel, watch the exact point where the splash starts, not the plume of dust blowing downwind a moment later. On painted steel, look for the dark lead mark or bright chipped paint, and listen for the delayed metallic ring (sound travels roughly 1,125 feet per second, so a 600-yard steel hit rings back more than one and a half seconds after impact).

Shooter-spotter communication. Agree on a shared language before the first shot is fired. Always speak in angular units matching the shooter’s reticle (MIL or MOA), never linear inches. A spotter who calls “12 inches left” forces the shooter to do mental division under time pressure, and 12 inches means completely different corrections at 300 yards versus 800 yards (see Chapter 3).

Keep calls short, standard, and unambiguous. State either the impact or the correction consistently, never mixing the two without saying which one you mean:

  • Correction call (preferred in matches): “Right 0.4, up 0.2.”
  • Impact plus correction: “Impact 0.4 MIL left, 0.2 low. Hold right 0.4, up 0.2.”

Confirm beforehand whether the shooter is dialing turrets or holding in the reticle so both people stay synchronized on follow-up shots.
