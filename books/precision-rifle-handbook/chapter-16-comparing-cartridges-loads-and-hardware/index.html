Key points

  • A plan fixed before the test keeps you from choosing luck after it.
  • A fixed sample is simple and fair but costly. Two-phase screening saves rounds but can cut a good load. A sequential tournament can save the most when one load is much worse, but needs a stricter threshold.
  • A small sample can reject a bad load but cannot rank good ones. The recommended default screens briefly and gives the large sample to one finalist.
  • The big gains come from the bullet, the powder and the barrel, not from fine charge changes.
  • Every extra load, and every extra look at the data, is one more chance for luck. The threshold tightens to match.

Why it matters

Small samples make identical loads look different. Shoot seven identical loads as one 5-shot group each, and the best group averages 0.66 times the load’s average 5-shot group (see Chapter 15). Pick it and you have mostly picked luck. Real differences are expensive to prove. Separating two loads whose dispersion differs by 1.5 to 1 takes about 35 shots per load, and 1.25 to 1 takes about 110 (see Table 16-1).

Small samples can still answer big questions. A few shots are enough to show that something is badly wrong. The skill is matching the shot count to the size of the question.

How it is done

For most testing, follow “The recommended default” below. Methods 1 to 3, after it, rank several loads at once.

Decide before you shoot

Write down the test plan before you prepare a single test round. Start with the question. Which of four loads groups best? Or does one load meet a 600-yard goal? Those are different tests.

Use mean radius for dispersion and velocity SD for consistency, and report each with its confidence interval. Keep extreme spread for spotting a gross fault. Set your goal as a maximum mean radius and maximum velocity SD for your target distance, such as 0.50 MOA and 12 fps. You can derive these limits from your desired hit probability using a drop chart (see Chapters 20 and 21). Until you have measured muzzle velocity, build a preliminary drop chart from the bullet maker’s BC and the published velocity adjusted for your barrel length, then update the limits once you have chronograph data. Choose your test method, shot counts and rejection threshold in advance so the goalposts cannot move after you see the targets.

What a p-value means

A statistical test of two loads returns a p-value. It is easy to misread.

  • The p-value is the probability of seeing a gap at least this large if the two loads were really the same.
  • It is not the probability that one load is better or worse. A p-value under 0.05 does not mean 95 percent certainty that the load with the smaller group is better.
  • A p-value under 0.05 means identical loads would show a gap at least this large less than 1 time in 20. That is good evidence of a real difference. It is not proof.
  • Each new test is a new chance for luck to fool you. Testing again after every block adds chances, and so does testing several loads at once. Both need a stricter threshold (see “Testing more than two loads” and “Method 3. The sequential tournament” below).

Evidence · Folklore

The claim is that one 3-shot or 5-shot group shows how a load shoots. Assuming normal scatter, two identical loads shot as one group each often look clearly different (see Table 16-2 and Figure 16-2). A small group can rule out a disaster, but it cannot rank loads.

Comparing two loads by hand

Intervals give a quick first look (see Chapter 15). When two loads’ intervals overlap almost completely, these shots cannot say which load is better. When the intervals do not overlap at all, the difference is very unlikely to be luck (see Figure 16-3). When they overlap a little, run a test.

With a calculator. When comparing two loads fired for the same number of shots in 5-shot groups, divide the larger mean radius by the smaller. Call the load with the larger mean radius worse if the ratio reaches or exceeds the threshold in Table 16-3 (which pre-calculates the F-test below). When comparing more than two loads, divide each load’s mean radius by the smallest mean radius and use the column for the total number of loads tested (see “Testing more than two loads” below).

With a spreadsheet. For any shot counts and group sizes, use an F-test on the two mean radii. It compares the square of their ratio with the F distribution, on each load’s degrees of freedom (see Appendix A.5 for the degrees of freedom and A.6 for the test).

For each load: n = shots, g = groups, MR = mean radius,
corrected for small groups (see Chapter 15)

df = 2 × (n − g) × 0.915
F  = (larger MR ÷ smaller MR)²
two-sided p = 2 × F.DIST.RT(F, df of larger, df of smaller)

The 0.915 adjusts the degrees of freedom for mean radius (see Appendix A.5). Use the two-sided test, because only the data tell you which load looks worse. Spreadsheets round the degrees of freedom down, which changes p very little.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Load A has 30 shots in six 5-shot groups and a mean radius of .42 inch. Load B has the same layout and a mean radius of .60 inch.

df          = 2 × (30 − 6) × 0.915 = 43.9 for each load
F           = (0.60 ÷ 0.42)² = 2.04
two-sided p = 2 × F.DIST.RT(2.04, 43.9, 43.9) = 0.020

Here a gap at least this large would turn up about 2 times in 100 if the two loads were really the same. That is not the chance that load B is worse (see “What a p-value means” above).

For one planned test of two loads, a p-value below 0.05 is the usual line. On that rule you would call load B worse. Table 16-3 agrees: the ratio is 1.43, past the line of 1.35 for 30 shots.

Two cautions apply to every comparison. First, a small sample has little power. With one 5-shot group per load, only a very large gap clears 0.05, and a p-value above it does not show the loads are the same (see Table 16-1). Second, radii measured from a group’s own center read small, and more so in small groups. Apply the small-group correction to each load before testing (see Chapter 15).

The recommended default

A short screen and one large confirmation answer most questions.

  1. Choose the candidates. Start from proven factory SKUs or proven components, and make the candidates differ in something that matters, such as the bullet, powder, or ammunition brand/lot.
  2. (Handloaders only; factory-ammo shooters start at Step 4.) Work up each candidate for pressure and velocity from its published start load (see The Handloading Handbook). One shot per step is fine for safety. It cannot find a node (see Chapter 17).
  3. (Handloaders only; factory-ammo shooters start at Step 4.) Choose each candidate’s charge for your velocity goal (see The Handloading Handbook), with margin below the step where its work-up stopped (see The Handloading Handbook).
  4. Screen each candidate with 5 to 10 shots, interleaved in one session (for .22 LR testing across brands with different bullet waxes, do not interleave every 5 rounds—shoot one brand per session or patch and season between brands; see Chapters 14 and 23). Reject the ones that are clearly bad. Extreme spread is good at catching a bad load.3
  5. Pick one finalist.
  6. Confirm the finalist with a 20 to 30 shot composite, ideally over two or more sessions.
  7. Judge it against your goal using the upper end of its 90 percent interval (see Chapter 15). For a hit goal at distance, judge it by its hit chance (see Chapter 21). For velocity SD across multiple sessions, use the within-session SD (see Chapter 15, “Pooling across days”) if your drop chart corrects velocity for temperature (see Chapter 23). Otherwise, use the pooled SD. Record which one you used.
  8. If it passes, you are done. If it fails, go back to step 6 with the next candidate that passed the screen. Or change the bullet or the powder and go back to step 2. A new bullet or powder needs its own work-up. Do not adjust the charge by 0.1 gr.

The last step matters most. A 0.1 gr change is tiny next to a change of bullet or powder. Small-sample charge nodes rarely repeat in large tests (see Chapter 17). Proving the effect of a 0.1 gr change would take far more shots than even a 1.25 to 1 difference needs.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Candidate 4 goes. Its 81 fps velocity ES is a red flag. A 10-shot SD has a 90 percent interval of 0.73 to 1.65 times the measured value. That puts its true SD between about 18 and 40 fps, wholly above the goal.

Candidate 3 goes too. Even a single 5-shot group has a 90 percent interval of 0.71 to 1.77 times, and 10 shots are tighter than that. At the low end, 0.71 × 0.79 = 0.56 MOA, which is still above the goal.

Ten shots cannot separate candidates 1 and 2. Pick one on whatever matters next, such as bullet supply, cost or ballistic coefficient. Say you pick candidate 1.

The confirmation is six 5-shot groups over two sessions. The measured mean radius is 0.36 MOA and the pooled velocity SD is 9 fps. First correct the mean radius for 5-shot groups.

corrected MR = measured MR × √(k ÷ (k − 1))
k = shots per group
0.36 × √(5 ÷ 4) = 0.36 × 1.118 = 0.40 MOA

For six 5-shot groups, the 90 percent interval for dispersion is 0.85 to 1.22 times. For 30 shots, velocity SD has a 90 percent interval of 0.83 to 1.28 times.

Mean radius:  0.40 × 0.85 = 0.34    to   0.40 × 1.22 = 0.49 MOA
Velocity SD:  9 × 0.83 = 7.5        to   9 × 1.28 = 11.5 fps

Both upper ends sit inside the goal, so candidate 1 passes. Suppose instead the corrected mean radius had come back at 0.62 MOA. Its whole 90 percent interval, 0.53 to 0.76 MOA, would sit above the goal. You would then move to candidate 2 or to a new bullet or powder, not to a 0.1 gr change.

Evidence · Supported by data

In large-sample tests, bullet, powder and barrel dominate precision.4 Screening small and confirming large follows from those results.

Testing more than two loads

Every extra load is one more chance for luck. You test the leader against each of the others, and the leader is the luckiest load of the group. That works like testing every pair. Take five loads that are truly the same and shoot 35 shots of each. Test the leader against the other four at a plain 0.05. At least one load will be called worse about 27 times in 100.

The fix is to divide 0.05 by the number of pairs. Five loads make 10 pairs, so the threshold is 0.005. The count is loads × (loads − 1) ÷ 2.

Each threshold is rounded down, which errs on the strict side. This keeps the chance of wrongly calling any load worse at or below about 5 in 100. The price is power. With more loads in the test, it takes a larger gap or more shots to call one worse.

To rank several loads at once, use one of the three methods below. In every one, a load that shows pressure signs stops at once, whatever the numbers say.

Method 1. Fixed sample

Choose a large sample, prepare it all, shoot it all and then decide.

  1. Choose a sample size per load: 30 to 35 rounds.
  2. Prepare everything first. Five loads of 35 rounds is 175 rounds.
  3. Fire in interleaved 5-round blocks (see Chapter 14).
  4. Pool each load’s shots and compute its mean radius.
  5. The smallest mean radius leads. Test the leader against each other load at the threshold from Table 16-5. A load that does not clear it is not shown to be worse, so treat it as tied with the leader.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Five loads, 35 shots each, all in 5-shot groups. Five loads make 10 pairs, so the threshold is 0.005.

Thirty-five shots each were enough to reject C. They were not enough to rank A, D, B and E. E looks worse, and a plain 0.05 would call it worse. With five loads in the test, that line is too loose. Choose among the four on velocity SD, cost or supply. Or shoot more.

Cost. The most rounds of any method. Every load gets the full count, even one that was plainly poor after its first block.

Risk. Low. No load is cut early by bad luck, and there is no math to do at the range. Ties among the leaders are common, because a 1.25 to 1 difference needs far more than 35 shots per load to show.

Method 2. Two-phase screening

Screen every load with a small sample. Cut the worst. Spend the large sample only on the rest.

  1. Phase 1. Before you prepare ammunition, write down the screening level, such as 0.05. Prepare 10 rounds of every load.
  2. Fire them in interleaved blocks and compute each mean radius. Test each load against the Phase 1 leader. Cut a load only when its p-value falls below the screening level, or when it shows a gross fault such as a wild extreme spread. Carry every other load into Phase 2.
  3. Phase 2. Prepare 25 more rounds of each survivor.
  4. Fire them interleaved. Pool each survivor’s two phases. Test each against the leader at the Table 16-5 threshold for the number of loads you screened.
  5. Check that the Phase 2 shots alone tell the same story. If they do not, treat those loads as tied and shoot more before you choose.

This table holds two lessons. First, B led the screen, but its next 25 shots were ordinary. Selection flatters the survivors, because a load that passes a screen was partly lucky. That is why step 5 checks the Phase 2 shots alone. Second, D trailed at 0.52. Its gap was well inside the noise of 10 shots, so the rule carried it. Its next 25 shots matched the leaders. A rule that cut the worst half would have lost it.

Cost. In this example 160 rounds, against 210 for a fixed sample of 35.

Risk. Cutting a load as good as the leader. With six equal loads, a 0.05 screen cuts at least one of them about 1 time in 3, and a 0.01 screen about 1 time in 12. A 10-shot screen cannot reliably separate even a 1.5 to 1 difference, which needs about 35 shots per load, so it saves rounds only when some loads are far worse.

Method 3. The sequential tournament

Test after every block. Each of these tests is a look. Drop a load as soon as the evidence says it is worse. Keep shooting the rest.

  1. Before the first shot, write down the loads, the block size, the maximum shots per load and the threshold for every look.
  2. Prepare one 5-round block of every load.
  3. Fire the blocks. Record the raw X and Y of every shot.
  4. Recompute each load’s mean radius from all of its shots so far.
  5. Find the leader, the load with the smallest mean radius.
  6. Run the two-sided F-test of each other load’s mean radius against the leader’s.
  7. Cut a load only when its p-value falls below the threshold for this look.
  8. Prepare and fire the next block only for the survivors. Stop when one load remains or you reach the planned maximum.

The threshold must be stricter than 0.05, for two reasons. Each look is one more chance for luck to cut a good load. And the leader is the luckiest load of the group (see “Testing more than two loads” above). Take four loads that are truly the same. A plain 0.05 at each of six looks cuts at least one of them nearly half the time.

Use the O’Brien-Fleming thresholds, divided by the number of pairs as in Table 16-5.5 They are very strict at the early looks, when each load has few shots. They ease toward 0.05 at the last look, when the most shots stand behind the decision. Over the whole tournament the chance of cutting a load as good as the leader stays at or below about 5 in 100.

Barrel-life budgeting for overbore cartridges

Overbore cartridges (7mm SAUM, 6.5 PRC, hot 6mm designs) often deliver only 800 to 1,300 competitive rounds before throat erosion degrades precision, with lands advancing roughly .010 to .015 inch per 100 rounds (see Chapter 23). A 175- to 210-round five-load fixed test burns 15 to 25 percent of that life before a single match. On barrel-burners, use two-phase screening (Method 2) or a sequential tournament (Method 3), limit the initial slate to two or three proven pairings, drop clear losers early, and save 30-shot confirmation for your finalist.

Testing any claim yourself

Any precision claim can be tested by treating each setup as an arm:

  1. State the claim numerically (“setting 3 groups a third smaller than setting 1,” not “shoots better”) and fix the shot count and decision rule beforehand.
  2. Size each arm from Table 16-1 for two arms. With more arms, Table 16-5 tightens the threshold: finding a 1.5 to 1 group-size difference needs about 45, 55, and 60 shots per arm for three, four, and five arms (about 67, 77, and 84 for velocity SD).
  3. Work up from the published start load for any arm that changes a component or seats bullets deeper or into the lands (see The Handloading Handbook).
  4. Interleave and rotate arms each pass (see Chapter 14), blinding the shooter via a helper when possible.
  5. Analyze with confidence intervals and F-tests on mean radius and velocity SD (above; Appendix A.6 and A.8), then repeat winner versus runner-up on another day to confirm.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. Testing whether annealing every firing cuts velocity SD from 15 to 10 fps (1.5 to 1) requires about 50 interleaved shots per arm (50 annealed, 50 not). A smaller claim—15 to 12 fps (1.25 to 1)—needs about 160 shots per arm. When a test costs more than the gain is worth, that is a useful answer too.

Closing the loop

When a candidate misses your precision or velocity SD goals (see Chapter 21), act on the numbers rather than guessing:

  • Handloaders: Take your composite mean radius, velocity SD, and intervals back to the bench (see The Handloading Handbook). Skip 0.1 gr tweaks—change a major variable (bullet, powder, or primer) and work up safely from the published start load.
  • Factory shooters: Log the failing lot and test another lot, bullet weight, or match brand. For .22 LR rimfire, structured lot testing is the primary way to match ammunition to a barrel.

Keep failed tests in your log (see Chapter 13) so you never waste components or barrel life repeating a dead end.
