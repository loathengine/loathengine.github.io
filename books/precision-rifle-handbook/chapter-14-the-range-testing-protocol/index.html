Chapter 14. The Range Testing Protocol

Key points

  • A rifle’s first session shoots its baseline. A comparison holds only while its hardware stays the same.
  • A target printed at true size doubles as a ruler. An aim mark the group never touches stays clean to aim at.
  • Conditions change from one range trip to the next, so the record has to carry them.
  • Interleaving spreads barrel heat, fatigue and weather evenly across the loads. Which shots count is settled before you fire.
  • Each chronograph reading belongs on its own shot’s line. Count readings against shots after every block.

Why it matters

Statistics can only work with what the range gives them. A careless session adds noise that no formula can remove. Worse, it can add bias. Bias makes one load look better for a reason that has nothing to do with the load. Bad data in gives bad results out.

The range protocol does three jobs. It holds the rifle and the shooter constant. It spreads the changes you cannot prevent evenly across the loads. It records everything else so you can see it later.

How it is done

Before the range: confirm the baseline

Before each session, confirm that the rifle’s hardware is unchanged since its baseline (see Chapter 4 and “Shoot the hardware baseline” below). Check that the scope, rings and action screws are still at their torque. Keep the cleaning routine the same. Update the barrel round count. Any hardware change ends the comparison and starts a new baseline. That includes a new scope, rings, trigger, stock or muzzle device.

Shoot from the steadiest position you can. A solid rest on a shooting bench is best. Prone with sandbags is next (see Chapter 7). Control every variable you can. Between loads, change only the one thing under test.

Print targets at actual size

A printed target doubles as a ruler. If the printer shrinks the page, every measurement shrinks with it.

  1. Choose a target that is easy to read and easy to carry home. Measuring at home is easier than measuring at the range.
  2. Print at 100 percent, also called Actual Size. Never use “fit to page”.
  3. Measure a known feature on the printed sheet with a rule or calipers. A 6-inch bullseye must measure 6 inches (see Figure 14-1).
  4. Use one sheet per load per session. Label it with the session ID, the date, the rifle and the load (see Chapter 13).
  5. Give every block its own aim mark (see “Blocks: how many rounds per load per session” below).

Offset the aim mark

Do not aim at the spot where the group will land. A group that lands on its aim mark slowly shoots the mark away. Each later shot is then aimed at a ragged hole, and the hold gets worse.

Set the scope so the group lands above and to the right of the aim mark instead. On a grid, up and right can both be positive numbers. That keeps the arithmetic simple and avoids sign errors.

  1. Zero the rifle at your test distance.
  2. Dial the scope up and right by a fixed amount. Move it far enough that no hole can touch the aim mark, such as 2 MOA up and 2 MOA right at 100 yards. One MOA is about 1 inch at 100 yards, so that is about 2 inches each way. Example numbers. Not load data.
  3. Write down the offset.
  4. Leave it alone for the whole test.

The offset does not change the group’s size. Mean radius is measured from the group’s own center, not from the aim mark (see Chapter 15). It is not a zero error either. Fire a zero group with the turrets back at zero before you measure the zero (see Chapter 20).

Record every shot as raw coordinates

Record each shot’s horizontal (X) and vertical (Y) distance from its aim mark. Keep these raw numbers for good, never only a group’s average or size. Every later analysis starts from them.

  1. Take the target home and lay it flat.
  2. Measure from the center of the aim mark to the center of each hole. A grid target makes this quick.
  3. Write each shot’s X and Y on its own line in your shot record, beside its velocity (see Chapter 13).
  4. Keep the sheet with your notes.

You turn those lines into a mean radius (see Chapter 15).

Session variance: what changes between range trips

You need many shots per load, so most tests span several range trips. Conditions between trips do not affect every result equally. They rank like this.

FactorEffect on muzzle velocityEffect on the trajectoryWhat to do
TemperatureCritical. Powder temperature moves velocity and pressure.Critical. Cold air is denser, so the bullet drops more.Record air and ammunition temperature. Keep ammunition shaded.
Elevation and station pressureNoneMajor. Thin air reduces drag.Record altitude and pressure.
Storage humidityModerate to severe. Damp powder loses velocity, and powder stored drier than at the work-up gains it (see The Handloading Handbook).MinorStore powder and ammunition sealed. Note where they were kept.
WindNoneMoves shots within a session only.Hold for the wind you have. Test in the calmest conditions you can.

Read the air temperature and the station pressure from a pocket weather meter (see Chapter 7). Station pressure is the pressure where you stand, not the sea-level figure in weather reports (see Chapter 19). Read the ammunition temperature with a thermometer in the ammunition box.

Temperature leads the list. Ammunition left in a hot car, or a round left in a hot chamber, can raise pressure to a dangerous level. Keep ammunition out of the sun. Chamber a round only when you are ready to fire it.

How much velocity shifts with temperature depends on the powder. Suppose you develop a load at 90 °F and fire it at 30 °F. A powder that shifts by 0.14 fps per °F loses about 8 fps. A powder at 0.5 fps per °F (where temperature sensitivity starts to matter) loses about 30 fps. Both estimates assume a straight-line change, though many powders drop off faster in the cold. Example numbers. Not load data. Keep your ammunition at a steady temperature during a session, and temperature shift within the session largely disappears (see Chapter 23).

The most important control is simple. Shoot every load you are comparing in the same session, interleaved. Then the day’s weather falls on all of them alike. A cold day may slow every load, but it will not favor one.

How the rounds were stored matters too. In one test, damp storage moved velocity more than heat or cold usually does (see The Handloading Handbook).1 Temperature response also varies by cartridge, lot and rifle (see The Handloading Handbook).

Blocks: how many rounds per load per session

A block is a run of one load fired in a row, such as 5 rounds. A pass fires one block of every load, and a session holds one or more passes. Blocks add shots to a growing record for each load. The total number of shots sets most of your statistical power. The block size sets the practical trade-offs.

Small blocks carry one statistical cost. Each group’s center comes from its own shots, so each separate group uses up a little of the information (see Chapter 15, “Composite groups”).

BlockStrengthsWeaknesses
3 roundsFast. Little barrel heat. Saves components early.Many small groups to track. A 30-shot record takes 10 sessions and mixes many days of weather.
5 roundsThe practical middle. Ammunition boxes hold rows of five. Six loads of five make a 30-round session.Still needs several sessions, so some weather mixing remains.
10 roundsFewer sessions. Most of a load’s data shares one day’s conditions.More barrel heat and fatigue. Spends more components on a load that loses.
20 roundsA load’s data shares one day’s conditions.The most heat and fatigue. Wastes components if the load was poor from the third shot.

To build a 30-shot record with one block per load per session takes 10 sessions with 3-round blocks. It takes 6 sessions with 5-round blocks, 3 with 10-round blocks and 2 with 20-round blocks.

For most testing a 5-round block is the best balance. Use 10-round blocks when weather between sessions worries you more than barrel heat. Rimfire (.22 LR) shooters using 50-round match boxes with minimal barrel heat often shoot 10-round blocks (five 10-shot groups per box) for better statistical efficiency.

Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Barrel heat and fatigue are the usual explanations for groups that open late in a long string. The effect varies by rifle and barrel. In one controlled test, 50-shot strings were slow-fired through steel and carbon-fiber barrels of several contours. Point of impact shifted by more than 1 MOA as a barrel heated, and heavier barrels generally shifted less.2

Interleave the loads

Never fire one load’s entire allotment in a row—the last load would meet the hottest barrel and most tired shooter. Fire one block of each load in turn instead. Never interleave a work-up: fire its steps in order, lowest first (see The Handloading Handbook).

  1. Box each load in blocks, in firing order, and fire every load at the same distance.
  2. Fire one block of load A, then B, then C, rotating the starting load each pass (A B C, then B C A, then C A B) so no load always goes first or last.
  3. Keep a steady pace and rest between blocks, noting each block’s start time.

Exception for .22 LR bullet waxes. Interleaving 5- or 10-round blocks works across different lots of the same .22 LR brand. Do not interleave different brands with incompatible waxes (such as oil/wax blends versus beeswax/tallow), as mixing lubricants opens groups until the bore re-seasons. Test one brand per session, or patch out the old wax (dry and solvent patches) and fire 15 to 30 seasoning shots before recording the next brand (see Chapter 23).

Foulers and sighters

Fouling shots after cleaning and sighters that check aim offset are not test data. Exclude them, but decide in advance:

  1. Write down your fouler and sighter count before the session, and fire them at a separate aim mark or sheet.
  2. Log each on its own numbered line marked fouler or sighter, since the chronograph reads them too (see Chapter 13).
  3. Once you fire at a test aim mark, every shot counts.

Never drop a bad-looking shot after the fact—a flyer is often just the edge of normal spread. Exclude a test shot only for a shooter or equipment error documented when fired, before looking at the target (see Chapter 15).

Chronographs: set up and check

Doppler radar is best for load testing (see Chapter 7). Do not shoot group tests with a barrel-mounted magnetic chronograph unless on-versus-off testing confirms it does not shift point of impact or group size (see Chapter 7). Every session:

  1. Align the unit per its manual (point Doppler units along the line of fire) and verify readings on your foulers.
  2. Count readings against shots after every block—a missed or extra reading shifts every pairing after it.
  3. Call each shot before looking at the target, then spot its hole via spotting scope or target camera (see Chapter 7) so every reading—including foulers and sighters—lands on its own shot’s line.
  4. Never change chronographs mid-test; to compare two units, record the same shots on both and note the offset.

Shoot the hardware baseline

Your first session establishes the rifle’s hardware baseline with repeatable ammunition (see Chapter 4), against which every later load is judged:

  1. Use a proven load or one lot of factory match ammunition, following the protocol above (actual-size targets, offset aim marks, separate foulers, every shot logged).
  2. Fire five 5-shot groups—never trust a single group.3
  3. Label the targets, pool the five groups into one composite mean radius (see Chapter 15), and record it in the baseline log with the barrel round count (see Chapter 13).
  4. (If handloading) Save and label the fired cases for this rifle; their chamber-formed shape sets your sizing die (see The Handloading Handbook).
