Key points

  • Every load—factory or handloaded—performs specifically to one rifle, and your baseline data only applies to the exact setup it was tested in.
  • Cut, button and hammer-forged barrels can all shoot well. A heavier contour warms less, and heat moves the point of impact.
  • A damaged crown or a new muzzle device moves the point of impact.
  • Barrel length moves velocity. It barely moves peak pressure.
  • Record twist, sight height, magazine length, and (if handloading) distance to the lands. The lands move forward as the throat wears.

Why it matters

Every result you measure comes from the rifle and the load together. Two rifles in the same chambering can fire the same load at different velocities because their bores, throats and finishes differ.1 A shifting scope, a loose action screw or a new trigger changes your groups as surely as a new powder does. Until the rifle stays the same from one test to the next, you cannot tell what the load is doing.

How it is done

Learn the barrel first. Then measure the rifle, lock its hardware and record it.

The barrel

A rifle barrel is a precisely made steel tube. The chamber is cut into the breech end, and the crown is cut at the muzzle. The bore joins the two. Spiral ridges called rifling line the bore. They grip the bullet and spin it so it stays stable in flight (Figure 4-1).

  • Lands are the raised spiral ridges inside the bore. Grooves are the recessed channels cut between them.
  • Bore diameter is measured across the tops of the opposing lands. Groove diameter is measured across the bottoms of the grooves. Bullets are made to the larger groove diameter, so the raised lands engrave the bullet as it enters the rifling. The engraved bullet seals the propellant gases behind it and takes up the spin.
  • The crown is the machined exit of the bore at the muzzle. It is the last point the bullet touches before free flight. A burr or an uneven edge there deflects the escaping gas and moves the point of impact (see “The muzzle” below). Protect the crown from knocks, and keep a cleaning rod from wearing it.

Most modern rifle barrels are made from chrome-moly steel (such as 4140) or martensitic stainless steel (such as type 416).2 The two differ in how well they resist rust and how easily they machine. Neither is more accurate than the other.3 Whether stainless keeps its accuracy longer is not settled.3

At the breech end, the chamber supports the cartridge case. The rifling begins just ahead of the case mouth. The transition between them is the throat, where the loaded round meets the barrel (see The Handloading Handbook).

Contour and fluting

The contour, or profile, is the outside shape of the barrel along its length. The barrel blank is turned on a lathe centered on the bore. That keeps the outside of the barrel concentric with the bore.2 Most contours have a straight shank just ahead of the action and then taper toward the muzzle. Makers give their profiles names such as sporter, varmint or heavy. They also publish drawings that give the diameters along the barrel. The names are not standard, so one maker’s varmint contour differs from another’s. Compare the drawings, not the names.

A heavier contour has more steel. That makes it stiffer. The extra steel also soaks up more heat, so each shot raises the barrel temperature less. In controlled tests over long firing strings, the point of impact shifted by more than 1 MOA as barrels heated. Heavier contours generally moved less.4 One MOA (minute of angle) is about 1.047 inches at 100 yards. The price of a stiffer barrel that holds steadier under heat is more weight to carry and support.

Fluting cuts lengthwise grooves into the outside of the barrel. It saves weight and adds surface area, which aids cooling. A fluted barrel is stiffer than an unfluted barrel of equal weight, because the unfluted barrel needs a slimmer contour to weigh the same.5 But a fluted barrel is always less stiff than an unfluted barrel of the same starting contour. Removing steel reduces stiffness.

A carbon-fiber barrel wraps a thin steel liner in structural carbon fiber. It is very stiff for its weight. In the same heat tests, a carbon-fiber barrel ranked among the most stable. Its point of impact shifted little as it heated.6

Heat in the barrel

Every shot puts heat into the barrel. The hot powder gas heats the bore hardest at the throat, where temperature and pressure peak. That heat moves outward through the steel to the outside of the barrel. The surface then sheds heat to the air by radiation and by convection, where moving air carries it away. A barrel takes in heat in seconds but needs many minutes to cool.

How fast a barrel warms depends on the powder charge compared with the weight of the barrel steel. A large case behind a small bore in a slim barrel heats the fastest. A small case in a heavy barrel heats the slowest.

Heat that builds up changes how the rifle and the load shoot.

  • Point of impact moves. As a barrel heats, its shots can walk and its groups can open (see “Contour and fluting” above).
  • Velocity climbs. Velocity can rise through a string as the chamber heats. A round left in a hot chamber warms, and its pressure rises. Never leave one there (see Chapter 14).
  • The throat wears faster. Heat speeds the erosion that moves the lands forward (see “Throat erosion” below).

Pace your shots to keep heat from skewing a test. Start each test with a cool barrel and fire at a steady pace, so every load is shot at the same barrel heat (see Chapter 14). Open the action between strings so air can move through the bore. Keep the rifle in the shade.

Contested a black barrel cools faster than a bright one

The claim. A black barrel sheds heat faster than a bright stainless one.

The physics. The finish does not change how much heat a shot puts in. It changes how well the surface radiates. A flat black finish radiates over 90 percent of the most any surface can. Cleaned stainless steel radiates about 30 percent, and polished stainless even less.7 With the barrel at about 140 °F in 70 °F still air, radiation carries about as much heat as the air does. A black barrel can then shed roughly half again as much heat as a bright one. In a 10 mph breeze, the moving air carries most of the heat. A black barrel then sheds only about a tenth more heat than a bright one.8 A coating is too thin to hold heat in. Sunlight works the other way. A black finish takes in more of the sun’s heat, so in full sun a black barrel sits warmer between strings.

The test. Heat two barrels of one contour, one black and one bright, to the same temperature. Log their cooling with thermocouples. Do it in still air and in wind, and in shade and in sun. An infrared thermometer misleads here. It reads bright steel far cooler than it is, because bright steel radiates less.

The data. No controlled cooling test of black and bright rifle barrels has been published. The physics rests on measured surface values and standard heat-transfer methods.

The verdict. Plausible but unproven. Expect a real gain in still air and shade, little in wind and a loss in full sun. Time between strings cools a barrel more than any finish.

Throat erosion

Hot gas at high pressure wears the throat on every shot. The wear eats away the start of the rifling and moves the lands forward. In 6 mm and 6.5 mm barrels the lands move a few thousandths of an inch per 100 rounds. At a fixed seating length, the bullet’s jump grows over time, so (if handloading) measure the distance to the lands from time to time (see The Handloading Handbook). Manage throat wear and velocity drift over the life of the barrel (see Chapter 23).

The muzzle

When the bullet leaves the muzzle, the gas behind it is still under high pressure. As the bullet clears the crown, that gas expands around its base and overtakes it. Even expansion pushes the bullet straight ahead. If gas escapes early on one side, it pushes unevenly on the bullet’s base and tips the bullet slightly off line. That tilt is called yaw. In tests of large-caliber barrels, uneven pressure at the muzzle tracked the bullet’s first yaw closely.9

Muzzle pressure is the gas pressure still behind the bullet as it leaves. It is far lower than the peak pressure reached early in the shot (see The Handloading Handbook). In a test of a 5.56x45mm NATO rifle, peak pressure near the chamber was about 55,700 psi. Muzzle pressure was about 4,800 psi from a 24-inch barrel, 8,150 psi at 14.5 inches and over 25,000 psi at 5 inches. It rose faster and faster as the barrel got shorter.10 Slower powders and heavier charges also leave more pressure at the muzzle, with more blast and noise.11

The crown. A crown cut square to the bore with a sharp, even edge lets gas escape evenly around the bullet base. A nick, a burr or wear from a cleaning rod lets gas out early on one side. Look over the crown from time to time under bright light and a magnifier. A gunsmith can recut a damaged crown square to the bore. Re-zero the rifle after any crown work.

Contested a damaged crown ruins a rifle’s precision

The claim. A nick in the crown permanently opens a rifle’s groups.

The physics. A flaw in the crown lets gas out early on one side and tilts the bullet. A tilt that is the same on every shot moves the point of impact. A tilt that changes from shot to shot opens the group.

The test. Fire enough groups to find a reliable mean radius for the rifle (see Chapter 15). Damage the crown on purpose and fire the same number of shots. Then recut the crown square to the bore and fire a third set. Compare the three sets (see Chapter 16).

The data. Three published tests damaged rifle crowns on purpose to measure the effect. All three saw a clear shift in the point of impact. A crown cut with a grinder moved the point of impact about 16 MOA.12 A crown struck with a hammer moved it well over a foot. Recutting the crown brought it back.13 The effect on group size is less clear. The ground crown still shot six 5-shot groups that averaged about 1 MOA, against a single 0.55 MOA group before the damage.12 The hammered crown averaged 2.26 inches over five 5-shot groups, against 1.55 inches before. Yet the same rifle averaged 1.40 inches after its muzzle was sawn off and deburred with a knife.13 A third test damaged the crowns of four barrels, from mildly to severely. Every damaged barrel shot less accurately than before, and the first shots strayed the most. The report gave no group sizes.14 None of these tests fired enough shots to tell a small change in group size from chance (see Chapter 1).

The verdict. Plausible but unproven. Every test shows that a damaged crown moves the point of impact. Whether crown damage reliably opens groups is not settled. Treat a damaged crown as a hardware change: recut it, re-zero the rifle and shoot a new baseline.

Muzzle devices

A muzzle brake vents gas to the sides to cut recoil. A suppressor traps the gas and lets it out slowly. Either one changes the blast the bullet flies through. Either one also adds weight at the muzzle, and that changes how the barrel vibrates (see Chapter 17). In one test, muzzle devices were threaded to run true to the bore within .002 inch.9 A device that runs off the bore line lets the gas out unevenly.

  • Point of impact moves. Expect the zero to move when you fit or remove a device. Zero with the device you will shoot with.
  • Velocity can change. In one test, a suppressor lowered one load’s velocity by about 30 fps (about 1 percent). It raised two others a little. Measure velocity with the device you will shoot with.9
  • Yaw can change. In the same test, a .300 Winchester Magnum rifle fired four bullets with no device and with three suppressors. It fired 5 to 10 shots of each bullet in each setup. For three bullets, the drag changed by 4 percent or less. One bullet was prone to yaw. Its drag was highest with no device and fell with each suppressor, most with a two-stage design.15
  • Discipline rules. Check your competition rules before fitting a muzzle brake. Both F-Open and F-TR F-Class rules ban muzzle brakes to protect neighboring prone shooters from side blast.
Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Brakes and suppressors are said both to open groups and to tighten them. In one published test, a rifle fired one 5-shot group with each of five brakes and one with none, too few to tell a real change from luck (see Chapter 1).16 A device is part of the rifle’s hardware. Fitting, removing or changing one voids the baseline (see “The hardware baseline” below).

How rifling is made

Every barrel begins as a solid round bar. A deep-hole drill bores a hole slightly under the final bore diameter. A reamer then brings the hole to size and smooths its surface.2 The rifling is then formed by one of three main methods (Figure 4-3).

  • Cut rifling. A hook-shaped cutter with a single point is drawn through the bore again and again. It turns as it travels, so the groove follows the twist rate. Each pass shaves a very thin layer of steel from one groove. The cutter removes steel without cold-working it, so it leaves very little stress in the barrel.2 Cut rifling is the oldest method and the slowest.17
  • Button rifling. A button is a hard carbide die ground with the reverse of the rifling. It is pulled or pushed through the reamed bore in one pass. The button does not cut metal. It presses the grooves in by pushing the steel aside. That cold-forming leaves a lot of stress in the steel, so the barrel must be heat-treated after rifling to relieve it. Without stress relief, the bore can open up or the barrel can warp when its outside is turned to contour.2
  • Hammer forging. A short, thick-walled barrel blank is fitted over a hard mandrel that carries the reverse of the rifling. Opposing power hammers beat the outside of the blank down onto the mandrel. The steel stretches longer and thinner as the rifling forms inside the bore.17 Done at room temperature, the process is called cold hammer forging. The machinery costs a great deal, but the cost per barrel is low. Many modern factory rifles use hammer-forged barrels.17

The method a maker uses tends to follow its size. The largest makers mostly hammer forge, mid-sized makers button rifle and small shops cut.18

Whatever the method, a match bore needs the same bore and groove diameters along its full length. Its twist must also follow its design from breech to muzzle. Most barrels have a constant twist. Some match barrels use a gain, or progressive, twist that quickens toward the muzzle. A flaw in the bore can be pressed into each bullet that passes through it. A bullet marked that way can be less stable in flight.

Evidence · Plausible but unproven

Cut rifling is widely held to be the most accurate method. No controlled test of well-made barrels from each process has been published. All three methods can make an equally accurate barrel.18 What counts is the finished bore, not the process that made it. Cut rifling is slow, and small shops favor it. A small shop can inspect each barrel closely. Button rifling and hammer forging are faster and suit mass production. Judge a barrel by its maker’s quality control and the groups it shoots.

Barrel length and velocity

Unload the rifle and check that the chamber is empty before you take any measurement.

To measure barrel length, take off any muzzle brake, suppressor or thread protector so the crown is bare. Close the bolt on the empty chamber. Slide a cleaning rod in from the muzzle until it stops against the bolt face. Mark the rod flush with the crown. Pull the rod out and measure from its tip to the mark (see Figure 4-4).

Published load data comes from test barrels that your rifle will rarely match. A shorter barrel gives less muzzle velocity. A longer barrel gives more, but each added inch gains less. Published barrel tests show a change of about 17 to 43 fps per inch, depending on the cartridge.19 Test barrels also often give higher velocities than sporting rifles.20 Treat a published velocity as a reference, not a promise. Rimfire .22 LR is an exception: its small, fast-burning powder charge finishes accelerating the bullet early, so velocity typically peaks around 16 to 18 inches and may drop slightly in longer barrels due to bore friction.

Barrel length has almost no effect on peak pressure. Pressure peaks in the first few inches of bullet travel, long before the bullet reaches the muzzle (see The Handloading Handbook).21

To estimate how barrel length changes velocity, multiply the difference in length by the change per inch. For a shorter barrel (if handloading), lower your work-up velocity ceiling by 43 fps per inch unless you have a published test for your cartridge. The 43 fps figure gives the lower, safer ceiling. Do not raise the velocity ceiling for a longer barrel, because the gain from extra length is only an estimate (see The Handloading Handbook).

Twist and stability

A rifled barrel spins the bullet so it stays stable and flies point first. Twist rate is the length of bore in which the rifling makes one full turn. One turn in 8 inches is written as 1:8. A shorter length is a faster twist, and it gives the bullet more spin.

Longer bullets need a faster twist. Check that your barrel’s twist will stabilize a bullet before you buy it (see Chapter 18).

Use the maker’s figure for the twist or measure it yourself. Push a tight patch on a free-spinning cleaning rod a short way into the bore, so the patch grips the rifling. Mark the rod at the muzzle and stick a tape flag on it near the handle. Push the rod in until the flag has made one full turn, then mark the rod at the muzzle again (see Figure 4-6). The distance between the two marks is the twist rate. Watch which way the rod turns from the handle end as you push it forward. Clockwise is a right-hand twist, and counterclockwise is a left-hand twist. Record both the rate and the direction (such as 1:8 right-hand), because spin drift depends on both (see Chapters 19 and 20).

Sight height and trajectory

The scope sits above the bore, so the line of sight and the bullet’s path start apart. The bullet leaves the muzzle below the line of sight. It rises through the line of sight to its highest point and drops back through it at the zero distance. Sight height is the distance from the center of the bore to the center of the scope. It sets where the two lines cross, and every drop chart needs it. Inside the zero distance, an error in sight height can move the predicted point of impact by more than an inch.22 Beyond the zero distance, the error grows with distance. With a 100-yard zero, it reaches about 0.9 MOA per inch of error at 1,000 yards. A drop chart trued at long range absorbs that far error. A wrong sight height then shows up at close range (see Chapter 20).

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

To find sight height, use calipers that read to .001 inch. Measure the gap between the bottom of the scope’s main tube and the round receiver or barrel below it. Measure that part’s diameter at the same spot. Then add half of each diameter to the gap. This assumes the receiver or barrel is round and centered on the bore.

sight height = gap + (tube diameter ÷ 2)
                   + (receiver or barrel diameter ÷ 2)

gap                                    = .290 inch
30 mm tube = 1.181 inch, so half       = .591 inch
receiver   = 1.350 inch, so half       = .675 inch

sight height = .290 + .591 + .675      = 1.556 inch, about 1.56 inch

Magazine length and seating

A round that feeds from a repeating rifle’s magazine must fit inside it. The longest round that feeds reliably is the rifle’s magazine length. It can be shorter than the cartridge’s maximum COAL (cartridge overall length, measured from the case head to the bullet tip). For rounds that must feed from the magazine, magazine length caps how far out you can seat a bullet. If the lands sit beyond that limit, those bullets must jump to reach the rifling.

A repeater can also be single-fed. A round too long for the magazine can be loaded by hand, one round at a time. Some Highpower service rifle shooters do this with semi-automatic rifles (see The Handloading Handbook, Chapter 26). In the slow-fire stages they load each round by hand, so they seat their bullets long, past magazine length. They keep magazine-length rounds for the rapid-fire stages.

If you shoot factory ammunition, any factory round that feeds and chambers reliably already gives you a length known to fit. Measure maximum magazine length with dummy rounds (if handloading) once you have a seating die, sized cases, and bullets (see The Handloading Handbook).

  1. Make dummy rounds with no primer and no powder. Mark them for good so they can never mix with live ammunition. Coloring the case or drilling a hole through the case wall works well (see Figure 4-8).
  2. Seat a bullet long on purpose and try the round in the magazine. Seat the bullet deeper in small steps until the round fits inside the magazine.
  3. Cycle the dummy round from the magazine through the action several times. Check that the bullet tip clears the front of the magazine without binding and that the round chambers smoothly.
  4. Record the longest overall length that feeds reliably every time. That length is your magazine length.

Three limits cap how long you can load a round (if handloading): magazine length (for magazine-fed rounds), distance to the lands, and neck grip. Seat the bullet at least one caliber deep in the neck so the case holds it securely. The published COAL is the lower limit. If you seat a bullet deeper than the published COAL or deeper than your tested recipe, work up the load again from the start load (see The Handloading Handbook). Choose an overall length between these upper and lower limits (see The Handloading Handbook).

Platforms: bolt-action, semi-automatic, and rimfire

Different rifle actions impose different constraints on precision, recoil control, and ammunition selection.

  • Bolt-action centerfire. A bolt action locks the cartridge into a rigid, closed receiver with no reciprocating parts moving while the bullet travels down the bore. Manual cycling gives consistent chambering and lets you tune bullet seating length freely. Bolt actions are the standard platform for Benchrest, F-Class, PRS, and most long-range hunting.
  • Semi-automatic (gas guns and Service Rifles). A semi-automatic rifle taps propellant gas to cycle a heavy bolt carrier group. Moving mass, gas-port timing, and automatic feeding add mechanical variables that a bolt action lacks. A free-floated handguard is essential so sling tension or bipod pressure cannot flex the barrel and shift the zero. In Highpower Service Rifle, shooters balance magazine-fed rapid-fire reliability with slow-fire single-loading precision, and verify that primers sit safely below flush to prevent slam-fires (see The Handloading Handbook, Chapter 26).23
  • Rimfire (.22 LR) precision rifles and trainers. A bolt-action or match semi-automatic .22 LR rifle serves both as a dedicated precision competition rifle (NRL22, PRS Rimfire, smallbore) and as a low-recoil, modest-cost trainer for realistic wind-reading practice on short ranges. Because a standard-velocity .22 LR bullet leaves the muzzle around 1,050 to 1,100 fps, wind pushes it at 100 to 200 yards much like wind pushes a centerfire match bullet at 600 to 1,000 yards. Rimfire cartridges cannot be handloaded by the shooter, so ammunition selection relies on testing multiple factory brands and lot numbers to find the lot that shoots best in your specific barrel (see Chapter 12).

Factory rifles, staged upgrades, and custom actions

Precision rifles fall into four build tiers. Comparing them up front keeps you from paying twice for the same rifle.

  • Factory rifle (as-issued). Ready to shoot out of the box at the lowest entry cost. Many shoot well enough for hunting, club matches, and learning marksmanship when paired with a reliable optic and known-good ammunition. Common limits are thin sporter barrels that heat quickly, flexible stocks, and heavier factory triggers.
  • Factory rifle with stock and trigger upgrades. Fitting a rigid, adjustable stock or chassis and a crisp aftermarket trigger immediately improves cheek weld, length of pull, balance, and trigger break without touching the barrel. If you choose a common action footprint, these parts transfer to a custom action later.
  • Upgraded factory rifle with an aftermarket prefit barrel. When a factory barrel wears out or limits precision, a match-grade barrel-nut or shouldered prefit brings custom-grade bore uniformity to an existing receiver. Always verify headspace with go and no-go gauges after any barrel change before firing (Safety · See Chapter 2).
  • Custom action build. Machined to tight concentricity tolerances, usually on a standard footprint (such as the Remington 700 pattern) with an integral recoil lug, integral scope rail, smooth bolt lift, and reliable extraction. Tight receiver tolerances allow true shouldered prefit barrels you can swap at home with an action wrench and barrel vise.

Decide early: upgrade a factory rifle or buy a custom action. Tally the full cost of the finished rifle before buying your first part. Upgrading a factory rifle with a chassis, match trigger, bottom metal, rail, and prefit barrel often matches or exceeds the cost of starting with a custom action, while leaving you with a factory receiver that lacks integral features and strong resale value. If you plan to shoot a factory rifle as-is or stop at a stock and trigger, start with a factory rifle on a widely supported footprint. If you already know you want a match chassis, competition trigger, and heavy prefit barrel, buying a custom action from the start usually saves money and avoids building the same rifle twice.

Chassis and stock ergonomics

The stock or aluminum chassis links the barreled action to your body and to your support gear. A poorly fitted stock forces muscle tension into every shot, opening groups and hiding what the ammunition can do.

  • Length of pull (LOP). LOP is the distance from the face of the trigger to the center of the buttpad. If LOP is too long, you stretch your neck and cant the rifle; if too short, your face crowds the scope ocular. Set LOP in your primary shooting position (usually prone) so your firing hand reaches the trigger straight back without strain.
  • Cheek weld (comb height). Adjust the cheek piece so your cheekbone rests firmly on the stock with your eye naturally centered behind the scope. You should see a full, clear sight picture the moment your head settles, without craning your neck up or scrunching down.
  • Balance point and discipline stock rules. How weight and forend shape are configured depends on the discipline. Benchrest and F-Open rifles (up to 22 lb, with a 3-inch flat forend riding on a coaxial mechanical front rest and an eared rear bag; muzzle brakes banned) are balanced to ride flat and track straight back in their bags. F-TR (F-Class Target Rifle) rifles are limited to 18.18 lb, chambered in .223 Remington or .308 Winchester, supported by a front bipod and rear bag (muzzle brakes also banned), and balanced to recoil straight back into the shoulder. PRS and positional rifles are often weighted along the forend so the balance point sits two to four inches ahead of the magazine well. A rifle balanced there rests neutrally on a barricade bag without tipping muzzle-high or muzzle-low.
  • Mounting interfaces (Arca-Swiss and M-LOK). A full-length Arca-Swiss dovetail rail along the bottom of the forend lets a bipod, tripod clamp, or bag rider slide quickly to the exact point needed for a barricade or terrain slope. M-LOK slots along the sides and bottom of the forend let you add internal or external weights, sling mounts, or short rail sections cleanly without clamping onto the barrel.

Rifle mass and the TOP Gun Principle

Heavy rifles are easier to shoot precisely than light rifles. More mass slows recoil velocity, reducing how far the rifle moves during the millisecond the bullet travels down the bore and making small flaws in follow-through less punishing.

The TOP Gun Principle (Theory of Precision) expresses this relationship as a simple rule of thumb linking a rifle’s expected average group size to the ratio of cartridge muzzle kinetic energy to rifle weight:

Muzzle KE (ft-lbs) = (bullet_weight_gr × MV_fps²) / 450,437
Expected group size (MOA) ≈ Muzzle KE (ft-lbs) / (200 × rifle_weight_lbs)

Worked example. Example numbers.

Compare a 7-pound hunting rifle and a 16-pound match rifle, both firing a 140 gr bullet at 2,750 fps.

Muzzle KE = (140 × 2,750²) / 450,437 = 2,350 ft-lbs

7-pound rifle:  2,350 / (200 × 7)  = 1.68 MOA
16-pound rifle: 2,350 / (200 × 16) = 0.73 MOA

The lighter rifle carries more easily in the mountains, yet physics gives the heavier rifle a clear precision advantage with the exact same cartridge. Note that the TOP Gun Principle models centerfire recoil-limited systems and does not apply to .22 LR rimfire rifles, where recoil energy is negligible and precision is dominated by ammunition lot quality and chamber fit.

Evidence · Supported by data

Large-sample firing data across hundreds of rifles and cartridges confirms that heavier rifles firing lower-recoil cartridges consistently average smaller groups than lightweight rifles firing heavy magnums.24 Treat the formula as a baseline expectation for multi-group average precision rather than a hard physical limit; barrel quality, ammunition consistency, and shooter skill still move any individual rifle above or below the estimate.

Bedding, rings and trigger

The action, stock, scope, and trigger work as one system, and every joint is a place where something can shift. A thin witness line of paint across each screw head onto its seat reveals movement at a glance. Check that a free-floating barrel clears the stock along its full length under load, and record the trigger pull weight if you have a gauge.

A loose scope shows up quickly on target. If a rifle prints a 6-inch 3-shot group at 100 yards, fix the hardware or ammunition fault before testing loads; if replacing rings still leaves a 5-inch spread, the rings were not the cause. Example numbers. Not load data.

The hardware baseline

A baseline records how the rifle shoots in one fixed setup. Changing the scope, rings, stock, trigger, or muzzle device voids the baseline—never swap hardware mid-test, or you cannot tell whether the hardware or the ammunition moved the group.

  1. Finish all hardware work (scope, rings, base, stock, bedding, trigger, muzzle device) before testing.
  2. Torque every fastener to spec with a torque driver (see Chapter 7), mark each screw with witness paint, and record the values.
  3. Log the full setup and date in the rifle record, then shoot a baseline (see Chapter 14).
  4. Leave the hardware untouched until the test ends; if anything changes or shifts, close the old entry and shoot a new baseline (see Chapter 13).

Record the rifle

Record every detail in the rifle log (see Chapter 13): cartridge stamped on the barrel; barrel length, twist rate and direction, sight height, and magazine length; distance to the lands (if handloading) for each bullet (see The Handloading Handbook); barrel maker, rifling method, contour, finish, and round count; and scope, rings, base, stock, trigger, muzzle device, and torque values.
