Chapter 7. Range and Field Equipment

Key points

  • Every chronograph adds its own noise to each reading. A noisy unit can hide a real difference between loads.
  • For load testing, prefer Doppler radar. Light and weather do not affect it.
  • A barrel-mounted magnetic unit can shift point of impact. Test it before you shoot groups with it on.
  • A steady rest keeps the shooter out of the groups. A spotting scope or a target camera pairs each hole with its reading.
  • A pocket weather meter and a thermometer in the ammunition box record the conditions that move velocity and drop.

Why it matters

You judge every load by the data you collect at the range. Your range gear decides how much of each result comes from the load and how much comes from outside noise. A noisy chronograph blurs the velocity spread. An unsteady rest blurs the group.

Conditions you cannot control still have to be recorded. Air temperature, station pressure and ammunition temperature all change from one range trip to the next. Readings taken at every session let you see their effect later.

How it is done

Chronographs

Velocity data is only as good as the instrument. Every chronograph adds its own error to each reading. That error adds to the true spread of your ammunition. The two combine like the sides of a right triangle.

measured SD = √(true SD² + instrument SD²)

For example, ammunition with a true SD of 5 fps shows 15.8 fps when read through 15 fps of instrument noise.1

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

Your load has a true SD of 10 fps.

Noisy unit, 15 fps of noise:  √(10² + 15²) = √325 = 18.0 fps
Good unit, 3 fps of noise:    √(10² + 3²)  = √109 = 10.4 fps

Through the noisy unit, a 5 fps load reads 15.8 fps and a 10 fps load reads 18.0 fps. The true SD doubled, but the reading moved about 2 fps. A noisy chronograph cannot tell a good load from an ordinary one (see Figure 7-1).

If you know your instrument’s noise, you can take it back out.

true SD = √(measured SD² − instrument SD²)
Example: √(12² − 3²) = √135 = 11.6 fps

Use this only with a noise figure you trust. A guessed figure gives a guessed answer.

There are three common chronograph types used in precision shooting. For load testing, prefer Doppler radar.

TypeHow it readsPractical considerations
Doppler radarTracks the bullet by radar from beside the muzzlePoints along the bullet’s path. Light and weather do not affect it.
OpticalThe bullet passes over light sensors in front of the muzzleSunlight, cloud shadows, poor alignment and muzzle blast all change readings.
MagneticSenses the bullet from a bayonet mounted on the barrelHangs extra weight off the barrel.

A barrel-mounted magnetic unit can shift your point of impact and change barrel harmonics and group size. The effect varies from rifle to rifle.2 You can leave the unit on during a work-up, because a work-up checks velocity, not group size. Before you shoot groups with a barrel-mounted unit attached, test the rifle with the unit on and off in interleaved strings (see Chapter 16, “Testing any claim yourself”).3 Until you prove it does not change your groups, remove it for group shooting and chronograph separate strings. When you shoot groups and velocities in separate strings, you cannot pair each bullet hole with its velocity to check vertical spread against speed (see Chapter 24).

Evidence · Supported by data

In one test, optical units with screens less than 2 feet apart had average errors of 13 to 55 fps. An average error moves a drop chart and the charge at which you reach the velocity ceiling, not the SD. Two of those units added only about 4 fps of noise, and the other two added far more.1 In another test, two Doppler units agreed within about 1 to 5 fps.4

Set up and check the unit at every session (see Chapter 14).

Rests, bags, tripods, and slings

A steady support isolates the rifle and ammunition from shooter wobble. Match your support gear to the job you are doing—pure cartridge evaluation demands different hardware than positional match shooting.

  • Benchrest front rests and rear bags. A heavy cast-iron or aluminum pedestal front rest with a coaxial or windage-adjustable top, paired with a heavy sand-filled rabbit-ear rear bag, lets the rifle ride straight back under recoil with minimal shooter input. This is the cleanest setup on a shooting bench for testing ammunition dispersion and comparing loads (see Chapter 14).
  • Bipods. A bipod mounts to the forend via a swivel stud, Picatinny rail, or Arca-Swiss clamp and supports the front of the rifle in prone and field positions. Look for rigid legs with minimal play, a cant lock you can tighten easily, and interchangeable feet (rubber for concrete benches, spiked or clawed feet for dirt and grass). Load the bipod forward lightly and consistently so the rifle recoils straight back rather than hopping sideways (see Chapter 8).
  • Rear bags and barricade bags. In prone shooting, a compressible rear squeeze bag under the toe of the stock lets your support hand adjust elevation smoothly without muscling the rifle. For PRS stages and hunting over natural cover, a flat or waxed-canvas barricade bag filled with heavy sand or polymer beads drapes over fence posts, boulders, or props, absorbing vibration and giving a dead, non-bouncing platform (see Chapter 9).
  • Tripods. A sturdy carbon-fiber or aluminum tripod fitted with a ball head or leveling base and an Arca-Swiss clamp lets you shoot standing, kneeling, or seated over tall grass and uneven terrain. The same tripod stabilizes spotting scopes, binoculars, and laser rangefinders for steady long-range observation.
  • Service Rifle slings. In Highpower Service Rifle and traditional positional matches, a leather or web loop sling links your upper support arm to the forend swivel, transferring the rifle’s weight onto bone structure rather than muscle. Use a free-floated handguard so tight sling tension cannot bend the barrel and shift your zero.

Spotting scopes and target cameras

A spotting scope or target camera lets you see each bullet hole from the firing line. Use it to match each impact on paper with its chronograph reading in your log (see Chapter 14). If you call a bad shot, write down your call before you look at the target (see Chapter 15).

Laser rangefinders

An accurate distance is essential before you dial elevation, especially past 400 yards where a 20-yard range error can cause a clean miss. A laser rangefinder (LRF) emits pulses of infrared light and times their return to calculate distance.

Every LRF has beam divergence—the beam widens into a rectangle or oval, commonly 1 to 3 MIL wide by 0.5 to 1.5 MIL tall (6 feet wide at 1,000 yards for a 2 MIL beam). Overlapping foreground brush or a background hillside can return a false distance.

  • Brace the unit. Rest the rangefinder on a tripod, shooting bag, or rifle forend when ranging small targets past 500 yards. Handheld wobble spreads the beam across background terrain.
  • Know your beam alignment. The beam may not sit dead center in the aiming box; sweep slowly across an isolated pole or sign at 300 to 500 yards from all four sides to map where it triggers.
  • Verify suspect readings. Take three readings on the target and compare them. If readings jump between two distances, switch to near/first-target mode if available, or cross-check the distance by milling the target with your scope reticle (see Chapter 11).

Weather meters, pressure, and density altitude

A pocket weather meter records the air conditions that control aerodynamic drag and bullet drop (see Chapter 14 and Chapter 19).

  • Station vs. barometric pressure. Station pressure is the actual uncorrected pressure where you stand, dropping with altitude to reflect true air density. Barometric pressure (from airports and forecasts) is adjusted to sea level. Mixing the two in a ballistic solver causes large high-altitude elevation errors (see Chapter 20); always match meter and solver pressure modes.
  • Density altitude (DA). DA combines station pressure, temperature, and humidity into one standard-atmosphere altitude: high DA (hot, high, low pressure) means thinner air and less drop; low DA (cold, low, high pressure) means denser air and more drop. DA suits printed range cards, though raw inputs are more exact in a solver.
  • Ammunition temperature. Air temperature moves drag, while powder temperature moves muzzle velocity. Keep your test ammunition in an insulated box with a thermometer inside, and log its temperature at every session (see Chapter 23).

Torque drivers, witness paint and scope levels

A torque driver sets fasteners to spec, and a paint-pen witness line across each screw head onto its seat shows instantly if a screw turns—keeping the hardware baseline fixed (see Chapter 4). Level the reticle to a plumb line at mounting, then check the scope level on every shot (see Chapters 6 and 20).
