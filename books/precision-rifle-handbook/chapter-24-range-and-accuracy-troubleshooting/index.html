Chapter 24. Range and Accuracy Troubleshooting

On this page (5 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • Problems at the range
  • Common questions
  • Notes

Key points

  • Unusual force is a symptom. More force is never the fix. A pressure sign is never noise, and its absence is not proof of safety.
  • Change one thing at a time, then measure enough shots to confirm what fixed the problem.
  • Check mechanical and optic causes first: loose rings, base, or action screws, parallax error, cant, and turret tracking error masquerade as load problems.
  • Small samples mislead, so many range faults are only noise. Judge stringing and far shots against chance.
  • Vertical stringing is a velocity problem only when the slow shots are the low ones. At 100 yards it almost never is.

Why it matters

A fault at the range costs ammunition, barrel life, and trust in your data. Worse, shooters often blame a good load for a loose scope ring, a parallax error, a carbon ring in the chamber neck or rimfire leade, or normal small-sample scatter.

Most range problems trace to one mechanical, optical, environmental, or ammunition cause. A fix that changes two things at once teaches you nothing. If the fault comes back, you will not know which change mattered. At the range, some faults are real and others are noise from too few shots. The skill is telling the two apart. One fault is never noise: a pressure sign. For workshop-only reloading faults during sizing, priming, charging, or seating, see The Handloading Handbook.

How it is done

Problems at the range

  1. Stop at once at a pressure sign or at a shot that sounds or feels wrong. After a wrong shot, unload and check the bore for a stuck bullet before you fire again (see Chapter 2). If a round fails to fire, keep the muzzle downrange for at least 30 seconds before you open the action.1 Everything else can wait. Pressure signs are unreliable early warnings, and their absence does not prove a load safe (see The Handloading Handbook).
  2. Look at the target. A flyer may be a hole marked in the wrong place or a shot you pulled.
  3. Count your shots. Read every number with its interval.
  4. Find the symptom in the table below. Change one thing. Then shoot enough rounds to see whether the change worked (see Chapter 16).
  5. Record the conditions, the change and the result.

Stringing

Compare the spread up and down with the spread side to side. Vertical much larger than horizontal is vertical stringing. Suspect velocity spread or uneven primer strikes from a weak firing-pin spring. A barrel touching the stock does it too (see Chapter 4). Horizontal much larger than vertical points at wind, the bipod or trigger push.

Judge stringing against chance, not against a fixed ratio. A rule that calls a group strung whenever one axis is 1.5 times the other would call almost half of all round 5-shot groups strung.6 Use the lines in Table 24-1 instead. A round group is then called strung about 1 time in 20.

  1. Find SD horizontal and SD vertical from the group’s center, as in the mean radius worked example (see Chapter 15).
  2. Divide the larger SD by the smaller.
  3. Call the group strung only when the ratio reaches the line for your shots.

For a composite, measure each shot from its own group’s center. Then divide each sum of squares by the shots minus the number of groups, not by the shots minus 1.

Table 24-1. When to call a group strung, and when to call velocity linked to height.

ShotsStrung when the larger SD ÷ the smaller SD reachesVelocity linked to height when the correlation reaches

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data.

The 10-shot group in Chapter 15 has an SD of .33 inch across and .25 inch up and down. The ratio is .33 ÷ .25 = 1.3, well short of 2.01. That group is not strung.

Next, ask whether velocity is causing the vertical.

  1. Take each shot’s velocity from the string’s average (dv) and its height from the group’s center (dh). In a composite, take both from each group’s own averages.
  2. Work out the correlation between the two. CORREL of the two columns in a spreadsheet gives the same number.
correlation = Σ(dv × dh) ÷ √(Σdv² × Σdh²)
  1. If the correlation is positive and reaches the line in Table 24-1, the slow shots are the low ones. Velocity is moving your vertical, and a lower SD will tighten it. Below the line, a lower SD is not shown to help this group.

Distance matters a great deal here. Take a 140 gr 6.5 Creedmoor load at 2,700 fps. Example numbers. Not load data. There, 10 fps of velocity moves the bullet about .18 inch at 300 yards. At 1,000 yards it moves it about 3 inches.7 At 100 yards velocity explains almost none of the vertical.6 Look at the rifle, the rest and the barrel heat first.

Check the drop from gravity, not only the correlation. A positive correlation alone does not mean velocity caused a shot to land low. In a short string, a slow shot can happen to land low purely by chance, producing a high correlation coefficient ($r$). Always check the physical drop against what gravity actually does.

The change in bullet drop from a velocity change $\Delta v$ is:

drop change = drop(v + Δv) − drop(v)

or approximately from time of flight $t$ and total gravity drop from the bore axis $y$:

drop change ≈ −2 × y × (Δv ÷ v)

At 100 yards with the example 6.5 Creedmoor load, a shot 20 fps slow has its gravity drop increased by only about .04 inch. If that shot printed .75 inch below the group center, velocity explains only .04 inch of that displacement (about 5 percent). The other .71 inch came from rifle dispersion, aim, cant or rest movement. When the observed vertical displacement is several times larger than the ballistic drop, velocity did not cause the vertical error, even if the correlation happens to be positive.

This is also why real groups almost always remain circular. If velocity variation were a primary driver of vertical dispersion, targets would routinely stretch into vertical ellipses. When your vertical and horizontal spreads are roughly equal (the ratio in Table 24-1 is below the line), velocity variation is not the limiting factor.

From neck tension to the target (for handloaders). You can apply the same correlation formula to test the full chain: neck tension (or seating force) to muzzle velocity, and velocity to target height. If you log seating force at the bench and match each round to its chronograph reading, compute the correlation between seating force and velocity. You will usually find very little correlation across normal neck interference. And because velocity explains very little vertical dispersion inside 300 to 600 yards, neck tension variation rarely reaches the target at all.

Flyers

Every committed shot counts (see Chapter 15). A wide shot is often just normal dispersion: about 14 shots in 100 land beyond two standard deviations, so a 30-shot composite holds about four (see Figure 24-3).

Judge a suspected flyer against the average radial distance of all other shots (excluding the farthest prevents it from inflating the yardstick):

  1. Measure each shot’s distance from its group center (see Chapter 15), average all except the farthest, and divide the farthest distance by that average.
  2. Flag it as a far shot worth investigating only when the ratio exceeds 3.

This threshold holds for 5- to 30-shot groups and composites (testing the single farthest shot once against all others). Even then, ~1 clean target in 20 flags by chance. Investigate causes (such as a loose scope mount), but keep the shot unless you documented the error when fired.

Worked example. Example numbers. Not load data. In Chapter 15’s group, shot 9 is farthest at .711 inch; the other nine total 2.888 inches (average .321 inch). The ratio .711 ÷ .321 = 2.2 (< 3) marks shot 9 as normal tail dispersion.

Common questions

QuestionShort answerSee
Should I choose a MIL or MOA scope?Either works if reticle and turrets match. MIL (0.1 mrad) simplifies decimal math and dominates practical rifle; MOA (1/4 or 1/8 MOA) gives finer steps for F-Class and benchrest.3, 5
Should I choose a First Focal Plane (FFP) or Second Focal Plane (SFP) scope?FFP keeps subtensions true at all magnifications (ideal for PRS and hunting); SFP keeps reticle thickness constant at max power (preferred for F-Class and benchrest).5
How do I know if my scope turrets track accurately?Run a 100-yard Tall Target Test along a plumb line: dial a known value (e.g., 10 MIL or 30 MOA), compare actual to dialed shift, and enter any correction factor in your solver.6, 20
How many shots do I need to evaluate a factory lot or handload?5–10 shots rule out a bad load; proving a good load takes a 20–30-shot composite; separating two close loads takes 35+ shots per load.15, 16
My rifle shot a tiny group last week and a big one today. What changed?Maybe nothing—one of two 5-shot groups from the same load is ≥1.5× larger 30% of the time. Check ring and action torque, then shoot a larger composite.15, 16, 24
What velocity SD is good enough?Depends on cartridge, target, range, and wind. Inside 300 yards, a 15 fps SD adds ~0.3″ vertical in centerfire but ~4–5″ in subsonic .22 LR; 1,000-yard F-Class uses 5–7 fps to protect the 10/X-ring.15, 21
Why do my drops match at 100 yards and miss at 800 yards?Short range hides velocity, BC, sight-height, and tracking errors. Verify zero, sight height, and tracking first; true velocity supersonically before adjusting drag near Mach 0.9.6, 20
Should I use G1 or G7 ballistic coefficients?Use G7 for sleek boat-tail bullets, G1 for centerfire flat-bases, and RA4 or custom Doppler curves for .22 LR (where G1 diverges downrange). Never mix G1 BCs into G7 settings.19
Do I need to handload to shoot a precision rifle well?No. Modern factory match ammunition often meets practical hit-probability goals out of the box. Test by lot with real sample sizes and buy winning lots in bulk.12, 16, 23
How do I tune a .22 LR rimfire rifle if I cannot handload?Structured lot testing across match brands/lots (interleaving same-brand lots; patching and seasoning between different waxes) is primary. Track cold-bore shift, clean chamber/carbon ring, and avoid over-cleaning the bore.12, 14, 16, 23
How do I set my ethical maximum hunting range?Take the shorter of two limits: where impact velocity drops to the bullet’s minimum expansion velocity, or where field-position vital-zone hit probability falls below 90%.21, 22
When is the cosine rule accurate for uphill or downhill shots?Within ~0.1–0.2 MIL under 20–25° and 400–500 yards. For steeper angles or longer ranges, enter slant range and look angle directly into a solver.11, 20
Why is my new barrel speeding up, and when should I finalize my DOPE?New barrels smooth out and typically gain 20–80 fps over their first 100–200 rounds. Wait for velocity to stabilize before finalizing long-range DOPE.20, 23
How often should I clean my barrel, and should I clean to bare metal?Clean on a consistent round-count cadence guided by target and velocity logs, keeping the chamber neck (or rimfire mouth/leade) free of carbon rings. Stripping to bare metal every trip adds no proven benefit and requires re-fouling.14, 23
