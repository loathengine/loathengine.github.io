Chapter 9. Alternate and Positional Shooting

On this page (7 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • Sitting, kneeling, and standing
  • Rapid-fire positional cadence
  • Barricades, props, packs, and tripods

Key points

  • Positional shooting trades raw stability for speed and field adaptability.
  • Triangulate support using slings, bags, packs, barricades, and tripods to shrink your wobble zone.
  • Never rest a bare barrel directly on a hard prop; pad the forend to prevent recoil hop and zero shift.
  • Dry-fire practice isolates trigger and position faults without burning barrel life.

Why it matters

Matches (PRS, Service Rifle) and ethical hunting rarely offer a flat concrete shooting bench or a level firing point. Every step off the ground raises your center of gravity and widens your wobble zone. Measuring and shrinking positional wobble tells you what targets you can hit reliably and expands your practical effective range.

How it is done

Positional shooting follows the same rules as prone shooting: use bone support instead of muscle, build triangles of contact, and accept your natural wobble instead of snatching the trigger.

Sitting, kneeling, and standing

Unsupported and sling-supported field positions follow a clear stability order: prone is most stable, followed by sitting, kneeling, and standing. Choose the lowest position that lets you clear grass, brush, and terrain while keeping a clean view of the target.

Sitting. Sitting provides a wide, low base with two contact points on the ground (your hips and feet) and two contact points supporting the rifle (both arms on your legs). Common variations include crossed-ankle, crossed-leg, and open-leg sitting. In every variation, avoid placing the bony point of your elbow directly on the bony tip of your kneecap. Bone-on-bone point contact rolls and wobbles. Instead, place the flat of your upper arm or triceps just behind the elbow against the flat inside of your shin or thigh, or rest your forearm across the top of your knee. Lean slightly forward so your torso weight locks the position into the ground.

Kneeling. Kneeling gets you above tall grass faster than sitting. In low kneeling, sit back on the heel or side of your rear boot while your support foot sits flat on the ground in front of you. Place the flat of your support arm just forward of your kneecap so the arm locks over the leg without bone-on-bone rolling. High kneeling (hips raised off the rear heel) is quicker to enter and higher for clearing obstacles, but it introduces much more horizontal sway.

Standing (offhand). Standing removes all lower-body bracing and leaves the largest wobble zone. Turn your body roughly 60 to 90 degrees off the target line (a bladed stance) with your feet about shoulder-width apart. Tuck your support elbow tightly against your ribcage or hip so your forearm forms a vertical bone column under the forend. Shift your hips slightly toward the target to counterbalance the rifle’s weight.

In standing, the reticle will never stop moving completely. Do not try to ambush the bullseye by jerking the trigger as the reticle flashes past center. Instead, settle into your natural wobble zone centered on the target, build steady rearward trigger pressure while the reticle drifts inside the scoring zone, and let the shot break smoothly.

Service Rifle sling support. A properly fitted loop sling turns your support arm, torso, and rifle into a rigid triangle. Put the sling loop high on your upper arm, above the bicep and tight enough that it cannot slip down toward the elbow. Twist the sling so it lies flat across the back of your support hand, then adjust the length until the sling carries the weight of the rifle without your bicep doing the work. Check your NPOA by closing your eyes and relaxing; adjust your whole body if the reticle settles off target.

For quick field shots where a loop sling is too slow, a hasty sling (wrapping the support arm once around a standard carry strap and pulling it snug against the back of the hand) adds meaningful stability in a few seconds.

Rapid-fire positional cadence

Across-the-Course Highpower Service Rifle matches test your ability to drop from standing into a sling-supported position, verify NPOA, execute a clean reload under the clock, and fire 10 aimed shots without rushing the trigger:

  • 200-yard rapid-fire sitting: Start standing with a loaded 2-round magazine, drop into sitting (or kneeling), fire 2 rounds, reload an 8-round magazine, and fire the remaining 8 rounds—10 rounds total in 60 seconds.
  • 300-yard rapid-fire prone: Start standing with a 2-round magazine, drop into prone, fire 2 rounds, reload an 8-round magazine, and fire the remaining 8 rounds—10 rounds total in 70 seconds.

Consistency under time pressure comes from mechanical preparation rather than frantic speed. Before the command to load, pre-set your loop sling on your upper arm and index your foot or mat placement so your body lands aligned with the target. Drop smoothly into position, take a brief beat to check NPOA and shift your hips if needed, and break the first 2 shots cleanly. Execute the 2+8 reload without breaking your support-elbow anchor or sling tension, re-acquire your sight picture, and settle into an even rhythm of roughly one shot every 3 to 4 seconds. Dry-firing the full drop, NPOA check, and 2+8 reload sequence builds a repeatable cadence that leaves 10 to 15 seconds to spare.

Barricades, props, packs, and tripods

Practical precision matches and field hunts often let you rest the rifle on a fence post, roof ridge, rock, tank trap, pack, or tripod. A prop improves stability only if you build a solid structure around it.

Building triangles of support. A rifle resting only on a front barricade pivots like a seesaw. Every twitch of your firing shoulder moves the muzzle. Add a second point of contact whenever time and terrain allow:

  • Rest your firing elbow on a knee, hip, or lower rail of the barricade.
  • Trap a rear bag between the buttstock and a lower prop surface, or between your firing arm and ribcage.
  • Run a sling under tension from the forend to your body or foot to pull the rifle down into the prop.

Each added support point closes a triangle and shrinks your wobble zone.

Managing bag fill. A shooting bag bridges the gap between a hard, irregular prop and the rifle’s forend. Heavy, dense fills (such as sand or fine glass beads) conform tightly to sharp edges and absorb recoil energy, reducing bounce. Lighter fills (such as polymer pellets) save weight when carrying gear in the mountains, though they shift slightly more under recoil.

Do not overfill a barricade bag until it feels like a hard brick. A bag filled to roughly 75 to 85 percent capacity drapes over rails, branches, and angled boards, forming a flat, wide shelf for the rifle stock to settle into.

Minimizing recoil hop on awkward props. Never rest a bare barrel directly on wood, steel, rock, or a hard pack frame. A barrel vibrates during firing and bounces sharply away from a hard contact point, causing erratic point-of-impact shifts and vertical stringing. Always keep the stock forend, or a padded bag between the stock and the prop, as the sole contact point.

Align your body as straight behind the rifle as the prop permits. Avoid leaning your full body weight sideways into an unstable barricade or pushing hard forward on a loose fence rail. Let the rifle’s weight settle naturally into the bag, build even cheek and shoulder contact, and press the trigger straight back.

Packs and tripods. A firm field pack laid flat on the ground works well as an improvised prone or seated front rest. Cinch the straps tight so the pack does not roll under recoil, and rest the rifle’s forend, never the barrel, across the center of the pack.

A sturdy tripod lets you shoot cleanly from standing, kneeling, or seated positions over tall brush or across steep slopes. You can clamp the rifle directly into a tripod head using a rail adapter or saddle, or rest a flat support bag on top of a wide tripod platform. Set the tripod legs wide enough for stability, and point one leg directly toward the target so it braces straight against rearward recoil. Hang your pack lightly from the center hook only if wind is not swinging the pack like a pendulum.

Dry-fire diagnostic protocols

Dry fire lets you test your position, trigger press, and wobble zone hundreds of times at zero ammunition cost and zero barrel wear.

Safety · See Chapter 2

Before every dry-fire session, remove the magazine, open the action, and physically and visually inspect the chamber to confirm the rifle is empty. Remove all live ammunition from the room or shooting bench. Point the muzzle only at a safe backstop that can stop a bullet. On rimfire rifles, use an inert snap cap or dry-fire block if the maker warns that dry-firing on an empty chamber can peen the breech face.

Use four structured protocols to diagnose specific faults:

  1. NPOA and reticle settle check. Build your prone, sitting, kneeling, or barricade position on a small, high-contrast aim point (such as a 1-inch dot on a wall or a downrange target). Close your eyes, exhale to your natural respiratory pause, and open your eyes. If the reticle moved off the dot, adjust your body or support gear, not your arms, until it returns to the dot automatically.
  2. Trigger break isolation. Watch the reticle closely as you press the trigger through the break. In a clean break, the reticle does not twitch when the firing pin falls. A twitch down and left (for a right-handed shooter) usually signals anticipation (flinch) or tightening the whole firing hand. A twitch right or left signals side pressure from the trigger finger or a loose rear bag. Repeat until ten consecutive dry breaks show zero reticle jump.
  3. Wobble-zone measurement. Get into a positional hold (sitting, kneeling, standing, or on a barricade) and look through your scope at a target of known distance or a scaled dry-fire target. Use your reticle’s MIL or MOA hash marks to measure the full width and height of your reticle’s movement during your natural respiratory pause. Record that angular wobble diameter in your log (see Chapter 13). Comparing your measured wobble zone to the angular size of a target tells you honestly whether a shot is high-percentage or a gamble (see Chapter 21).
  4. Ball-and-dummy live-fire check. At the range, have a partner mix one inert dummy round randomly into a magazine of live rounds (or single-feed rounds into the chamber without letting you see which one is inert). Fire the string normally. When the firing pin clicks on the dummy round instead of firing, any flinch, shoulder dip, or blink shows up immediately in the scope.
Evidence · Supported by data

Dry-fire diagnostics and ball-and-dummy drills separate mechanical rifle dispersion from shooter-induced trigger and flinch errors. Tracking positional wobble in angular units gives an objective measure of positional skill before live ammunition is fired.1
