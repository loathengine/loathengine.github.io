Chapter 6. Scope Mounting, Leveling, and Mechanical Verification

On this page (5 sections)
  • Why it matters
  • How it is done
  • Step-by-step mounting and torque
  • Leveling the reticle to gravity
  • The Tall Target Test

Key points

  • Mount the scope to your natural shooting position, not the other way around.
  • Level the reticle to gravity using a plumb line; cant turns elevation into horizontal error at distance.
  • Torque fasteners to spec and mark them with witness paint.
  • Run a Tall Target Test to measure your scope’s true click value (tracking factor) before truing a solver.

Why it matters

A 2-degree cant or a 3-percent turret tracking error looks invisible at 100 yards and causes systematic misses at 800 yards.

How it is done

Step-by-step mounting and torque

Safety · See Chapter 2

Unload the rifle, remove the magazine, and visually and physically verify the chamber is empty before mounting or adjusting an optic.

Mount the scope to your natural shooting position rather than forcing your neck to adapt to a poorly placed scope.

  1. Degrease threads and contact surfaces. Wipe the receiver’s mounting holes, base screws, and ring surfaces with isopropyl alcohol or acetone. Oil on threads acts as a lubricant and raises clamping force for a given torque reading, which can strip screws or stress the tube.
  2. Install the base or rail. Set the rail onto the receiver and press down on each end in turn. If the rail rocks, have it bedded so tightening does not warp the action. Use blue removable threadlocker only if the maker calls for it, and follow their dry or wet torque specification. Tighten base screws evenly from the center outward with a calibrated torque driver.
  3. Seat the lower ring halves. Place the lower rings into their Picatinny slots and push each ring firmly forward against the front shoulder of its slot while tightening the cross-bolts to spec. Pushing the rings forward prevents recoil from sliding the mount forward during firing.
  4. Set eye relief in your natural position. Lay the scope in the lower rings and snug the top caps just tight enough that the scope can slide and rotate under gentle hand pressure. Set the scope to maximum magnification, where the eye box is tightest. Get into your primary shooting position (usually prone), close your eyes, settle your cheek naturally on the comb, and open your eyes. Slide the scope forward or backward until you see a full, edge-to-edge sight picture with no black shadow ring.
  5. Focus the ocular diopter. Turn the parallax knob to infinity and point the unloaded rifle at a plain overcast sky or bright blank wall. Glance through the scope for one to two seconds at a time and adjust the diopter ring until the reticle snaps into razor-sharp focus the instant your eye opens. Do not stare continuously while turning the ring; your eye muscles will strain to focus a blurry reticle and mask the true setting. Lock or witness-mark the diopter ring.
  6. Level the reticle to gravity (see “Leveling the reticle to gravity” below).
  7. Torque the ring caps evenly. Tighten the cap screws in an X-pattern in small alternating increments while keeping the gaps on the left and right sides of each ring equal. Stop at the scope maker’s specification (commonly 15 to 20 inch-pounds). Over-torquing crushes the main tube, pinches the erector assembly, and causes erratic tracking.
  8. Apply witness paint. Draw a thin line of paint across every base screw, cross-bolt, and ring cap screw onto its adjacent metal seat (see Chapter 4). Check those lines before every range session.

Leveling the reticle to gravity

Gravity pulls a bullet straight down toward the earth. If your reticle is canted (tilted sideways) relative to gravity, dialing elevation tilts the barrel slightly sideways as well as upward. At 100 yards, a small cant is invisible. At 800 to 1,000 yards, it walks impacts systematically off the side of the target.

Do not rely solely on laying a small level across the top elevation turret cap or wedging feeler gauges under the flat bottom of the scope saddle. Outer housings and turret caps are not always machined perfectly square to the internal reticle. Leveling the reticle directly against a gravity plumb line eliminates that error.

  1. Hang a plumb line. Suspend a weighted string (a dark cord against a bright background, or a bright cord against a dark wall) 25 to 50 yards away so it hangs true to gravity.
  2. Level the rifle. Clamp the unloaded rifle in a steady vise or cradle and level the action using a machined flat surface on the receiver or Picatinny rail.
  3. Align the vertical crosshair. Look through the scope at the plumb line and rotate the scope tube until the vertical crosshair overlays the string cleanly from top to bottom.
  4. Torque and re-check. Tighten the ring caps in small alternating steps, checking the plumb line after each pass so tightening does not roll the tube.
  5. Zero your scope bubble level. Attach a rail- or tube-mounted anti-cant bubble level where you can see it with your non-aiming eye (or peripheral vision). Center the bubble while the reticle is locked onto the plumb line, then tighten the level mount and verify nothing shifted.

The math of cant error. When you dial elevation with a canted rifle, the bullet’s drop splits into a horizontal error and a small vertical error (see Figure 6-1):

horizontal_error = total_drop × sin(cant_angle)
vertical_error   = total_drop × (1 − cos(cant_angle))

Worked example. Example numbers.

A cartridge drops 10.0 MIL (360 inches) at 1,000 yards. The shooter cants the rifle by 3 degrees (sin(3°) = 0.0523, cos(3°) = 0.9986).

horizontal_error = 10.0 MIL × 0.0523       = 0.52 MIL (about 18.8 inches)
vertical_error   = 10.0 MIL × (1 − 0.9986) = 0.014 MIL (about 0.5 inch)

A 3-degree cant—barely perceptible without a bubble level—pushes the bullet nearly 19 inches sideways at 1,000 yards, enough to miss an 18-inch steel plate cleanly.

The Tall Target Test

A scope’s labeled click value (0.1 MIL or 1/4 MOA) is nominal; thread and erector tolerances commonly cause 1 to 4 percent tracking error. At 3 percent under-tracking, a 10.0 MIL dial at 1,000 yards moves only 9.7 MIL (nearly 11 inches low), which can mislead solver truing of MV or BC (see Chapter 20).

Run a Tall Target Test at 100 yards with a verified zero and a steady rest:

  1. Measure exact distance. Tape or LRF-measure from the elevation turret to the target face; assuming 100.0 yards when the berm is 2 yards off adds 2 percent error.
  2. Hang a plumb tall target. Staple a rigid target at least 40 inches tall, draw a vertical centerline, and plumb it with a level or plumb bob.
  3. Fire the lower group. Place an aiming dot near the bottom of the plumb line, center the bubble level, and fire 3 to 5 shots at zero.
  4. Dial up and fire the upper group. Dial 10.0 MIL (36.00″ at 100.0 yd) or 30.0 True MOA (31.42″ at 100.0 yd; 240 clicks on a 1/8 MOA F-Class/Benchrest scope, or 20.0 True MOA / 160 clicks / 20.94″ if travel is limited). Aim at the same bottom dot, center the bubble level, and fire 3 to 5 shots.
  5. Verify return to zero. Dial back to zero and fire 3 shots at the bottom dot; if they do not overlap the first group, the erector is sticking or the mount is slipping.
  6. Measure the shift. Measure the vertical distance (actual_shift) between lower and upper group centers along the plumb line, plus any sideways offset (horizontal_drift).

Compute the expected shift, the tracking factor, and any residual cant angle:

expected_shift (in) = dialed_MIL × 3.6 × (distance_yd / 100)
expected_shift (in) = dialed_True_MOA × 1.0472 × (distance_yd / 100)

tracking_factor = actual_shift / expected_shift
corrected_dial  = desired_correction / tracking_factor
cant_angle (deg) = arctan(horizontal_drift / actual_shift)

Worked example. Example numbers.

At a tape-measured distance of 100.0 yards, you dial up 10.0 MIL. The upper group center measures 34.92 inches above the lower group center and 0.60 inch to the right of the plumb line.

expected_shift  = 10.0 × 3.6 × (100.0 / 100) = 36.00 inches
tracking_factor = 34.92 / 36.00              = 0.970 (3.0% under-tracking)
cant_angle      = arctan(0.60 / 34.92)       = 0.98 degrees (about 1°)

For a 9.0 MIL solver drop, divide by the tracking factor: 9.0 / 0.970 = 9.28 MIL (dial 9.3 MIL). If cant exceeds 1 degree, re-check reticle and bubble-level alignment.

Rimfire (.22 LR) at 50 yards. Subsonic .22 LR wind drift at 100 yards can blur group centers and cant checks. Run the same test at a tape-measured 50.0 yards, where 10.0 MIL equals 18.00 inches and 30.0 True MOA equals 15.71 inches—cutting wind error and backer height while still testing full erector travel.
