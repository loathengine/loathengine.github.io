Chapter 15. Measurement Science for Shooters

Key points

  • Mean radius uses every shot. Extreme spread uses two, and it grows with the number of shots.
  • Distance from a group’s center follows a Rayleigh curve. About 39, 86 and 99 percent of shots land within 1, 2 and 3 single-axis SDs of the center.
  • Every number from a sample is an estimate. Its shot count and confidence interval show how far to trust it.
  • One small group is mostly luck. A composite of many groups tells you far more.
  • Every shot is data. Where a shot landed is never a reason to drop it.

Why it matters

A load decision is a measurement, and one 5-shot group is a very small sample. Suppose you shoot seven identical loads as one 5-shot group each. On average, the best group comes out at 0.66 times the load’s average 5-shot group, and the worst at 1.38 times. Pick the best of seven and you have picked luck.

Judging a load by its best group is like judging a whole pan of lasagna by one perfect corner square (see Chapter 1). The load is the whole pan.

Without a few statistical tools you chase noise. You change a charge by 0.1 gr because one group looked better, and the “best” load never repeats. With them you can say how big a group really is, how sure you are and how many more shots would settle the question (see Chapter 16).

How it is done

The terms

TermMeaning
POAPoint of aim: where you aimed.
POIPoint of impact: where a bullet struck, measured as x (right) and y (up) from the aim mark.
MPIMean point of impact: the average of all x values and of all y values. It is the group’s center.
ESExtreme spread: the center-to-center distance between the two shots farthest apart. For velocity, the fastest minus the slowest.
MRMean radius: the average distance of every shot from the MPI.
SDStandard deviation: how far a typical value sits from the average. You measure it for velocity and for each axis of a group.
nThe number of shots.
CIConfidence interval: the span the true value plausibly lies in, given the shots you have.
MOAMinute of angle. One MOA is about 1.047 inches at 100 yards.

Extreme spread and mean radius

Extreme spread measures the two widest shots and ignores every other shot. Mean radius measures every shot.

MPI x = (x1 + x2 + ... + xn) ÷ n
MPI y = (y1 + y2 + ... + yn) ÷ n
r     = √((x − MPI x)² + (y − MPI y)²)        for each shot
MR    = (r1 + r2 + ... + rn) ÷ n

ES has a second flaw. It grows with the number of shots, because every added shot is another chance to land wider than the widest pair so far. On average, the same load gives a larger ES over 10 shots than over 5. Never compare ES across different shot counts. Table 15-1 shows how fast velocity ES grows.

Table 15-1. Expected velocity ES, in units of SD.

ShotsExpected ESIf the true SD is 10 fps
5about 2.3 SDabout 23 fps
10about 3.1 SDabout 31 fps
30about 4.1 SDabout 41 fps

Example numbers. Not load data.

ES still has a job. It rules out a bad load or a broken rifle fast. If a rifle that should shoot 0.5 MOA groups prints a 6-inch 3-shot group at 100 yards, you need no more shots. Something is wrong. Check the scope mounts and the ammunition before you fire again. Adam MacDonald divides the labor this way: “Use extreme spread to rule out a bad load with 5 shots. Use standard deviation to prove a good load with confidence.”1

Velocity ES has a job too. One slow round can drop below a plate at long range. A shot far slower or faster than the rest of its string points at something mechanical, such as erratic ignition or uneven neck tension, or at a chronograph misread (see Chapter 24). Inspect that case. A blown primer is a pressure sign: stop (see The Handloading Handbook).

Evidence · Supported by data

Assuming normal scatter, group ES runs about 2.7 times the measured MR for 5 shots and 3.2 times for 10. Over more than 150 real groups, the measured ratio was 2.75.2 In a single 5-shot group, ES and MR are about equally noisy. MR pulls ahead as shots add up, and it pools across groups where ES cannot.

How shots scatter: normal and Rayleigh

Velocity has one dimension. Over many shots, velocities form a bell curve, the normal distribution. About 68 percent fall within 1 SD of the average, 95 percent within 2 SD and 99.7 percent within 3 SD. The same holds for the heights of shots on their own, or for their left-right positions on their own.

A group on paper has two dimensions. Each axis follows its own bell curve, but the distance from the center does not. A distance cannot be negative, so its curve starts at zero, rises to a peak and trails off to the right. This is the Rayleigh distribution, and its shares differ from the familiar ones. For a round group, with σ (sigma) the standard deviation along one axis:

Distance from the group’s centerShare of shots inside it
1σabout 39 percent
2σabout 86 percent
3σabout 99 percent
share of shots within z σ of the center = 1 − exp(−z² ÷ 2)
mean radius ≈ 1.25 × σ of one axis         (round group)

The 68, 95 and 99.7 percent rule does not apply to distance from the center. Applied there, it makes a normal group look as if it has flyers. About 14 percent of shots land beyond 2 SD, roughly one shot in seven. In a 10-shot group, one or two shots that far out are normal.

Velocity SD

average = (v1 + v2 + ... + vn) ÷ n
SD      = √( [(v1 − average)² + (v2 − average)² + ...
              + (vn − average)²] ÷ (n − 1) )

Divide by n − 1, not n. Because the sample average is calculated from those same shots, the shots sit slightly closer to the sample average than to the load’s true average. Dividing by n − 1 corrects that downward bias. The small-group correction below fixes the same bias for group size on paper.

Worked example. Five velocities: 2,795, 2,810, 2,802, 2,788 and 2,805 fps. Example numbers. Not load data.

  1. Average: 14,000 ÷ 5 = 2,800 fps.
  2. Differences from the average: −5, +10, +2, −12 and +5.
  3. Squares: 25, 100, 4, 144 and 25. Their sum is 298.
  4. Divide by n − 1, which is 4: 298 ÷ 4 = 74.5.
  5. Take the square root: the SD is 8.6 fps.

The ES is 2,810 − 2,788 = 22 fps, or about 2.6 SD. That is close to the 2.3 SD expected for 5 shots.

Two things inflate a measured SD.

  • Instrument error. The chronograph’s own scatter adds to the true velocity SD (see Chapter 7).
  • Pooling across days. Average velocity can shift from day to day, mostly with temperature. A single SD calculated across multiple days includes that day-to-day shift, so note if you report one. To measure within-session SD without day-to-day drift, subtract each session’s average velocity from the shots fired in that session. Square all those differences, add them together, divide by (total shots − number of sessions) and take the square root. Use (total shots − number of sessions) as the degrees of freedom in place of n − 1 in Appendix A.5.

There is no universal SD target. Set yours from hit probability at your distance (see Chapter 21).

Confidence intervals

Every SD or MR you measure is an estimate of the load’s true figure. A confidence interval says how far off the estimate could plausibly be. A 90 percent interval is built so that, over many tests, about 90 of every 100 such intervals contain the true value.

The intervals below are 90 percent. Use them to judge one load against a goal: the upper end of a 90 percent interval is a 95 percent upper bound. To compare two loads, use the wider 95 percent interval, the 0.05 level of the tests in Chapter 16 (see Appendix A.5 for both).

Table 15-2 shows how wide that uncertainty is for velocity SD. Multiply your measured SD by the two numbers to get the interval.

Table 15-2. The 90 percent interval for the true velocity SD, as a multiple of the SD you measured.

ShotsInterval
50.65 to 2.37
100.73 to 1.65
200.79 to 1.37
300.83 to 1.28

The multiples come from the chi-square distribution. It describes how far the SD of n normally distributed values tends to wander from the true SD. For 5 shots, n − 1 is 4, and the two formulas give 0.65 and 2.37.

low multiple  = SQRT((n-1)/CHISQ.INV(0.95, n-1))
high multiple = SQRT((n-1)/CHISQ.INV(0.05, n-1))

Apply the table to the 5-shot example. With 90 percent confidence, the true SD lies between 8.6 × 0.65 = 5.6 fps and 8.6 × 2.37 = 20.4 fps. That string could have come from excellent ammunition or from poor ammunition. Ten shots that measure 10 fps narrow the interval to 7.3 to 16.5 fps. Example numbers. Not load data.

Group size behaves the same way. The true dispersion behind one 5-shot group lies between 0.71 and 1.77 times its corrected mean radius (see “The small-group correction” below). Behind six 5-shot groups composited, it lies between 0.85 and 1.22 times. Appendix A.5 gives other layouts at 90 and 95 percent.

Intervals also tell you whether two loads are separated. If the intervals do not overlap, you have good evidence that the loads differ. If they overlap almost completely, these shots have not separated them yet. If they overlap a little, run the F-test in Chapter 16, which can still separate them. Overlap does not prove the loads are the same. A load at an MR of .35 inch and one at .38 inch, each from six 5-shot groups, have 95 percent intervals of .29 to .44 and .32 to .48 inch. They cannot yet be ranked. Fire more rounds (see Appendix A.5). Example numbers. Not load data.

Composite groups

A composite pools several groups into one measurement. Build one this way.

  1. Fire several groups of one load. Give each group its own aim mark.
  2. Work out each group’s own MPI from its own shots.
  3. Measure every shot from its own group’s MPI.
  4. If the groups were shot at different ranges, convert each shot’s distance from its MPI to an angle.
MOA = inches ÷ (1.047 × yards ÷ 100)
  1. Correct each shot’s distance for its own group’s shot count (k), with the factor in “The small-group correction” below.
  2. Pool all the corrected distances and average them. That is the composite MR.

Aligning the centers removes whatever moved each group as a whole, such as a change of zero or a steady wind. It keeps the spread, which is what you are measuring. Ten 5-shot groups composited give 50 shots, worth about 40 for measuring spread.

Keep every shot’s raw x and y. Never average old averages. An average of averages hides how many shots stand behind each number, so a 3-shot group counts as much as a 10-shot one.

Each group costs a little. Its center comes from its own shots, so a group of k shots tells you about as much about spread as k − 1 shots would. Twenty shots in one group pin the MR more closely than 20 shots in 10 groups.

Composite only what belongs together: the same rifle, the same load and the same hardware. Any hardware change starts a new baseline (see Chapter 4).

The small-group correction

A small group’s center comes from its own shots. So it sits closer to them than the load’s true center does, and distances measured from it read small on average. The effect is 18.4 percent for 3-shot groups, 10.6 percent for 5-shot groups and 5.1 percent for 10-shot groups.

corrected MR = measured MR × √(k ÷ (k − 1))
k            = shots in the group

Table 15-3. How far a group’s measured mean radius reads small, and the factor that corrects it.

Shots per group (k)Measured MR reads smallMultiply by
318.4%1.225
510.6%1.118
105.1%1.054

Without the correction, a load shot as many small groups looks tighter than the same load shot as a few large ones. The correction puts both on equal terms.

Every shot matters

Once you commit to the target, record every shot. Removing shots that spoil a group biases the result toward the answer you wanted. A wide shot can also carry information, such as a loose scope, a bad case or a hole marked in the wrong place. Every removed shot widens the interval. And exclusions that are not written down make your results impossible for anyone else to check or repeat.

Only two kinds of shot may be left out of the data.

Shots declared before firing. These include fouling shots after you clean the bore, sighters and warm-up rounds. Fire them at a separate aim mark or on a separate sheet, so they never mix with the test groups (see Chapter 14).

Shots with a documented shooter or equipment error. Examples are a rifle you felt jump off the rest, a shot that broke with the crosshair off the aim mark and a pulled trigger you called. Write the fault down as soon as you fire, before you look through a spotting scope or at the target.

Never leave out a shot because of where it landed. Calling an awkward shot an unexplained flyer only fools you.

To document an exclusion:

  1. Write down the shot number, the reason and when you noted it.
  2. Keep the shot in the raw record, marked with the part it leaves out: excluded group or excluded velocity (see Chapter 13).
  3. Run the analysis with and without it. If the conclusion changes, you need more shots, not fewer.
Evidence · Folklore

The claim is that a shot far from the rest is a flyer and does not belong to the load. In a round group, some shots land wide by chance alone (see “How shots scatter: normal and Rayleigh”). Being wide does not, on its own, take a shot out of the load.

  1. Measure each shot. For each hole, measure x to the right and y up from the aim mark to the center of the hole. Dial the offset so the group lands up and right of the aim mark (see Chapter 14). Then every number is positive.
  2. Find the MPI. The x values add to 14.00, and 14.00 ÷ 10 = 1.40. The y values add to 18.00, and 18.00 ÷ 10 = 1.80. The group’s center is 1.40 inches right of the aim mark and 1.80 inches above it.
  3. Find each shot’s offsets. dx = x − 1.40 and dy = y − 1.80. Each column of offsets adds to zero, which is a useful check.
  4. Square the offsets and add them. Work out dx² + dy² for each shot.
  5. Take the square root. That is the shot’s distance r from the center.
  6. Average the distances. 3.599 ÷ 10 = .360 inch. That is the measured mean radius.
  7. Correct for group size. This is one 10-shot group, so multiply by 1.054: .360 × 1.054 = .379 inch.
  8. Convert to an angle if you need one. At 100 yards, .379 ÷ 1.047 = 0.36 MOA.
  9. Find the ES for comparison. The two shots farthest apart are 9 and 10: √(1.05² + 0.50²) = √1.3525 = 1.16 inches. That is 3.2 times the measured MR, about average for 10 shots. Eight of the ten shots play no part in it.

The same table gives the spread on each axis, which is where a stringing check starts (see Chapter 24, Table 24-1).

SD horizontal = √(0.9850 ÷ 9) = .33 inch
SD vertical   = √(0.5750 ÷ 9) = .25 inch

By spreadsheet

Any spreadsheet does the same work in seconds. Put the x values in column A, the y values in column B and any velocities in column D, in rows 2 to 11.

E1:  =AVERAGE(A2:A11)                MPI x
E2:  =AVERAGE(B2:B11)                MPI y
C2:  =SQRT((A2-$E$1)^2+(B2-$E$2)^2)  distance r, copy down to C11
E3:  =AVERAGE(C2:C11)                measured MR
E4:  =E3*SQRT(10/9)                  corrected MR, one 10-shot group
E5:  =STDEV.S(D2:D11)                velocity SD

The ten example shots give .360 in E3 and .379 in E4.

For a composite, center each group on its own MPI. On the shot sheet (see Chapter 13), columns A to J run from Session to Note. Add six columns and copy each formula down.

K2:  =OR(F2="test",F2="excluded velocity")
L2:  =AVERAGEIFS(G:G,A:A,A2,B:B,B2,D:D,D2,K:K,TRUE)
M2:  =AVERAGEIFS(H:H,A:A,A2,B:B,B2,D:D,D2,K:K,TRUE)
N2:  =COUNTIFS(A:A,A2,B:B,B2,D:D,D2,K:K,TRUE)
O2:  =SQRT((G2-L2)^2+(H2-M2)^2)
P2:  =IF(AND(K2,N2>1),O2*SQRT(N2/(N2-1)),"")

Column K flags the shots whose bullet holes count in the group (test and excluded velocity). Columns L and M calculate that group’s MPI, N counts the valid shots in that group (k), O calculates each shot’s distance r from the group’s MPI, and P applies the small-group correction. Fouler, sighter and excluded group rows remain blank in column P, so they drop out automatically. The composite MR for load B3 is then:

=AVERAGEIFS(P:P,B:B,"B3")
