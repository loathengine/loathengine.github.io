Chapter 5. World War II and the Post-War Surplus Era (1940–1969)

Key points

  • World War II drove massive industrial production of IMR 4895 for the gas-operated M1 Garand and introduced IMR 4350 (1940) for slow-burning magnum and hunting cartridges.
  • Bruce Hodgdon launched the modern civilian surplus-powder market with 50,000 lb of WWII 4895 in 1947, 80 tons of .303 British ball powder as BL-C in 1949 (replaced by newly made BL-C(2) in 1961), and WWII DuPont 20mm cannon powder as H4831 in 1950.
  • Fred Olsen’s Olin Ball Powder water-slurry process scaled during WWII and Cold War NATO contracts, creating WC 846 (7.62mm NATO) and WC 844 (5.56mm NATO) along with commercial staples BL-C(2), H335, W748, and W760/H414.
  • As WWII surplus stocks depleted in the late 1950s and 1960s, Hodgdon transitioned from a surplus broker into a commercial packager contracting Olin and overseas extruded plants, permanently decoupling brand names from factory ownership.

Wartime bottleneck standards: IMR 4350 and IMR 4895

The onset of World War II pushed smokeless rifle propellant manufacturing from specialized batch chemistry into continental-scale industrial production. Two DuPont formulations defined the era for bottleneck riflemen: one built for high-expansion-ratio sporting cartridges on the eve of war, and one built to feed millions of semi-automatic infantry rifles.

IMR 4350 (1940)

In 1940, DuPont released IMR 4350, its slowest commercial single-base rifle propellant to date.1 Designed with thicker web dimensions and heavier surface deterrence than IMR 4064, IMR 4350 unlocked the ballistic potential of overbore hunting cartridges such as the .270 Winchester, .300 H&H Magnum, and .257 Roberts—and later became the gold standard for the .243 Winchester and 6.5mm-class cases. Its commercial success was so enduring that three rival packagers eventually named their own distinct powders 4350 to signal burn-rate territory (see Chapter 8).

IMR 4895 and the gas-system constraint

World War II also reshaped military small-arms propellant tolerances through the U.S. M1 Garand semi-automatic rifle. Unlike bolt-action rifles, which tolerated modest burn-rate drift so long as peak chamber pressure stayed safe, the Garand’s gas port near the muzzle required a tightly controlled pressure curve at the forward end of the barrel. Powder that burned too slowly raised port pressure and risked bending the operating rod; powder that burned too fast under-gassed the action in freezing weather.2

IMR 4895—a medium-burning, single-base extruded nitrocellulose propellant coated with DNT—became the signature wartime propellant for .30-06 M2 Ball ammunition.2 To meet wartime volume, DuPont and government-owned contractor-operated (GOCO) plants such as the Hercules-operated Radford Ordnance Works in Virginia produced tens of millions of pounds of 4895 across hundreds of military lots.2 Because wartime blending aimed at matching ammunition velocity and port pressure in specific factory brass and bullets rather than strict canister-to-canister uniformity, individual surplus lots varied noticeably in burn rate—a fact that would soon educate an entire generation of post-war American handloaders.

Bruce Hodgdon and the post-war surplus revolution

Before 1947, commercial handloading in the United States was a relatively costly niche supplied mainly by small, high-priced canisters from DuPont and Hercules. Victory in 1945 left the U.S. government sitting on mountainous stockpiles of surplus small-arms and cannon propellants slated for open-pit burning or ocean dumping.

1947: 50,000 pounds of surplus 4895

In 1947, Brewster Eltinge “Bruce” Hodgdon—a World War II Navy veteran, salesman, and avid Kansas rifleman—bought 50,000 pounds of WWII surplus 4895 .30-06 propellant from government liquidation.3 Working initially with his wife Amy and sons J.B. and Bob out of Kansas, Hodgdon packaged the surplus propellant into inexpensive mail-order containers and sold it directly to handloaders as H4895 at roughly one-third the price of commercial DuPont canisters.3

Because wartime lots varied slightly in burn rate, Hodgdon tested and published lot-specific data for his customers, turning low-cost military surplus into a practical, approachable foundation for mass-market American reloading.3

1949: BL-C and the road to BL-C(2)

In 1949, Hodgdon expanded into spherical propellants by purchasing 80 tons of WWII surplus .303 British Olin Ball Powder, marketing it to civilian shooters as BL-C (“Ball Powder, Lot C”).3 Handloaders immediately prized BL-C’s fluid, uniform metering through mechanical powder measures and its strong performance in mid-capacity bottleneck cartridges such as the .222 Remington, .308 Winchester, and .30-06. When the wartime BL-C stockpile finally ran dry, Hodgdon contracted Olin to manufacture a fresh, canister-grade commercial successor—introducing BL-C(2) in 1961, a propellant that remains in production more than six decades later.3

1950: H4831 and the 20mm Oerlikon cannon salvage

Hodgdon’s most consequential surplus acquisition arrived in 1950.3 During World War II, DuPont had manufactured large quantities of a slow-burning, single-base extruded nitrocellulose propellant—designated IMR 4831 in wartime ordnance records—for 20mm Oerlikon anti-aircraft cannon ammunition.3 Rather than letting the leftover cannon powder be destroyed, Bruce Hodgdon purchased the DuPont surplus and introduced it to civilian riflemen as H4831.3

(Historical accounts occasionally misattribute H4831’s origin to a 1941 or 1947 Australian design at Mulwala. In fact, H4831 began in 1950 strictly as salvaged WWII DuPont 20mm cannon propellant; newly manufactured H4831 moved first to Scotland and only much later to ADI Mulwala in Australia.)

H4831 filled a void no commercial canister powder had touched. It burned significantly slower than IMR 4350, allowing wildcatters and factory cartridge designers to drive heavy-for-caliber bullets in overbore bottleneck cases—most famously the .270 Winchester, .25-06 wildcat (standardized by Remington in 1969), .264 Winchester Magnum, and 7mm Remington Magnum—with mild pressures and remarkable accuracy.3 Demand for H4831 became so overwhelming that DuPont itself eventually launched a newly manufactured commercial IMR 4831 in 1973, formulated to a slightly faster burn rate than Hodgdon’s H4831—cementing one of the most famous naming divergences in rifle history (see Chapter 8).3

The Olin Ball Powder revolution

While surplus extruded propellants reshaped the civilian reloading bench, a completely different manufacturing technology was transforming military ammunition plants and, by extension, commercial rifle canisters.

Fred Olsen’s water-slurry process

In 1933, Dr. Fredrich (“Fred”) Olsen—a former Picatinny Arsenal chemist working for Western Cartridge Company (soon Olin Industries) in East Alton, Illinois—invented the Ball Powder manufacturing process, securing foundational U.S. Patent 2,027,114 in 1936.4 Instead of laboriously pressing colloided nitrocellulose dough through heavy hydraulic extrusion dies and drying the grains in hazardous air ovens for days, Olsen dissolved nitrocellulose (including aged military surplus powder) in ethyl acetate inside a warm water bath, emulsified the lacquer into suspended droplets, and distilled off the solvent under water to harden the droplets into dense microscopic spheres.5

The industrial advantages were immense (see Chapter 2). Water-bath processing slashed fire risk, shortened production cycles from days to hours, allowed precise tuning of burn rate by rolling spheres into flattened oblate spheroids and impregnating them with nitroglycerin and dibutyl phthalate (DBP) deterrents, and yielded a high-bulk-density powder that flowed like liquid through high-speed military loading machines.4

Scaled rapidly during World War II for U.S. .30 Carbine and British Lend-Lease .303 ammunition, Olin’s East Alton plant ran at capacity into the Cold War.6 As suburban growth closed in around East Alton in the late 1960s, Olin built a dedicated, modern Ball Powder complex on forested coastal land in St. Marks, Florida—completing construction in 1969 and entering full production in 1970.6

Cold War NATO military propellants and commercial descendants

Olin’s spherical technology anchored both standard NATO small-arms rifle cartridges of the Cold War, and each military specification spawned enduring civilian canister staples:

  • WC 846 (7.62×51mm NATO): Developed for M80 Ball ammunition in the M14 rifle and M60 machine gun, this double-base flattened ball propellant formed the technological baseline for both Hodgdon’s newly manufactured BL-C(2) (1961) and Winchester’s commercial Winchester 748.7
  • WC 844 (5.56×45mm NATO): A slightly faster spherical propellant standardized for M193 (and later M855) 5.56mm service ammunition, closely paralleled on the commercial market by Hodgdon H335.6
  • Winchester 760 and Hodgdon H414: Slower-burning, heavily deterred double-base spherical rifle powders engineered for .243 Winchester, .270 Winchester, and .30-06-class hunting loads. Sharing the same St. Marks/Olin spherical lineage, W760 and H414 gave handloaders a clean-metering spherical alternative in the 4350 burn-rate bracket (see Chapter 10).6
  • Hodgdon H380: Entering Hodgdon’s spherical catalog during the 1950s, H380 bridged the gap between W748 and W760, gaining wide adoption in high-velocity mid-bore varmint cartridges such as the .22-250 Remington.3

The Vietnam-era M16 propellant controversy

The transition from extruded to spherical propellants in military service did not occur without severe growing pains. When the U.S. Army fielded the 5.56mm M16 rifle in Vietnam in the mid-1960s, the cartridge had originally been developed around DuPont’s single-base extruded IMR 4475.8 When DuPont could not meet updated military pressure-velocity specs at wartime volume, Army ordnance switched 5.56mm loading contracts to Olin’s double-base spherical WC 846 (and subsequently the cleaner-tailored WC 844).8

Compared with IMR 4475, the early WC 846 ball propellant generated higher gas-port pressure in the M16’s direct-impingement system—raising cyclic rate—and contained higher levels of calcium carbonate stabilizer/neutralizer that contributed to chamber and gas-tube fouling in unchromed chambers.8 Congressional investigations led to chrome-plated chambers, heavier buffers, cleaner propellant specifications (WC 844 with reduced calcium carbonate), and proper maintenance kits.8 The episode underscored a lasting engineering lesson: in gas-operated bottleneck rifles, propellant burn-rate profile and residue chemistry must be matched to the rifle system as a whole.

From salvaged surplus to purpose-built commercial contracts

By the end of the 1960s, the golden age of cheap WWII surplus powder was drawing to a close. Military stockpiles of wartime 4895, BL-C, and 20mm 4831 were dwindling, and shooters who had built decades of rifle loads around Hodgdon’s designations expected continuous, consistent supply.

Rather than closing shop, Hodgdon pioneered a business model that now defines much of the commercial reloading industry: the marketing and specification packager operating without owning a primary nitration plant. For spherical propellants (BL-C(2), H335, H380, H414), Hodgdon contracted directly with Olin.3 For newly manufactured extruded replacements—specifically H4831 and H4895—Hodgdon turned across the Atlantic to ICI / Nobel in Ardeer, Scotland, contracting fresh single-base extruded production formulated to match the beloved burn-rate profiles of the original WWII surplus lots.3

At the same time, European commercial makers began widening their American footprint. Sweden’s Norma introduced its Bofors-manufactured 200-series rifle propellants (200, 201, 202, 204) to U.S. handloaders during the 1950s and 1960s, while Hercules launched its commercial Reloder rifle series (Reloder 7, 11, and 21) in the mid-1960s.9 The stage was set for a globalized, competitive canister market in which shooters would soon demand far tighter consistency than wartime surplus had ever offered.
