Chapter 6. The Precision and Temperature-Insensitive Era (1970–2005)

Key points

  • Organized benchrest, Palma, High Power, and F-Class competition exposed two major limits of mid-century propellants: velocity drift across ambient temperature swings and uneven metering from long tubular grains.
  • Hodgdon partnered with Australia’s Thales/ADI Mulwala plant to launch the temperature-insensitive Extreme extruded line, opening with Varget (AR2208) in 1995 and expanding to H4350 (AR2209), H4831/H4831SC (AR2213/AR2213SC), H1000 (AR2217), and Retumbo (AR2225).
  • Finland’s Vihtavuori rose to global prominence with clean-burning single-base N100 staples (notably N133 in 6mm PPC benchrest) before introducing nitroglycerin-infused N500 high-energy propellants in the 1990s.
  • Sweden’s Bofors supplied both Norma’s 200-series / MRP and Alliant’s Reloder 15 / 19 / 22 / 25 lines, while Belgium’s PB Clermont and the Czech Republic’s Explosia expanded into North American canisters through Ramshot and Accurate.
  • Hodgdon’s acquisition of IMR (2003) and the Winchester powder marketing license (2005) began a historic consolidation of North American retail brands.

Precision competition exposes thermal and metering limits

Between 1970 and 2005, precision rifle shooting matured from an informal pastime into an exacting instrument discipline. The introduction of the 6mm PPC in 1974–1975 revolutionized short-range benchrest accuracy, while international Palma (long-range .308 Winchester), NRA High Power Service Rifle, and the emerging F-Class discipline pushed practical rifle competition out to 1,000 yards.

As electronic chronographs entered widespread civilian use during the 1970s and 1980s, shooters gained objective data on what had previously been blamed on barrels or wind. Two propellant shortcomings stood out immediately:

  1. Ambient temperature sensitivity. A match load tuned in mild spring air frequently climbed into unsafe pressure signs during midsummer tournaments or dropped enough velocity in winter matches to miss vertical elevation targets at 1,000 yards (see Chapter 3). Traditional double-base spherical powders often showed steeper velocity-versus-temperature slopes than well-deterred single-base extrusions, though deterrent chemistry, ignition strength, and loading density also influenced thermal drift.
  2. Grain geometry and metering uniformity. Classic long-cut single-base tubular propellants—such as traditional IMR 4064, IMR 4350, and long-grain H4831—bridged in small bottleneck necks and sheared unevenly inside rotary powder measures, forcing competitive shooters to trickle individual charges slowly by hand.

Solving both problems simultaneously—preserving or improving thermal stability while shortening grain length for smooth metering—became the central engineering race of the era.

The Australian connection: ADI Mulwala and Hodgdon’s Extreme line

The decisive breakthrough in commercial temperature-insensitive propellants came from rural New South Wales, Australia. Established by the Australian government in 1943 during World War II to supply Allied small-arms and artillery ammunition, the Mulwala Explosives Factory evolved into Australian Defence Industries (ADI Limited) and later Thales Australia / Australian Munitions.1 Because Australian defense forces operated across scorching outback summers and cool southern winters, Mulwala’s propellant chemists focused heavily on single-base extruded formulations with specialized deterrent and surface-coating chemistry engineered to flatten burn-rate response across wide ambient temperature bands.2

1995: Varget and the Extreme revolution

Hodgdon had begun sourcing extruded propellants from ADI Mulwala to replace Scottish ICI/Nobel supply lines and expand its catalog with tighter lot control. In 1995, Hodgdon launched Varget (manufactured at Mulwala as ADI AR2208), the first propellant explicitly engineered and marketed under Hodgdon’s Extreme banner for thermal stability.3

Positioned right in the heart of the medium-rifle burn-rate zone—serving heavy-bullet .223 Remington, .308 Winchester, 6mm BR, and mid-capacity match cartridges—Varget combined short, uniform single-base cylindrical grains with remarkably flat velocity behavior from freezing cold to summer heat. Its adoption across Palma, Service Rifle, F-Class, and tactical precision shooting permanently altered market expectations: temperature insensitivity moved from an obscure military requirement to the primary buying criterion for serious riflemen.

Expanding the ADI Extreme family

Through the late 1990s and early 2000s, Hodgdon built an entire bottleneck spectrum around ADI Mulwala’s AR22xx and BM (Bench Mark) extrusions.4 Every major Extreme canister mapped directly to an Australian factory code—mappings that remain essential for historical clarity, though lot variance and independent packaging still preclude blind grain-for-grain substitution without consulting current data for the exact canister in hand (see Chapter 10):4

  • H4198 (AR2207), H322 (AR2219), and Benchmark (BM2) anchored small-bore, varmint, and benchrest cartridges.
  • H4895 (AR2206H) modernized Bruce Hodgdon’s original 1947 flagship into a temperature-stabilized short-cut extrusion.
  • H4350 (AR2209) emerged as the dominant mid-bore precision propellant of the early twenty-first century, especially as 6mm and 6.5mm match cartridges gained popularity.
  • H4831 (AR2213) and its shorter-cut metering twin H4831SC (AR2213SC) completed H4831’s remarkable three-plant historical odyssey—from WWII DuPont 20mm Oerlikon surplus (1950), to ICI/Nobel in Scotland, and finally to ADI Mulwala in Australia.3
  • H1000 (AR2217) and Retumbo (AR2225) extended the Extreme single-base architecture into overbore magnums such as the .300 Winchester Magnum, 7mm Remington Ultra Magnum, and .338 Lapua Magnum. (Note the exact factory codes: AR2217 is H1000, and AR2225 is Retumbo—a pairing frequently swapped in informal internet cross-reference tables.)4

IMR’s transition and Hodgdon’s 2003–2005 brand consolidation

Meanwhile, DuPont’s historic IMR line went through major industrial and corporate shifts. DuPont added IMR 4831 (1973) and the slow magnum powder IMR 7828 (1985, later joined by short-cut IMR 7828 SSC) while gradually divesting its commercial sporting-powder business.5 IMR extruded manufacturing consolidated north of the border at the former Canadian Industries Limited (CIL) / Expro facility in Valleyfield, Quebec—today General Dynamics Ordnance and Tactical Systems – Canada (GD-OTS Canada) (see Chapter 9).

In 2003, Hodgdon Powder Company acquired the IMR brand outright, reuniting the rival 4895, 4350, and 4831 names under one corporate umbrella while preserving their separate manufacturing plants (Mulwala for Hodgdon Extreme, Valleyfield for traditional IMR).5 Two years later, in 2005, Hodgdon secured the exclusive license to market Winchester smokeless reloading propellants (manufactured at St. Marks Powder in Florida, which Olin had sold to General Dynamics in 1996).6 Within two years, the former Kansas surplus dealer had become the dominant commercial marketer of North American reloading propellants.

Scandinavian precision: Vihtavuori and Bofors

While Australia transformed American thermal-stability expectations, Finland and Sweden built an unmatched reputation for chemical purity, clean burning, and high-energy rifle chemistry.

Vihtavuori: N100 purity and the N500 high-energy launch

By the 1970s and 1980s, Finland’s Vihtavuori plant had expanded aggressively into civilian sporting exports, earning worldwide acclaim for its single-base N100 series.7 Produced from carefully refined nitrocellulose with strict moisture and solvent recovery controls, N100 powders burned notably cleaner than most legacy American extrusions.

In short-range benchrest competition, Vihtavuori N133 became virtually synonymous with the 6mm PPC cartridge, winning national and world championships for decades on the strength of tight velocity spreads and clean-burning consistency.7 Across mid-bore and magnum target shooting, N140, N150, N160, and N165 established a clean, cool-burning, single-base standard favored across Europe and North America alike.8

In the 1990s, Vihtavuori introduced a major chemical departure: the N500 high-energy series—opening with staples such as N540, N550, and N560 (later joined by N530).9 By infusing nitroglycerin into extruded nitrocellulose grains, Vihtavuori increased specific energy density (heat of explosion, Qex), allowing handloaders to achieve higher muzzle velocities in high-capacity bottleneck cartridges without raising peak chamber pressure.9 The physics carried an honest engineering tradeoff: higher energy density brought higher flame temperatures and faster barrel throat wear than cooler-burning N100 counterparts (see Chapter 3). Shooters could now choose deliberately between N100 barrel longevity and N500 peak velocity.

Bofors, Norma, and Alliant Reloder

In Sweden, Bofors AB (Karlskoga, later Eurenco Bofors AB) became the quiet engine behind two of the world’s most respected rifle propellant lines.10 For domestic and international hunting and target shooters, Bofors manufactured Norma’s commercial rifle lineup—the 200-series (200, 201, 202, 203-B, 204) and the iconic magnum propellant Norma MRP (Magnum Rifle Powder).11

In the United States, Hercules’ aerospace and sporting-powder division became Alliant Techsystems (ATK) in 1995, marketing its commercial propellants under the Alliant Powder name.12 Alliant contracted Bofors in Karlskoga to manufacture its modern extruded Reloder rifle family: Reloder 7, Reloder 10x, Reloder 15, Reloder 19, Reloder 22, and Reloder 25.12

Reloder 15 earned particular fame when the U.S. military selected it for M118LR 7.62×51mm long-range match ammunition and Mk 262 5.56×45mm precision ammunition, validating Swedish double-base extruded chemistry in both military sniper systems and civilian Service Rifle matches.12 Because Norma and Alliant sourced overlapping burn-rate classes from the same Karlskoga plant—such as Norma 203-B alongside Reloder 15, Norma 204 alongside Reloder 19, and Norma MRP alongside Reloder 22—shooters frequently treated them as identical twins. In reality, each packager maintained its own lot specifications and acceptance tolerances, meaning close Swedish counterparts still required independent published load data rather than blind 1:1 charge substitution (see Chapter 10).

Belgian and Czech commercial expansion

The final quarter of the twentieth century also brought two major continental European OEM plants into mainstream North American rifle canisters.

PB Clermont, Accurate Arms, and Western Powders’ Ramshot

In 1952, PB Clermont established a spherical propellant facility in Engis, Belgium, originally licensing Olin’s Ball Powder technology to supply European NATO defense contracts.13 Later integrated into France’s SNPE group (and today part of Eurenco), PB Clermont refined rolled spherical ball propellants known for uniform grain sizing, high bulk density, and smooth metering.13

In the United States, Accurate Arms—founded in 1980 in McEwen, Tennessee—built its commercial catalog by sourcing high-quality military-spec surplus and newly manufactured propellants from overseas factories, including Israel Military Industries (IMI), Belgium’s PB Clermont, and the Czech Republic’s Explosia. In 1999, Montana-based Western Powders launched the Ramshot brand as a dedicated U.S. commercial showcase for PB Clermont’s Belgian spherical rifle propellants: TAC, X-Terminator, Big Game, Hunter, and Magnum.13

Western Powders acquired Accurate Arms in 2004, consolidating both lines under one roof. Under Western Powders, PB Clermont supplied both Ramshot and key Accurate spherical rifle powders (2230, 2460, 2520)—with Ramshot X-Terminator serving as the direct Belgian counterpart to Accurate 2230 (while Ramshot TAC sat slightly slower alongside 2460 and 2520).13

Explosia / Lovex enters the Western canister market

Following the end of the Cold War, Explosia a.s. of Pardubice-Semtín, Czech Republic—one of Central Europe’s oldest energetic-materials complexes—opened its Lovex commercial propellant lines (S for single-base extrusions, D for double-base formulations) to Western export markets.14 Explosia supplied early extruded rifle propellants for Accurate Arms (including Czech-made lots of 2015, 2495, 4064, and 4350 before Western Powders later moved several of those extruded lines to GD-OTS Canada in Valleyfield to Accurate’s own specifications), laying the groundwork for Lovex’s eventual U.S. rebranding under Shooters World in the following decade.14
